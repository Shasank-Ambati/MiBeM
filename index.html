Computational materials science

A reference series for materials scientists and metallurgists: the physical models, numerical methods and practical habits behind simulating materials, from electrons to components.

Topics

Read in order, or jump to any topic. Each page states what you should be able to do afterwards and ends with references.

  • Introduction to computational materials science Scales, method families, multiscale linking, validation, software and data resources.
  • Crystal structures, supercells and periodic boundaries Lattices, unit cells, Miller indices, defects, building simulation cells, periodic boundary conditions.
  • Statistical mechanics for simulation Microstates, ensembles, the partition function, free energy, ergodicity and sampling.
  • Thermodynamics and CALPHAD Gibbs energy, common tangent, solution models, phase diagrams and equilibrium calculation.
  • Density functional theory in practice The Kohn–Sham scheme, functionals, the SCF cycle, convergence parameters, what to compute.
  • Molecular dynamics Integrators, interatomic potentials, thermostats, equilibration and analysis.
  • Monte Carlo and kinetic Monte Carlo Importance sampling, Metropolis, the Ising model, rejection-free kinetics.
  • Diffusion and kinetics Fick’s laws, Arrhenius behaviour, atomistic mechanisms and numerical solution.
  • Phase-field modelling Diffuse interfaces, Allen–Cahn and Cahn–Hilliard, spinodal decomposition and numerics.
  • The finite element method Weak form, shape functions, meshing, convergence and nonlinear problems.
  • Machine learning for materials Data, descriptors, models, validation, machine-learned potentials and active learning.

About

MiBeM is written for anyone who studies, makes or tests materials: students, researchers and engineers working with metals, ceramics, polymers, semiconductors or composites. Pages assume a materials-science background (thermodynamics, crystal structure, mechanical behaviour) but not prior experience with simulation software.

The animated figures are produced from small real simulations or computed curves, not drawn by hand, so what they show is what the method does.
