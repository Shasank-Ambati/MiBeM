Statistics, error analysis and fitting

Measurements and simulations both produce noisy numbers, and what you can claim depends on how well you understand that noise. This course teaches the working statistics of computational materials science: how to put honest error bars on averages (including the correlated data of molecular dynamics), how uncertainties propagate through formulas, how to fit models to data and quote parameter uncertainties, and how to avoid fitting noise. It is also the mathematical bridge to machine learning for materials, which is regularised regression at scale.

Lecture 1. Estimating a mean and its uncertainty#

Sample mean, sample variance and standard error#

Given $N$ independent measurements $x_1,\ldots,x_N$ of a quantity with true mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the natural estimates are the sample mean $\bar x=\frac1N\sum x_i$ and the sample variance $$s^2=\frac1{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N(x_i-\bar x)^2 .$$ The divisor $N-1$ (Bessel’s correction) makes $s^2$ an unbiased estimator: $\langle s^2\rangle=\sigma^2$. (The deviations are measured from $\bar x$, which was itself fitted to the data and so fits slightly too well; one degree of freedom is used up.) The uncertainty of the mean is the standard error $$\mathrm{SE}=\frac{s}{\sqrt N},$$ which falls with more data, whereas $s$ itself does not: $s$ describes the scatter of individual measurements, $\mathrm{SE}$ the precision of their average. Always say which one you are quoting.

Confidence intervals and the $t$ distribution#

By the central limit theorem (MATH 204), for large $N$ the interval $\bar x\pm1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$ contains the true mean in $95\%$ of repeated experiments. For small $N$, using the estimated $s$ instead of the unknown $\sigma$ widens the interval: replace $1.96$ by the Student $t$ quantile with $N-1$ degrees of freedom:

The distinction matters for the typical case in simulation or experiment of $3$ to $10$ independent repeats. A “confidence interval” is a statement about the procedure (95% of such intervals cover the truth), not a probability that the true value lies in this particular interval; a Bayesian credible interval (section 6) is the latter. To judge whether two means differ significantly, compare the difference with its combined standard error, $z=\dfrac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_1^2+\mathrm{SE}_2^2}}$; $|z|\lesssim2$ is consistent with no difference.

Why the divisor is $N-1$

Write $x_i-\bar x=(x_i-\mu)-(\bar x-\mu)$. Then $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=\sum(x_i-\mu)^2-N(\bar x-\mu)^2$. Taking expectations, $\langle\sum(x_i-\mu)^2\rangle=N\sigma^2$ and $\langle N(\bar x-\mu)^2\rangle=N\cdot\sigma^2/N=\sigma^2$, so $\langle\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\rangle=(N-1)\sigma^2$. Dividing by $N-1$ makes the estimate unbiased; dividing by $N$ would underestimate $\sigma^2$ by the factor $(N-1)/N$.

Worked example 1.1: five lattice-parameter measurements

Five independent determinations of a lattice parameter (in Å) are $4.04,4.06,4.05,4.07,4.03$. Give the mean with a $95\%$ confidence interval.

$\bar x=4.050$. The deviations are $-0.01,+0.01,0,+0.02,-0.02$, so $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=0.001$ and $s=\sqrt{0.001/4}=0.0158$. The standard error is $s/\sqrt5=0.00707$. With $\nu=4$ degrees of freedom $t_{0.975}=2.776$, so the interval is $4.050\pm0.0196$, i.e. $4.050\pm0.020$ Å. The normal value $1.96$ would give $\pm0.0139$, an interval that is too narrow by $30\%$ for five measurements.

Lecture 2. Propagation of errors#

Most results are computed from measured inputs. If $f=f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ and the inputs have independent uncertainties $\sigma_i$, linearising (the first-order Taylor expansion of MATH 102) gives $$\sigma_f^2=\sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)^2\sigma_i^2 .$$ For correlated inputs, add $2\sum_{i<j}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\mathrm{Cov}(x_i,x_j)$ or, in matrix form, $\sigma_f^2=\mathbf g^TC\mathbf g$ with gradient $\mathbf g$ and covariance matrix $C$. The commonly needed special cases:

FormulaUncertainty
$f=x\pm y$$\sigma_f=\sqrt{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_y^2}$ (absolute errors add in quadrature)
$f=x\,y$ or $x/y$$\dfrac{\sigma_f}{|f|}=\sqrt{\bigl(\tfrac{\sigma_x}{x}\bigr)^2+\bigl(\tfrac{\sigma_y}{y}\bigr)^2}$ (relative errors add in quadrature)
$f=x^n$$\dfrac{\sigma_f}{|f|}=|n|\dfrac{\sigma_x}{|x|}$
$f=\ln x$$\sigma_f=\sigma_x/|x|$
$f=e^{x}$$\sigma_f/|f|=\sigma_x$

Compared with the worst-case sum of absolute errors in MATH 102, Problem 2.2, independent errors rarely all conspire, so adding in quadrature gives a smaller (and more realistic) result. Two warnings: the formula is linear, so it fails when errors are large or the function strongly nonlinear (use sampling or the bootstrap), and it assumes independence: for two quantities extracted from the same fit (slope and intercept) the covariance term is essential (Problem 6). Differences of nearly equal numbers amplify relative error: $x=10.0\pm0.1$ and $y=9.8\pm0.1$ give $x-y=0.2\pm0.14$, a $70\%$ error from two $1\%$ measurements. This is why quantities such as formation energies, defined as small differences between large total energies, need cancellation of errors between the terms.

Worked example 2.1: three propagation problems

(a) A binding energy $E_b=E_{AB}-E_A-E_B$ where each energy has an uncertainty of $0.02$ eV. (b) A modulus $E=\sigma/\varepsilon$ from $\sigma=200\pm4$ MPa and $\varepsilon=(1.000\pm0.030)\times10^{-3}$. (c) A diffusivity $D=D_0e^{-Q/k_\mathrm BT}$ with $Q=1.50\pm0.05$ eV at $900$ K.

(a) Absolute errors add in quadrature: $\sigma_{E_b}=0.02\sqrt3=0.035$ eV, larger than any input. Subtracting nearly equal large numbers magnifies the relative error. (b) Relative errors add in quadrature: $\sqrt{0.02^2+0.03^2}=0.036$, so $E=200\pm7.2$ GPa. (c) $\sigma_D/D=\sigma_Q/k_\mathrm BT=0.05/0.0776=0.64$: a seemingly small error of $0.05$ eV in the activation energy produces a $64\%$ error in $D$ (and by the linearisation warning, the interval is really asymmetric: $D$ lies between a factor $e^{\pm0.64}=0.53$ and $1.9$ of its central value). Barriers must be known to a few hundredths of an eV to give useful rates.

Lecture 3. Correlated data from simulations#

Consecutive configurations of a molecular dynamics or Metropolis Monte Carlo run are not independent: the system remembers where it was. The formula $s/\sqrt N$ then underestimates the error, often by a factor of $5$ or $10$. Quantify the memory with the autocorrelation function $\rho_k=\dfrac{\langle(A_i-\bar A)(A_{i+k}-\bar A)\rangle}{\sigma^2}$ of the quantity $A$ at lag $k$, with $\rho_0=1$. For a long run the variance of the mean is $$\mathrm{Var}(\bar A)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\Bigl(1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_k\Bigr)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N_\mathrm{eff}},\qquad N_\mathrm{eff}=\frac N{2\tau_\mathrm{int}},\quad\tau_\mathrm{int}=\tfrac12+\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_k .$$ The integrated autocorrelation time $\tau_\mathrm{int}$ counts how many successive samples carry the information of one independent sample (in units of the sampling interval). A common model is exponential decay $\rho_k=e^{-k/\tau}$, which for $\rho=\rho_1$ gives $\tau_\mathrm{int}=\dfrac{1+\rho}{2(1-\rho)}$; with $\rho=0.9$ it is $9.5$, so $N_\mathrm{eff}=N/19$ and the true error is $\sqrt{19}=4.4$ times the naive one (Problem 4). Correlation times grow near phase transitions (critical slowing down) and for slow processes such as diffusion across a cell, so longer runs, not more frequent sampling, are needed.

Blocking#

A robust practical method needing no explicit autocorrelation estimate: divide the series into blocks of length $b$, average within each block, and compute the standard error of the $N/b$ block means treated as independent. For small $b$ the estimate is too low (blocks are still correlated); as $b$ exceeds a few $\tau_\mathrm{int}$ the block means decorrelate and the estimated error rises to a plateau, which is the correct standard error. If it never plateaus the run is too short, a warning that the simulation has not sampled the relevant dynamics. Discard an initial transient (equilibration) before analysis.

Worked example 3.1: how many independent samples do you have?

An MD run of $N=10\,000$ frames has $\sigma=0.5$ for some property, with autocorrelation $\rho_k=0.9^k$. Find $\tau_\mathrm{int}$, $N_\mathrm{eff}$ and the true standard error.

For a geometric sequence $\sum_{k\ge1}\rho^k=\rho/(1-\rho)=9$, so $\tau_\mathrm{int}=\tfrac12+9=9.5$ and $N_\mathrm{eff}=N/(2\tau_\mathrm{int})=10\,\allowbreak 000/19=526$. The naive error is $\sigma/\sqrt N=0.005$, whereas the true error is $\sigma/\sqrt{N_\mathrm{eff}}=0.5/\sqrt{526}=0.0218$, larger by $\sqrt{19}=4.4$. Blocking would show the plateau of the block error near $0.022$ once the block length exceeds $\sim2\tau_\mathrm{int}\approx20$ frames. Quoting the naive number overstates the precision by a factor of four.

Lecture 4. Least squares#

Linear models#

Suppose $y_i=\sum_{j=1}^p\beta_jf_j(x_i)+\varepsilon_i$ with unknown parameters $\beta_j$ and noise $\varepsilon_i$ of zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$. In matrix form $\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, where $X_{ij}=f_j(x_i)$ is the $n\times p$ design matrix. A straight line has $X=[\mathbf1\ \ \mathbf x]$; a quadratic adds a column $\mathbf x^2$. The least-squares estimate minimises the sum of squared residuals $S(\boldsymbol\beta)=\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2$. Setting $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}S=0$ gives the normal equations $$X^TX\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=X^T\mathbf y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y .$$ Geometrically (MATH 103), $X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf y$ onto the column space of $X$, and the residual vector is perpendicular to every column: $X^T\mathbf r=0$. Numerically one does not form $X^TX$ (squaring the condition number); one uses the QR decomposition or the SVD, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=X^+\mathbf y=V\Sigma^{-1}U^T\mathbf y$ (MATH 202). The uncertainty of the fitted parameters follows from linear propagation of the noise: $$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})=\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1},\qquad s^2=\frac{S(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})}{n-p}\ \text{(estimate of }\sigma^2),$$ and the standard error of $\hat\beta_j$ is the square root of the $j$-th diagonal entry. The off-diagonal entries give correlations between parameters, which are often strong: for a line fitted far from the origin the intercept and slope are almost perfectly anti-correlated (Problem 6). For a straight line, with $S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$: $$\hat b=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{S_{xx}},\quad\hat a=\bar y-\hat b\bar x,\quad\mathrm{SE}(\hat b)=\frac{s}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}},\quad\mathrm{SE}(\hat a)=s\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}}.$$ The slope is better determined the wider the range of $x$ (larger $S_{xx}$), which is why calibration data should span the range of interest. The coefficient of determination $R^2=1-S/\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2$ is the fraction of variance explained, but a high $R^2$ does not make a model right (Problem 5).

Weights and $\chi^2$#

When the data have known, unequal uncertainties $\sigma_i$, minimise $$\chi^2=\sum_i\Bigl(\frac{y_i-f(x_i;\boldsymbol\beta)}{\sigma_i}\Bigr)^2,$$ i.e. weight each point by $1/\sigma_i^2$. If the model is right and the $\sigma_i$ are correct, $\chi^2$ is distributed with mean $\nu=n-p$ (the degrees of freedom), so the reduced $\chi^2=\chi^2/\nu\approx1$: much larger values mean the model is inadequate or the errors underestimated; much smaller ones mean overestimated errors (or suspiciously good data). The weighted mean of independent measurements $x_i\pm\sigma_i$ is $\bar x=\sum w_ix_i/\sum w_i$ with $w_i=1/\sigma_i^2$ and uncertainty $\bigl(\sum w_i\bigr)^{-1/2}$: precisions add.

Nonlinear least squares#

For models nonlinear in the parameters (an exponential decay, a Birch–Murnaghan equation of state, a Gaussian peak) there is no closed form. Gauss–Newton linearises the model about the current guess, $f(\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx f+J\boldsymbol\delta$ with the Jacobian $J_{ij}=\partial f(x_i)/\partial\beta_j$, and solves the linear problem $J^TJ\,\boldsymbol\delta=J^T\mathbf r$ for the step; Levenberg–Marquardt adds a damping term $\mu I$ to make it robust far from the minimum. The parameter covariance is again $s^2(J^TJ)^{-1}$ at the solution. Unlike the linear case, the result depends on the starting guess and can land in a local minimum, so good initial values (from a linearised or graphical estimate) and a check of the fit by eye are essential. Many nonlinear laws can be linearised by taking logarithms: $\ln D=\ln D_0-Q/k_\mathrm{B}T$ is a straight line in $1/T$ (an Arrhenius plot), but note that the transformation also changes the weights: errors in $D$ become errors in $\ln D$ of size $\sigma_D/D$.

In materials science: equation-of-state fits

The total energy $E(V)$ of a crystal computed by DFT at several volumes is fitted to extract the equilibrium volume $V_0$, bulk modulus $B_0=V_0\,E''(V_0)$ and its pressure derivative $B_0'$. A quadratic fit is linear least squares but only accurate near the minimum, since it ignores the anharmonic skew of the well; the Birch–Murnaghan or Vinet form is nonlinear in $V_0$ and $B_0'$. The answers depend on the volume range and number of points (Problem 7), and the fitted $B_0$ and $B_0'$ are strongly anti-correlated, so quote them with their covariance.

Worked example 4.1: a straight-line fit with errors

Fit $y=a+bx$ to $(1,2.1),\allowbreak (2,3.9),\allowbreak (3,6.2),\allowbreak (4,7.8),\allowbreak (5,10.1)$ and give the standard errors.

$\bar x=3$, $\bar y=6.02$, $S_{xx}=10$, $S_{xy}=19.9$, so $\hat b=1.99$ and $\hat a=6.02-1.99\times3=0.05$. The residuals are $0.06,-0.13,0.18,-0.21,0.10$, so $S=0.107$, $s=\sqrt{0.107/3}=0.189$. Then $\mathrm{SE}(b)=s/\sqrt{S_{xx}}=0.060$ and $\mathrm{SE}(a)=s\sqrt{1/5+9/10}=0.198$, and $R^2=0.9973$. The intercept ($0.05\pm0.20$) is consistent with zero while the slope is $1.99\pm0.06$. The correlation between $a$ and $b$ is $-\bar x/\sqrt{\bar x^2+S_{xx}/n}=-0.90$: when the line is steeper the intercept must be lower, which is why quoting parameters without their covariance can mislead when you propagate them (centring $x$ removes the correlation).

Lecture 5. Overfitting, regularisation and model selection#

Any model with enough adjustable parameters can fit the noise. A polynomial of degree $n-1$ passes exactly through $n$ points, with zero training error and wild oscillations between them, useless for prediction. The tension is the bias–variance trade-off: a model that is too simple has high bias (systematic error), one that is too flexible has high variance (its fit changes drastically with the particular noise in the data). The training error always decreases as parameters are added, so it cannot choose the model. What matters is the generalisation error on data not used for fitting.

Cross-validation#

Hold out part of the data, fit on the rest, and measure the error on the held-out part. In $k$-fold cross-validation the data are split into $k$ groups, each used once as the test set; leave-one-out (LOO) is the extreme $k=n$. The model with the lowest cross-validated error is preferred (Problem 10). For structured data, such as many atoms from a few structures, the split must be by structure rather than by atom, or the test data leak information from the training data and the error is badly underestimated, one of the commonest mistakes in machine-learning potentials. Information criteria, $\mathrm{AIC}=2p-2\ln L$ and $\mathrm{BIC}=p\ln n-2\ln L$, penalise parameters analytically (for Gaussian errors $-2\ln L=\chi^2+\mathrm{const}$).

Ridge regression#

If columns of $X$ are nearly dependent, $X^TX$ is nearly singular, the coefficients are huge and unstable, and a tiny change in the data swings the answer (the ill-conditioning example of MATH 103, Problem 4.12). Ridge (Tikhonov) regression minimises a penalised sum $$S_\lambda(\boldsymbol\beta)=\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2+\lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y .$$ In terms of the SVD $X=U\Sigma V^T$, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda=\sum_i\dfrac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}(\mathbf u_i\cdot\mathbf y)\mathbf v_i$: each component is multiplied by the filter $\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)$, which is near $1$ for well-determined directions ($\sigma_i^2\gg\lambda$) and near $0$ for the poorly determined ones ($\sigma_i^2\ll\lambda$). Noise-dominated directions are damped instead of amplified by $1/\sigma_i$. The parameter $\lambda$ is chosen by cross-validation. The lasso penalty $\lambda\sum|\beta_j|$ instead drives many coefficients exactly to zero (feature selection). Kernel ridge regression and Gaussian-process regression, the workhorses of machine-learned interatomic potentials (Machine learning for materials), are ridge regression in a high-dimensional feature space.

Worked example 5.1: filtering unstable directions

A design matrix has singular values $\sigma_1=10$ and $\sigma_2=0.01$. How does ridge regression with $\lambda=0.1$ treat each direction, and what does a degree-5 polynomial through 6 points tell us about overfitting?

The filter factors are $\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)$: $100/100.1=0.999$ for the well-determined direction and $10^{-4}/0.1001=0.001$ for the poorly determined one. Without regularisation noise in $\mathbf y$ is amplified by $1/\sigma_i$: a factor $100$ in the second direction but only $0.1$ in the first. With ridge the amplification is $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)$, which is $0.1$ in the second direction, removing the unstable direction at the cost of a small bias. Likewise, 6 points always lie on a degree-5 polynomial exactly (zero residual), so a training error of zero says nothing about prediction; only error on held-out data does.

Lecture 6. Bayesian view and the bootstrap#

Bayes’ theorem (MATH 204) for a parameter $\theta$ and data $D$ reads $p(\theta|D)\propto p(D|\theta)\,\allowbreak p(\theta)$: posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior. For independent Gaussian errors the likelihood is $\propto e^{-\chi^2/2}$, so maximising it is least squares. With a flat prior the posterior mode is the least-squares fit; with a Gaussian prior on the parameters, $p(\boldsymbol\beta)\propto e^{-\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2/2\tau^2}$, the maximum a posteriori estimate is exactly ridge regression, with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$: regularisation is prior belief that the parameters are not huge. For one Gaussian measurement of a Gaussian-distributed quantity, precisions add: $$\frac1{\sigma_\mathrm{post}^2}=\frac1{\sigma_0^2}+\frac1{\sigma^2},\qquad\mu_\mathrm{post}=\sigma_\mathrm{post}^2\Bigl(\frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}+\frac{x}{\sigma^2}\Bigr),$$ a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the measurement (Problem 12). This is how a computed prediction with a known method error and an experimental value are combined, and how Bayesian optimisation and active learning decide where to sample next.

The bootstrap estimates the uncertainty of any statistic without formulas: resample the data set with replacement, $B\approx10^3$ times, compute the statistic for each resample, and use the spread of the results as its standard error (or the $2.5$ and $97.5$ percentiles as a $95\%$ interval). It treats the observed data as a stand-in for the unknown distribution. It works for medians, peak positions, ratios, fitted parameters, and for correlated data if you resample whole blocks of consecutive samples (block bootstrap) rather than single points. For the mean it reproduces the standard error up to a factor $\sqrt{(N-1)/N}$ (Problem 11); its value is for statistics that have no simple formula.

Worked example 6.1: combining a prior with data, and the bootstrap

Prior knowledge says a migration barrier is $0.80\pm0.10$ eV (Gaussian). A new calculation gives $0.90\pm0.05$ eV. Find the posterior. How would a bootstrap estimate the standard error for the five lattice parameters of Example 1.1?

Precisions (inverse variances) add: $1/0.1^2=100$ and $1/0.05^2=400$, so the posterior precision is $500$ and its standard deviation $1/\sqrt{500}=0.045$ eV. The posterior mean is the precision-weighted average, $(0.80\times100+0.90\times400)/500=0.88$ eV: closer to the more precise value, and with a smaller uncertainty than either input. The bootstrap resamples the five values with replacement many times (for instance $\{4.04,4.04,4.05,4.07,4.03\}$), computes the mean of each resample, and takes the standard deviation of those means. For a mean this converges to $\sqrt{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}/N=0.0063$, close to the analytic standard error $0.0071$ (smaller by $\sqrt{(N-1)/N}$); the point of the bootstrap is that it also works for statistics with no formula, such as the ratio of two fitted parameters or a median.

Summary of the unit#

Reporting a number honestly means reporting its uncertainty: the standard error for independent samples, enlarged by the autocorrelation time for simulation data, propagated through formulas and fits with the covariance of the parameters. Least squares is the geometry of projection, solved stably with QR or the SVD, whose small singular values warn of ill-conditioning. Training error is not prediction error; cross-validation, regularisation and prior knowledge guard against fitting noise. The last course reaches the mathematical core of the finite element and phase-field methods, where an unknown function is chosen to minimise an energy.

Key takeaways
  • An estimate without an uncertainty is not a result: the standard error of a mean is $s/\sqrt{N}$ for independent samples, with the Student $t$ factor for small $N$, and independent errors propagate in quadrature with the partial derivatives as weights, $\sigma_f^2=\sum(\partial f/\partial x_i)^2\sigma_i^2$.
  • Consecutive frames from a simulation are correlated; the effective number of independent samples is $N/(2\tau_\mathrm{int})$, so the true error bar can be several times larger than the naive one, and blocking analysis finds it.
  • Least squares minimises $\|\mathbf{y}-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2$ and is solved by the normal equations $X^TX\boldsymbol\beta=X^T\mathbf{y}$ (better by QR or the SVD); the parameter covariance is $s^2(X^TX)^{-1}$, and correlated parameters, such as an Arrhenius slope and intercept, must be reported together.
  • A good fit has reduced $\chi^2\approx1$ when the weights are $1/\sigma_i^2$; fitting more parameters always lowers the training error, so model choice needs held-out data (cross-validation) or a penalty (ridge regression, information criteria).
  • Ridge regression damps the poorly determined directions of the SVD and cures ill-conditioning; it is the maximum a posteriori estimate with a Gaussian prior, which links regularisation to the Bayesian view in which prior knowledge and data combine by adding precisions.
  • The bootstrap estimates uncertainties by resampling the data, and works for any statistic, provided the resampling respects correlations (block bootstrap).

Problem 11.2Basic

(a) A resistance is computed as $R=V/I$ with $V=5.00\pm0.05$ V and $I=0.250\pm0.004$ A. Find $R$ and its uncertainty by propagation in quadrature, and compare with adding the relative errors linearly. (b) A formation energy is $E_f=E_\mathrm{defect}-E_\mathrm{perfect}+\mu$ with $E_\mathrm{defect}=-1000.56\pm0.02$, $E_\mathrm{perfect}=-1005.00\pm0.02$ and $\mu=-3.74\pm0.01$ eV. Find $E_f$ and its uncertainty. (c) Why can the true uncertainty on $E_f$ be smaller than the quadrature estimate if the errors in the two total energies are correlated?

Hint

(a) Relative errors: $0.05/5.00=1.0\%$ and $0.004/0.250=1.6\%$. (b) For a sum, absolute errors add in quadrature.

Full solution

(a) $R=5.00/0.250=20.0\ \Omega$. In quadrature: $\sigma_R/R=\sqrt{0.010^2+0.016^2}=0.0189$, so $\sigma_R=0.38\ \Omega$: $R=20.0\pm0.4\ \Omega$. A worst-case linear sum would give $2.6\%$, i.e. $0.52\ \Omega$ (overestimate, since independent errors seldom all push the same way).

(b) $E_\mathrm{defect}-E_\mathrm{perfect}=-1000.56+1005.00=4.44$ eV, and $E_f=4.44+(-3.74)=0.70$ eV. Uncertainty: $\sqrt{0.02^2+0.02^2+0.01^2}=0.03$ eV, so $E_f=0.70\pm0.03$ eV ($4\%$). The result is a small difference of large numbers: a $0.7$ eV quantity from energies of order $1000$ eV, so each total energy must be accurate to about $3\times10^{-5}$ relative.

(c) The systematic parts of the errors (the exchange–correlation functional, pseudopotentials, basis set) are nearly the same in the defective and perfect cells computed the same way, so they cancel in the difference. The covariance term $-2\,\allowbreak \mathrm{Cov}(E_\mathrm{defect},E_\mathrm{perfect})$ reduces the variance of the difference. That is why DFT energy differences are far more reliable than total energies, provided both are computed with identical settings (cutoff, $k$-mesh, cell size).

$R=20.0\pm0.4\ \Omega$; $E_f=0.70\pm0.03$ eV

A time series from a molecular dynamics run has standard deviation $\sigma=1$ (in some units) and exponentially decaying autocorrelation with $\rho_1=0.9$ between consecutive samples. (a) Show that $\tau_\mathrm{int}=\dfrac{1+\rho}{2(1-\rho)}$ for $\rho_k=\rho^k$. (b) For $N=10^5$ samples compare the naive and true standard errors of the mean, and find $N_\mathrm{eff}$. (c) The energy is stored every $10$ fs and its correlation time is $\tau_\mathrm{int}=95$ fs$\,$($=\!9.5$ samples). (a) $\sum_{k\ge1}\rho^k=\rho/(1-\rho)$. (b) $\mathrm{SE}_\mathrm{true}=\sigma\sqrt{2\tau_\mathrm{int}/N}$.

Full solution

Diffusion coefficients measured at four temperatures are: $600$ K: $3.2\times10^{-14}$; $700$ K: $5.2\times10^{-13}$; $800$ K: $5.1\times10^{-12}$; $900$ K: $2.6\times10^{-11}$ m$^2$/s. Regress $y=\ln D$ on $x=1/T$. The standard errors come from $s^2(X^TX)^{-1}$ with $n-p=2$. Numerical results: slope $=-12121$ K, intercept $=-10.898$, $s^2=0.00404$, SEs $152.96$ K and $0.211$.

Full solution

Problem 11.9Challenge

Return to the ill-conditioned system $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1.0001\end{pmatrix}$ of MATH 103 (Problem 4.12). (a) Show that ridge regression with $\lambda=0.01$ gives stable answers for $\mathbf b=(2,2.0001)$ and $\mathbf b'=(2,2.0002)$. (b) Express the ridge solution in terms of the SVD filter factors; the singular values of $A$ are $\sigma_1\approx2.0$ and $\sigma_2\approx5\times10^{-5}$. (c) What price is paid in accuracy?

Hint

Ridge: $(A^TA+\lambda I)\mathbf x=A^T\mathbf b$. Filter factor: $f_i=\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)$.

Full solution

(a) Without regularisation, $\mathbf b\to\mathbf x=(1,1)$ but $\mathbf b'\to\mathbf x'=(0,2)$. With $\lambda=0.01$, solving $(A^TA+0.01I)\mathbf x=A^T\mathbf b$ gives $\mathbf x_\lambda=(0.9975,\,0.9975)$ for $\mathbf b$ and $(0.9975,\,0.9976)$ for $\mathbf b'$: the two answers now differ in the fifth digit instead of by $100\%$. (b) $f_1=\dfrac{4}{4+0.01}=0.9975$ for the large singular direction, i.e. the well-determined combination $x_1+x_2$ (with eigenvector $(1,1)/\sqrt2$) is kept, being reduced only by $0.25\%$; and $f_2=\dfrac{(5\times10^{-5})^2}{(5\times10^{-5})^2+0.01}=2.5\times10^{-7}\approx0$: the ill-determined combination $x_1-x_2$ (direction $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$, determined only by the $10^{-4}$ difference between the columns) is suppressed entirely, so the solution is forced to $x_1\approx x_2$. The unregularised solution would amplify noise in that direction by $1/\sigma_2=2\times10^4$.

(c) The price is a small bias: the solution $0.9975$ instead of $1$ (a $0.25\%$ error from the shrinkage of the good direction), and the loss of the ability to distinguish the two columns. That is the bias–variance trade-off: a little bias for a huge reduction in variance. A smaller $\lambda$ reduces the bias but keeps more noise; $\lambda$ is chosen by cross-validation or from the known noise level (discrepancy principle).

Six measurements at $x=0,1,2,3,4,5$ give $y=1.1,\ 2.9,\ 5.2,\ 6.9,\ 9.1,\ 11.0$ (noise of order $0.1$ around the line $2x+1$). Polynomials of degree $1$ to $4$ are fitted. Problem 11.11Core

(a) For a sample $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, a bootstrap resample draws $n$ values with replacement. Show that the variance of the bootstrap resample mean (over the resampling distribution) is $\dfrac{1}{n}\cdot\dfrac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=\dfrac{n-1}{n}\cdot\dfrac{s^2}{n}$. (b) Evaluate for the lattice-parameter data of Problem 11.1 and compare with the standard error. (c) Why is the bootstrap useful for the median, and what must be changed for time-series data?

Hint

Each resampled value is a random draw from the $n$ observed values, with mean $\bar x$ and variance $\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$.

Full solution

(a) A single resampled value $x^*$ takes each $x_i$ with probability $1/n$, so $\langle x^*\rangle=\bar x$ and $\mathrm{Var}\,\allowbreak x^*=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=\frac{n-1}{n}s^2$. The resample mean $\bar x^*=\frac1n\sum x^*_j$ of $n$ independent draws has $\mathrm{Var}\,\allowbreak \bar x^*=\frac1n\mathrm{Var}\,\allowbreak x^*=\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{s^2}{n}$.

(b) $s=4.47\times10^{-4}$, $n=5$: bootstrap SE $=\sqrt{4/5}\times4.47\times10^{-4}/\sqrt5=1.79\times10^{-4}$ Å versus the usual $2.00\times10^{-4}$ Å. The bootstrap is $11\%$ low for $n=5$ (a small-sample bias of the $(n-1)/n$ factor), negligible for $n\gtrsim30$. For a $95\%$ interval with $n=5$ the $t$ quantile is still the better choice: the bootstrap shines when $n$ is moderate to large.

(c) The median has no simple closed-form standard error (it depends on the density at the median); the bootstrap just computes the median of each resample and takes the spread, with no derivation. Likewise for peak positions, ratios of fitted parameters, and the output of a complicated analysis pipeline. For time series, resampling single points destroys the correlations and gives the naive (too small) error; instead resample blocks of consecutive samples longer than the correlation time (block bootstrap), or fit-then-resample residuals.

Problem 11.12Challenge

(a) Derive the posterior for a Gaussian prior $N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)$ and one Gaussian measurement $x\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)$ and show that precisions add. (b) A DFT calculation predicts a lattice parameter of $4.050\pm0.030$ Å (the functional error as prior) and an X-ray measurement gives $4.0497\pm0.0008$ Å. Combine them. (c) A different property has prior $4.00\pm0.01$ and measurement $4.05\pm0.02$: find the posterior and say which dominates. (d) Show that ridge regression is the MAP estimate for a Gaussian prior and identify $\lambda$.

Hint

Multiply Gaussians and complete the square in $\theta$: $-\tfrac12\bigl[(\theta-\mu_0)^2/\sigma_0^2+(x-\theta)^2/\sigma^2\bigr]$.

Full solution

(a) $\ln p(\theta|x)=-\tfrac12\Bigl[\dfrac{(\theta-\mu_0)^2}{\sigma_0^2}+\dfrac{(x-\theta)^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr]+\mathrm{const}$. Collect terms in $\theta$: coefficient of $\theta^2$ is $-\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac1{\sigma_0^2}+\tfrac1{\sigma^2}\bigr)$, so the posterior variance obeys $\dfrac1{\sigma_\mathrm{post}^2}=\dfrac1{\sigma_0^2}+\dfrac1{\sigma^2}$ (precisions add); the linear term gives the mean $\mu_\mathrm{post}=\sigma_\mathrm{post}^2\bigl(\dfrac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}+\dfrac x{\sigma^2}\bigr)$, a precision-weighted average.

(b) Precisions: $1/0.030^2=1111$ and $1/0.0008^2=1.5625\times10^6$, total $1.5636\times10^6$, so $\sigma_\mathrm{post}=0.000800$ Å. The weights are $0.0007$ and $0.9993$: $\mu_\mathrm{post}=4.0497$ Å. The measurement is $1400$ times more precise than the prior, so the prior has no influence: $4.0497\pm0.0008$ Å. (This is the usual reason for trusting experiment over a DFT prediction when the experiment is available, and it is the right answer if the experimental uncertainty includes all systematics.)

(c) Precisions: $1/0.01^2=10\,000$ and $1/0.02^2=2500$, sum $12\,500$; $\sigma_\mathrm{post}=0.0089$. $\mu_\mathrm{post}=(4.00\times10^4+4.05\times2500)/12\,\allowbreak 500=(40\,000+10\,125)/12\,\allowbreak 500=4.010$. The prior carries $80\%$ of the weight, so the posterior $4.010\pm0.009$ is pulled only a fifth of the way to the measurement. If the two disagree by many sigma ($4.00\pm0.01$ against $4.05\pm0.02$ is a $2.2\sigma$ discrepancy), that is itself a warning that the prior or the data are wrong, not just a number to average.

(d) With likelihood $e^{-\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2/2\sigma^2}$ and prior $e^{-\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2/2\tau^2}$, the negative log posterior is $\dfrac1{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2+\dfrac1{2\tau^2}\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2$, proportional to $\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2+\dfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2$. This is the ridge objective with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$: strong prior belief in small coefficients (small $\tau$) means strong regularisation, and noisy data (large $\sigma$) also calls for it.

A time series has $\rho_k=0.9^k$ and $N=5000$. Find $N_\mathrm{eff}$.

Solution

$\tau_\mathrm{int}=\tfrac12+9=9.5$, $N_\mathrm{eff}=N/19=263$. The error is $\sqrt{19}=4.4$ times larger than the naive one.

Quiz 4Least squares

For $(1,2.1),\allowbreak (2,3.9),\allowbreak (3,6.2),\allowbreak (4,7.8),\allowbreak (5,10.1)$ state the fitted slope and its standard error.

Solution

$b=1.99\pm0.06$ (Example 4.1).

Quiz 5Goodness of fit

A fit with $\nu=8$ degrees of freedom has $\chi^2=25.6$. Interpret.

Solution

Reduced $\chi^2=3.2\gg1$ (a probability of about $0.1\%$ if the model were right): the model is inadequate or the errors are underestimated.

Quiz 6Ridge and Bayes

Prior $0.80\pm0.10$ eV and measurement $0.90\pm0.05$ eV. Find the posterior mean and standard deviation.

Solution

Precisions $100+400=500$; mean $(80+360)/500=0.88$ eV; standard deviation $0.045$ eV.
