Calculus of variations and optimisation

Nature minimises: a soap film minimises area, an elastic body minimises its energy, a ground-state electron density minimises the total energy, and a microstructure evolves downhill in free energy. When the quantity being minimised depends on an entire function, such as a concentration profile $c(\mathbf r)$ or a displacement field $\mathbf u(\mathbf r)$, the right tool is the calculus of variations. It yields the Euler–Lagrange equations, the chemical potential of the phase-field method, the Schrödinger equation as an eigenproblem, and the weak form that underlies finite elements. Optimisation algorithms (gradient descent, conjugate gradients, Newton) then find the minima on a computer.

Lecture 1. Functionals#

A functional $F[y]$ assigns a number to a whole function $y(x)$. The square brackets mark the difference from an ordinary function. Examples:

  • Length of a curve $y(x)$ from $a$ to $b$: $\displaystyle\int_a^b\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$.
  • Elastic energy of a stretched string or bar: $\displaystyle\int\Bigl(\tfrac12ku'^2-fu\Bigr)\mathrm dx$, internal energy plus the work of the load.
  • Ginzburg–Landau free energy of a composition or order-parameter field: $\displaystyle F[c]=\int\Bigl[f(c)+\tfrac12\kappa|\nabla c|^2\Bigr]\mathrm dV$, a bulk (double-well) part plus an interfacial penalty.
  • Action in mechanics, $\int(T-U)\,\mathrm dt$, whose stationary paths obey Newton’s laws.
  • Total energy as a functional of the electron density, $E[n]$, in DFT.

The task is to find the function that makes $F$ stationary, usually a minimum, subject to boundary conditions and possibly constraints.

Worked example 1.1: comparing trial functions

Evaluate $F[y]=\int_0^1y'^2\,\mathrm dx$ with $y(0)=0$, $y(1)=1$ for $y=x$, $y=x^2$ and $y=x^3$. Which is smallest, and can you prove the straight line is the minimiser?

$y=x$: $F=\int_0^11\,\mathrm dx=1$. $y=x^2$: $F=\int_0^14x^2\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac43$. $y=x^3$: $F=\int_0^19x^4\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac95$. The line is lowest of the three. Proof: any admissible function is $y=x+\eta$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(1)=0$, and $F=\int(1+\eta')^2\mathrm dx=1+2\int\eta'\mathrm dx+\int\eta'^2\mathrm dx=1+2[\eta]_0^1+\int\eta'^2\mathrm dx=1+\int\eta'^2\mathrm dx\ge1$, with equality only when $\eta'=0$. This is the second-variation argument in miniature: the linear term in $\eta$ vanishes (stationarity) and the quadratic term is positive (a minimum).

Lecture 2. The Euler–Lagrange equation#

Let $F[y]=\displaystyle\int_a^bL\bigl(x,y,y'\bigr)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$ with $y(a)$ and $y(b)$ fixed. Suppose $y$ is a minimiser and consider a nearby trial function $y+\varepsilon\eta$, where $\eta(x)$ is any smooth function with $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$. Then $F(\varepsilon)=F[y+\varepsilon\eta]$ is an ordinary function of $\varepsilon$ with a minimum at $\varepsilon=0$, so $\mathrm dF/\mathrm d\varepsilon|_0=0$ (MATH 101). By the chain rule, $$0=\int_a^b\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\eta+\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\eta'\Bigr)\mathrm dx=\int_a^b\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\Bigr)\eta\,\mathrm dx+\Bigl[\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\eta\Bigr]_a^b,$$ after integrating the second term by parts. The boundary term vanishes because $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$. Since $\eta$ is arbitrary, the bracket must vanish identically (the fundamental lemma): $$\boxed{\ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\Bigr)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}=0\ }\qquad\text{(Euler–Lagrange equation).}$$ This is a second-order ODE for $y$. It states that the first variation vanishes (stationarity); whether the solution is a minimum needs the second variation, but in most physical energy problems it is. If $y(a)$ or $y(b)$ is not fixed, the boundary term must vanish by itself, giving a natural boundary condition $\partial L/\partial y'=0$ at the free end; for the elastic bar this is the zero-force condition. For functionals of $y''$ the equation becomes $\partial_yL-\mathrm d_x(\partial_{y'}L)+\mathrm d_x^2(\partial_{y''}L)=0$ (Problem 12).

Worked example 2.1: the shortest path is a straight line

Minimise the length $\int\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,\mathrm dx$ between two points.

$L=\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ does not depend on $y$, so the equation reads $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\dfrac{y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=0$, so $y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ is constant, so $y'$ is constant and $y$ is a straight line. In general, whenever $L$ does not contain $y$, $\partial L/\partial y'$ is conserved (the analogue of momentum conservation, a case of Noether’s theorem).

The Beltrami identity#

If $L$ does not depend explicitly on $x$ (translation symmetry: the problem looks the same everywhere), multiplying the Euler–Lagrange equation by $y'$ shows that $$L-y'\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}=\text{constant}$$ along the solution: a first integral, reducing the order of the problem (the analogue of energy conservation). This is the key to one-dimensional interface problems.

Worked example 2.2: the equilibrium interface of a double-well free energy

A planar interface between two phases $c=\pm1$ minimises $F[c]=\int\bigl[f(c)+\tfrac12\kappa c'^2\bigr]\mathrm dx$ with $f(c)=\tfrac A4(1-c^2)^2$ and $c\to\pm1$ as $x\to\pm\infty$. Find its profile, width and energy.

With $L=f(c)+\tfrac12\kappa c'^2$, Beltrami gives $f+\tfrac12\kappa c'^2-\kappa c'^2=f-\tfrac12\kappa c'^2=\text{const}$. At $x\to\pm\infty$, $c'\to0$ and $f\to0$, so the constant is $0$: equipartition of the two contributions, $\tfrac12\kappa c'^2=f(c)$. Then $c'=\sqrt{2f/\kappa}=\sqrt{\tfrac{A}{2\kappa}}\,\allowbreak (1-c^2)$, which integrates to $$c(x)=\tanh\Bigl(\frac xw\Bigr),\qquad w=\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{A}} .$$ (Check against the Euler–Lagrange equation $\kappa c''=f'(c)=Ac(c^2-1)$: with $c=\tanh(x/w)$, $c''=-2c(1-c^2)/w^2$, and $\kappa c''=-\tfrac{2\kappa}{w^2}c(1-c^2)=Ac(c^2-1)$. ✓) The interfacial energy per unit area is $\gamma=\int(f+\tfrac12\kappa c'^2)\mathrm dx=\int\kappa c'^2\mathrm dx=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2\kappa f(c)}\,\allowbreak \mathrm dc=\sqrt{\tfrac{\kappa A}{2}}\int_{-1}^1(1-c^2)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dc=\tfrac43\sqrt{\tfrac{\kappa A}2}=\tfrac23Aw$. A steeper well (large $A$) or smaller gradient penalty makes the interface thinner but, for fixed $\kappa$, costlier, $\gamma\propto\sqrt{\kappa A}$. This calibrates a phase-field model: choose $\kappa$ and $A$ to reproduce the physical interface energy $\gamma$ and a width $w$ that the grid can resolve (Problem 3).

Worked example 2.3: a bar with a free end

A bar of stiffness $\kappa$ and length $L$ is fixed at $x=0$, free at $x=L$, and carries a uniform load $f$. Minimise $J[u]=\int_0^L\bigl(\tfrac12\kappa u'^2-fu\bigr)\mathrm dx$.

The Euler–Lagrange equation is $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}(\kappa u')+f=0$, i.e. $\kappa u''=-f$. At $x=0$ the displacement is fixed, $u(0)=0$. At the free end $u$ is not prescribed, so the boundary term $\kappa u'\eta\big|_{L}$ must vanish for arbitrary $\eta(L)$: the natural boundary condition $u'(L)=0$ (zero stress at a free surface) appears on its own. Integrating, $u=\dfrac f\kappa\bigl(Lx-\tfrac12x^2\bigr)$, which has $u'(L)=0$ ✓ and tip displacement $fL^2/(2\kappa)$. Fixing both ends instead gives $u=\dfrac f{2\kappa}x(L-x)$ with maximum $fL^2/(8\kappa)$, four times smaller at the midpoint than the tip of the cantilever-like bar: the free end is less constrained.

Lecture 3. Functional derivatives and gradient flows#

For a functional of a field $c(\mathbf r)$ in several dimensions the first variation is $$\delta F=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta c}\,\delta c\,\mathrm dV,$$ which defines the functional derivative $\delta F/\delta c(\mathbf r)$: the rate of change of $F$ when $c$ is changed at the point $\mathbf r$. It is the continuum analogue of the gradient $\partial F/\partial c_i$ of a function of many variables. For the Ginzburg–Landau functional $F=\int[f(c)+\tfrac12\kappa|\nabla c|^2]\mathrm dV$: $$\delta F=\int\bigl[f'(c)\,\delta c+\kappa\nabla c\cdot\nabla\delta c\bigr]\mathrm dV=\int\bigl[f'(c)-\kappa\nabla^2c\bigr]\delta c\,\mathrm dV+\oint\kappa\,\delta c\,\partial_nc\,\mathrm dS,$$ using the divergence theorem (MATH 201) for the second term; the surface term vanishes for periodic or no-flux boundary conditions. Hence $$\frac{\delta F}{\delta c}=f'(c)-\kappa\nabla^2c\equiv\mu ,$$ which is exactly the chemical potential of the Cahn–Hilliard equation (MATH 301). Equilibrium (a minimum of $F$ without constraint) is $\mu=0$; if the total amount $\int c\,\mathrm dV$ is conserved, a Lagrange multiplier (MATH 102) enters: $\mu=\text{const}$ throughout the system, the familiar equality of chemical potential between coexisting phases (Problem 4).

Dynamics as gradient flow#

The simplest dynamics that lowers the energy is steepest descent in function space. For a non-conserved order parameter $\eta$ (an ordering variable) the Allen–Cahn equation is $$\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=-M\frac{\delta F}{\delta\eta}=-M\bigl[f'(\eta)-\kappa\nabla^2\eta\bigr],$$ while for a conserved field the continuity equation with flux $\mathbf J=-M\nabla\mu$ gives the Cahn–Hilliard equation $\partial_tc=\nabla\cdot(M\nabla\mu)$ with $\mu=\delta F/\delta c$. In both cases the free energy is a Lyapunov functional: $$\frac{\mathrm dF}{\mathrm dt}=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}\mathrm dV=\begin{cases}-M\displaystyle\int\Bigl(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\eta}\Bigr)^2\mathrm dV\le0&\text{(Allen–Cahn)},\\[2mm]-M\displaystyle\int|\nabla\mu|^2\mathrm dV\le0&\text{(Cahn–Hilliard, by parts)}.\end{cases}$$ The energy never increases and stops changing only when $\delta F/\delta c$ (or $\nabla\mu$) vanishes. This guarantees that a numerical scheme with a decreasing energy is behaving, and it is the thermodynamic consistency that makes phase-field models robust. (The time evolution is the ODE gradient flow $\dot{\mathbf x}=-\nabla E$ of MATH 203, with the energy minima as stable fixed points.)

Worked example 3.1: relaxing near a pure phase

For $f=\tfrac A4(1-c^2)^2$ and the Allen–Cahn equation $\partial_tc=-M[f'(c)-\kappa\nabla^2c]$, find how a small perturbation of the uniform state $c=1$ relaxes.

$f'(c)=Ac(c^2-1)$ and $f''(c)=A(3c^2-1)$, so $f''(1)=2A$. Put $c=1+\delta$ and linearise: $\partial_t\delta=-M[2A\delta-\kappa\nabla^2\delta]$. A Fourier mode $e^{ikx}$ decays as $e^{-\sigma t}$ with $\sigma=M(2A+\kappa k^2)$. With the interface width $w=\sqrt{2\kappa/A}$ this is $\sigma=2AM\bigl(1+k^2w^2/4\bigr)$: long wavelengths relax at the rate $2AM$ and wavelengths shorter than the interface width are damped faster. For $A=1$, $\kappa=0.5$ ($w=1$), $M=1$ and $k=2$: $\sigma=2+0.5\times4=4$. Compare with the Cahn–Hilliard result of MATH 301, where in the unstable region $f''<0$ and the sign flips for long waves.

Lecture 4. Variational principles in quantum mechanics: Rayleigh–Ritz#

The ground-state energy $E_0$ of a Hamiltonian $\hat H$ is the minimum of the energy functional over all normalised wavefunctions: $$E[\psi]=\frac{\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\ \ge\ E_0 .$$ (Expand $\psi$ in eigenstates $\psi=\sum c_n\phi_n$: $E[\psi]=\sum|c_n|^2E_n/\sum|c_n|^2\ge E_0$, since every $E_n\ge E_0$.) This is the Rayleigh quotient of MATH 202. Two consequences. First, the eigenproblem is the Euler–Lagrange equation: minimising $\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle$ subject to the constraint $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$ with a Lagrange multiplier $E$ gives $\delta\bigl(\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle-E\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\bigr)=0$, i.e. $\hat H\psi=E\psi$. Second, any trial function gives an upper bound on $E_0$: choose a family $\psi(\alpha)$ with a few variational parameters, minimise $E(\alpha)$ and you obtain the best approximation within that family, always above the truth. The error in the energy is second order in the error of the wavefunction, so even rough trial functions give good energies. Expanding $\psi$ in a finite basis, $\psi=\sum c_\mu\phi_\mu$, and minimising gives the matrix eigenproblem $H\mathbf c=ES\mathbf c$ of MATH 202, and the energy can only decrease as the basis grows: the variational principle that makes basis-set convergence systematic. The Hohenberg–Kohn theorem makes the analogous statement for the density, $E[n]\ge E_0$, which is the foundation of DFT.

Worked example 4.1: a variational estimate for the harmonic oscillator

Use the trial function $\psi=e^{-\alpha x^2}$ for $\hat H=-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$.

For this Gaussian, $\langle T\rangle=\dfrac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2m}$ and $\langle V\rangle=\dfrac{m\omega^2}{8\alpha}$ (from the Gaussian integrals of MATH 101). So $E(\alpha)=\dfrac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2m}+\dfrac{m\omega^2}{8\alpha}$, with $E'(\alpha)=0$ at $\alpha=\dfrac{m\omega}{2\hbar}$ and $E=\dfrac12\hbar\omega$: the exact ground-state energy, because the trial family contains the exact ground state. The balance between kinetic energy (which favours spreading, small $\alpha$) and potential energy (which favours confinement, large $\alpha$) fixes the width. With a family that does not contain the exact answer, the bound is strict (Problem 5).

Worked example 4.2: a trial function for a particle in a box

Use $\psi=x(L-x)$ to estimate the ground-state energy of a particle in the box $0<x<L$ (units: $\hbar^2/m$ for energy, with $\hat H=-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2$ and $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$).

Integrating by parts, $\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int\psi'^2\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$. With $\psi'=L-2x$: $\int_0^L(L-2x)^2\mathrm dx=L^3/3$ and $\int_0^L\psi^2\mathrm dx=L^5/30$. The ratio is $10/L^2$, so $E[\psi]=\dfrac{10\hbar^2}{2mL^2}=5.000\,\allowbreak \dfrac{\hbar^2}{mL^2}$. The exact ground state is $\dfrac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}=4.935\,\allowbreak \dfrac{\hbar^2}{mL^2}$. The trial energy is above the exact one (as the variational principle demands) and only $1.3\%$ too high, although the parabola looks quite different from the sine: errors in energy are second order in the error in the wavefunction. Adding a second function, $x^2(L-x)^2$, and diagonalising the $2\times2$ matrix of MATH 202 improves this to within $0.0015\%$ of the exact value.

Lecture 5. Weak forms and the finite element method#

Many equations of materials modelling are Euler–Lagrange equations of an energy. The finite element method (FEM) exploits this by working directly with the integral form, avoiding second derivatives and handling complicated geometries and discontinuous coefficients. Consider the one-dimensional problem for a bar with stiffness $\kappa$ under a distributed load $f$ (or steady diffusion with a source): $$-\bigl(\kappa u'\bigr)'=f\ \ \text{on }(0,L),\qquad u(0)=u(L)=0\qquad\text{(strong form).}$$ Multiply by an arbitrary test function $v(x)$ with $v(0)=v(L)=0$ and integrate. Integrating the left side by parts moves one derivative onto $v$: $$\int_0^L\kappa\,u'v'\,\mathrm dx=\int_0^Lf\,v\,\mathrm dx\quad\text{for all admissible }v\qquad\text{(weak form).}$$ It needs only first derivatives, so $u$ may be piecewise linear. This is precisely the Euler–Lagrange equation of the energy $J[u]=\int\bigl(\tfrac12\kappa u'^2-fu\bigr)\mathrm dx$ (the first variation $\int\kappa u'\eta'-f\eta\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=0$ with $\eta=v$): the weak form is the principle of virtual work, and $u$ minimises the stored elastic energy minus the work of the loads. Natural boundary conditions (a prescribed flux or traction) appear automatically in the boundary terms.

Galerkin discretisation#

Divide the domain into elements with nodes $x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_{n+1}$ and approximate $u(x)\approx\sum_ju_j\varphi_j(x)$ with the piecewise-linear hat functions $\varphi_j$ ($=1$ at node $j$, $0$ at all other nodes, linear in between). Choosing the test functions from the same family ($v=\varphi_i$, the Galerkin method) turns the weak form into a linear system $$K\mathbf u=\mathbf F,\qquad K_{ij}=\int\kappa\,\varphi_i'\varphi_j'\,\mathrm dx,\quad F_i=\int f\varphi_i\,\mathrm dx .$$ $K$ (the stiffness matrix) is sparse (only neighbouring nodes interact), symmetric and positive definite (the energy $\tfrac12\mathbf u^TK\mathbf u>0$, MATH 202), so it is solved by Cholesky factorisation or conjugate gradients (section 6). For uniform spacing $h$ and constant $\kappa$ each element contributes $\frac\kappa h\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$, and the assembled matrix is $\dfrac\kappa h\,\allowbreak \mathrm{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$: the same three-point stencil as the finite difference method. The FEM solution is the best approximation to the true solution within the chosen function space, measured in the energy norm (Céa’s lemma), and its error decreases as $h^2$ in the $L^2$ norm for linear elements. In time-dependent problems the mass matrix $M_{ij}=\int\varphi_i\varphi_j\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$ appears, $M\dot{\mathbf u}+K\mathbf u=\mathbf F$, an ODE system of the kind studied in MATH 203, whose stiffness (largest eigenvalue of $M^{-1}K\sim\kappa/\rho h^2$) restricts explicit time steps as in MATH 301. The method is developed in Finite element method.

Worked example 5.1: a four-element solution

Solve $-u''=1$ on $(0,1)$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$ using $4$ equal linear elements ($h=0.25$) and compare with the exact solution.

The three interior nodes at $0.25,0.5,0.75$ give $K=\dfrac1h\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}$ and, since $\int\varphi_i\,\mathrm dx=h$, $F_i=h$. Multiplying by $h$: $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}\mathbf u=h^2\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}=0.0625\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}$. By symmetry $u_1=u_3$; the first two equations give $u_2=u_1+0.03125$ and $u_1=0.09375$, $u_2=0.125$. The exact solution $u=\tfrac12x(1-x)$ gives $u(0.25)=0.09375$ and $u(0.5)=0.125$: identical at the nodes. (For this 1D problem the linear-element solution is exact at the nodes; between nodes it is piecewise linear, with an error of order $h^2$.) The discrete strain energy is $\tfrac12\mathbf u^TK\mathbf u=\tfrac12\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf F=\tfrac12h\sum u_i=0.0391$, slightly below the exact $\tfrac1{24}=0.0417$. Equivalently the discrete potential energy $J=-0.0391$ lies above the exact minimum $-0.0417$: the finite element solution is the minimiser over a restricted family of functions, so it gives a variational upper bound on $J$, and the error shrinks as the mesh is refined.

Worked example 5.2: two elements with different stiffness

A bar of length $1$ is fixed at $x=0$ and pulled by $P=10$ at $x=1$. The left half has $\kappa=100$, the right half $\kappa=200$. Use two linear elements.

An element of length $h=0.5$ and stiffness $\kappa$ acts like a spring $k_e=\kappa/h$: $k_1=200$, $k_2=400$. Assembling for the free nodes ($x=0.5$ and $x=1$): $K=\begin{pmatrix}k_1+k_2&-k_2\\-k_2&k_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}600&-400\\-400&400\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf F=(0,10)^T$. Solving, $u_1=0.05$ and $u_2=0.075$; this is simply two springs in series, $u_1=P/k_1$ and $u_2=u_1+P/k_2$. Checks: $600(0.05)-400(0.075)=0$ and $-400(0.05)+400(0.075)=10$ ✓. Both strains are constant in each element ($0.1$ and $0.05$): FEM handles a jump in stiffness without any special treatment because only first derivatives enter the weak form.

Lecture 6. Optimisation algorithms#

After discretisation, a variational problem becomes: minimise a function $E(\mathbf x)$ of many variables (atomic coordinates, nodal values, parameters of a model). Algorithms use the gradient $\mathbf g=\nabla E$ and the Hessian $H$ (MATH 102).

Gradient descent and its convergence#

The simplest method follows the negative gradient: $\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k-\alpha\nabla E(\mathbf x_k)$, the explicit Euler discretisation of the gradient flow. Near a minimum $E\approx\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\mathbf x^*)^TH(\mathbf x-\mathbf x^*)$, so each eigen-component of the error (eigenvectors of $H$, MATH 202) is multiplied by $1-\alpha\lambda_i$ every step. All components decay only if $\alpha<2/\lambda_\mathrm{max}$ (the same stability limit as in MATH 203), and the best choice $\alpha=2/(\lambda_\mathrm{max}+\lambda_\mathrm{min})$ gives the contraction factor $$\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1},\qquad\kappa=\frac{\lambda_\mathrm{max}}{\lambda_\mathrm{min}}\ \text{(condition number of }H).$$ When $\kappa\gg1$ (a long narrow valley: stiff bonds and soft torsions, or a fine mesh) the factor is close to $1$ and convergence is painfully slow: the iterates zigzag across the valley, and the number of iterations to reduce the error by $10^{-6}$ is $\approx\tfrac12\kappa\ln10^6$. The stiff directions force a small step, and the soft directions then move slowly. This is stiffness again (MATH 203).

Conjugate gradients, Newton and quasi-Newton#

  • Conjugate gradients (CG) builds search directions that are orthogonal with respect to the Hessian, so progress along one direction is never undone by the next. For a quadratic of $n$ variables it terminates in at most $n$ steps in exact arithmetic, and its convergence factor is $\dfrac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$: it depends on $\sqrt\kappa$, not $\kappa$ (Problem 10). It needs only gradient evaluations, so it is the standard solver for the large sparse systems $K\mathbf u=\mathbf F$ of finite elements and for relaxing atomic structures; preconditioning reduces $\kappa$.
  • Newton’s method uses the Hessian: $\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k-H^{-1}\mathbf g$. It is the multi-dimensional version of the iteration of MATH 101, converges quadratically near a minimum, and solves a quadratic in one step, but needs $H$ (costly) and can head towards a saddle or maximum if $H$ is not positive definite (Problem 9).
  • Quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, and the limited-memory L-BFGS) build an approximation to $H^{-1}$ from successive gradients, giving superlinear convergence without second derivatives; L-BFGS and the damped-dynamics method FIRE are the usual choices for geometry optimisation of structures. A line search or trust region keeps each step productive.

Many minima: global optimisation#

The methods above find the nearest local minimum. Energy landscapes of clusters, alloys and crystal structures have enormous numbers of local minima. Simulated annealing runs a Metropolis random walk (MATH 204) at a temperature that is slowly lowered: at high $T$ the system crosses barriers (an uphill move of $\Delta E$ is accepted with probability $e^{-\Delta E/k_\mathrm{B}T}$), and as $T$ falls it settles into a deep minimum (Problem 11). Other approaches: basin hopping (random perturbation, local relaxation, Metropolis acceptance of the relaxed energy), genetic algorithms and particle swarm methods, and random structure searching. With a machine-learned energy model, stochastic gradient descent on mini-batches is how the model parameters themselves are optimised (Machine learning for materials), and the same landscape concerns (conditioning, saddles, learning rates) apply.

Worked example 6.1: how many iterations?

A quadratic energy has Hessian eigenvalues $1$ and $100$. Compare gradient descent, conjugate gradients and Newton's method for a $10^{-6}$ reduction of the error.

The condition number is $\kappa=100$. Optimal gradient descent uses $\alpha=2/(\lambda_\mathrm{max}+\lambda_\mathrm{min})=2/101$ and reduces the error by $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)=99/101=0.980$ per step, so $\ln10^{-6}/\ln0.980=691$ steps. Conjugate gradients has the factor $(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)=9/11=0.818$, needing about $69$ steps (and for a two-variable quadratic, at most $2$ steps in exact arithmetic). Newton's method solves a quadratic in one step. For a non-quadratic $E=e^x-2x$ (minimum at $x=\ln2=0.693147$), Newton's iteration $x\to x-(e^x-2)/e^x$ from $x=1$ gives $0.73576,\allowbreak \ 0.69404,\allowbreak \ 0.693148,\allowbreak \ 0.693147$: the error is $0.043,\allowbreak \ 9\times10^{-4},\allowbreak \ 4\times10^{-7},\allowbreak \ 8\times10^{-14}$, roughly squaring each step (quadratic convergence).

Summary of the unit#

A physical energy defined on functions is minimised by solving its Euler–Lagrange equation; in a field theory this uses the functional derivative, whose value is the chemical potential, and the dynamics that lowers the energy at maximum rate is the phase-field gradient flow. The same principle underlies quantum mechanics (Rayleigh–Ritz, giving upper bounds and eigenproblems) and the finite element method (the weak form is stationarity of an energy, and the discretised problem is a sparse positive-definite system). Finally, all of these end as minimisation in many variables, whose speed is set by the Hessian’s condition number: so the whole course comes together in one object, the Hessian, with its eigenvalues describing stiffness, stability, convergence speed and vibration frequencies.

Key takeaways
  • A functional maps a function to a number (an energy, an action, a free energy); its minimiser satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}$, obtained by integrating by parts, and the same logic with the functional derivative $\delta F/\delta c=f'(c)-\kappa\nabla^2c$ gives the chemical potential of phase-field theory.
  • Minimising the Ginzburg-Landau energy gives a diffuse interface $c=\tanh(x/w)$ of width $w=\sqrt{2\kappa/A}$ and interfacial energy $\tfrac23Aw$: the width and energy of the interface come from the competition between the double well and the gradient term.
  • Allen-Cahn and Cahn-Hilliard dynamics are gradient flows of the free energy, $\partial_tc=-M\,\delta F/\delta c$ (non-conserved) or $\nabla\cdot(M\nabla\,\delta F/\delta c)$ (conserved), so the energy never increases.
  • The Rayleigh-Ritz principle says any trial wavefunction has energy at or above the ground-state energy, so minimising over a family of trial functions gives an upper bound and, with Lagrange multipliers for normalisation, the Schrödinger eigenproblem; this is the basis of variational electronic-structure methods.
  • Multiplying a differential equation by a test function and integrating by parts gives the weak form, which is the finite element method's starting point; expanding in local basis functions turns it into a sparse symmetric positive-definite linear system $K\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f}$.
  • Gradient descent converges at a rate set by the condition number $\kappa$ of the Hessian, conjugate gradients at a rate set by $\sqrt{\kappa}$, and Newton's method quadratically near a minimum at the price of the Hessian; for non-convex landscapes annealing and basin hopping search for the global minimum.

Problem 12.1Basic

(a) Show that the Euler–Lagrange equation of $F[y]=\int_a^b\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$ gives straight lines, and find the shortest curve from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$ and its length. (b) For a functional with $L=L(y')$ only, show that every extremal is a straight line (unless $L''(y')=0$). (c) For $L=y'^2$, minimise $\int_0^1y'^2\,\mathrm dx$ subject to $y(0)=0$, $y(1)=1$ and interpret the result.

Hint

For $L=L(y')$ the Euler–Lagrange equation is $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}L'(y')=L''(y')\,\allowbreak y''=0$.

Full solution

(a) Shown in Example 2.1: $y''=0$. The line from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$ is $y=2x$, with length $\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4}\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\sqrt5=2.236$.

(b) With $L=L(y')$: $\partial L/\partial y=0$ and the equation is $\mathrm d(L'(y'))/\mathrm dx=L''(y')y''=0$. So either $y''=0$ (a straight line) or $L''(y')=0$ (only for degenerate $L$ linear in $y'$, where the functional is a boundary term and any curve extremises it).

(c) $L=y'^2$: $y''=0$, so $y=x$ (the unique line with the given endpoints), and the minimum value is $\int_0^11\,\mathrm dx=1$. Interpretation: a uniform temperature gradient minimises the dissipation $\int\kappa T'^2\,\mathrm dx$ for fixed end temperatures, the principle of minimum entropy production for steady conduction.

Problem 12.2Basic

A stretched string with unit tension carries a uniform load $g$. Its energy is $J[y]=\int_0^1\bigl(\tfrac12y'^2-gy\bigr)\mathrm dx$ with $y(0)=y(1)=0$. (a) Derive the Euler–Lagrange equation and solve it. (b) Evaluate the minimum energy. (c) Verify that a trial function $y_t=a\sin\pi x$ gives an energy at or above the minimum and find the best $a$.

Hint

$L=\tfrac12y'^2-gy$: $\partial_{y'}L=y'$, $\partial_yL=-g$, so $y''=-g$. For (c), $\int_0^1\sin\pi x\,\mathrm dx=2/\pi$ and $\int_0^1\cos^2\pi x\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\tfrac12$.

Full solution

(a) $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}(y')-(-g)=0\Rightarrow y''=-g$. Integrating with $y(0)=y(1)=0$: $y=\tfrac g2x(1-x)$.

(b) $J=\int(\tfrac12y'^2-gy)$; with $y'=\tfrac g2(1-2x)$: $\int_0^1\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{g^2}4(1-2x)^2\mathrm dx=\tfrac{g^2}8\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac{g^2}{24}$, and $\int_0^1gy\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\tfrac{g^2}2\cdot\tfrac16=\tfrac{g^2}{12}$. So $J_\mathrm{min}=\tfrac{g^2}{24}-\tfrac{g^2}{12}=-\tfrac{g^2}{24}$ (equal to $-\tfrac12\int gy\,\mathrm dx$, as expected for a linear system: elastic energy is half the work of the load).

(c) $y_t=a\sin\pi x$: $\int\tfrac12y_t'^2=\tfrac12a^2\pi^2\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac{\pi^2a^2}4$ and $\int gy_t=\tfrac{2ga}{\pi}$. $J(a)=\tfrac{\pi^2}{4}a^2-\tfrac{2g}{\pi}a$, minimised at $a=\dfrac{4g}{\pi^3}$, giving $J=-\dfrac{4g^2}{\pi^4}=-0.04106\,g^2$. Compare $-g^2/24=-0.04167\,g^2$: the trial energy is higher (less negative) by $1.5\%$, as the variational principle demands, and the trial shape $0.129g\sin\pi x$ differs from the true parabola by about $3\%$ at the midpoint ($0.1290g$ against $0.125g$), yet the energy is wrong by only $1.5\%$. This is the Rayleigh–Ritz method with a single basis function.

Problem 12.4Core

(a) Compute $\delta F/\delta c$ for $F=\int\bigl[ac^2+bc^4+\tfrac12\kappa|\nabla c|^2\bigr]\mathrm dV$ and write the Allen–Cahn and Cahn–Hilliard equations. (b) For $F=\int\tfrac12\kappa(c)|\nabla c|^2\mathrm dV$ with $c$-dependent $\kappa$, show $\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta c}=-\kappa\nabla^2c-\tfrac12\kappa'|\nabla c|^2$. (c) Prove $\mathrm dF/\mathrm dt\le0$ for the Allen–Cahn equation. (d) For the Cahn–Hilliard equation with the constraint $\int c\,\mathrm dV=\text{const}$, what is the equilibrium condition?

Hint

(b) Vary: $\delta\bigl[\tfrac12\kappa(c)|\nabla c|^2\bigr]=\tfrac12\kappa'\delta c|\nabla c|^2+\kappa\nabla c\cdot\nabla\delta c$; integrate the last term by parts.

Full solution

(a) $\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta c}=2ac+4bc^3-\kappa\nabla^2c$. Allen–Cahn: $\partial_tc=-M(2ac+4bc^3-\kappa\nabla^2c)$. Cahn–Hilliard: $\partial_tc=M\nabla^2(2ac+4bc^3-\kappa\nabla^2c)$ (for constant $M$), a fourth-order equation; the spinodal region corresponds to $a<0$.

(b) $\delta F=\int\bigl[\tfrac12\kappa'|\nabla c|^2\delta c+\kappa\nabla c\cdot\nabla\delta c\bigr]\mathrm dV$. Integrate the second term by parts: $\int\kappa\nabla c\cdot\nabla\delta c=-\int\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla c)\,\allowbreak \delta c+(\text{boundary})$. And $\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla c)=\kappa'|\nabla c|^2+\kappa\nabla^2c$. So $\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta c}=\tfrac12\kappa'|\nabla c|^2-\kappa'|\nabla c|^2-\kappa\nabla^2c=-\kappa\nabla^2c-\tfrac12\kappa'|\nabla c|^2$. The extra term, absent for constant $\kappa$, makes a composition-dependent gradient energy exert an additional force on interfaces.

(c) $\dfrac{\mathrm dF}{\mathrm dt}=\int\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta\eta}\partial_t\eta\,\allowbreak \mathrm dV=-M\int\Bigl(\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta\eta}\Bigr)^2\mathrm dV\le0$ for $M>0$, with equality only where $\delta F/\delta\eta=0$ everywhere (an equilibrium).

(d) Add a Lagrange multiplier: stationary points of $F-\lambda\int c\,\mathrm dV$ satisfy $\delta F/\delta c=\lambda$, i.e. $\mu(\mathbf r)=\text{const}$ throughout the system (and $\nabla\mu=0$, so the Cahn–Hilliard flux vanishes). Two coexisting phases then have the same chemical potential though different compositions: the common-tangent construction of phase diagrams. The value of the constant, $\lambda$, is the chemical potential of the reservoir.

Problem 12.5Challenge

Apply the Rayleigh–Ritz method to the harmonic oscillator $\hat H=-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ with the non-smooth trial function $\psi=e^{-\alpha|x|}$. (a) Compute $\langle T\rangle$, $\langle V\rangle$ and $E(\alpha)$. (b) Minimise to find the best $\alpha$ and the energy; compare with $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$. (c) Explain why the bound is above the exact value and why this trial function does worse than a Gaussian. (d) Verify the virial relation $\langle T\rangle=\langle V\rangle$ for the Gaussian and the failure for this trial function.

Hint

$\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-2\alpha|x|}\mathrm dx=1/\alpha$, $\int x^2e^{-2\alpha|x|}\mathrm dx=\dfrac1{2\alpha^3}$. Use $\langle T\rangle=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{\int\psi'^2}{\int\psi^2}$ (integration by parts, valid since $\psi$ is continuous).

Full solution

(a) $\int\psi^2=1/\alpha$. $\psi'=\mp\alpha e^{-\alpha|x|}$, so $\int\psi'^2=\alpha^2/\alpha=\alpha$. $\langle T\rangle=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{\alpha}{1/\alpha}=\dfrac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m}$. $\langle x^2\rangle=\dfrac{1/(2\alpha^3)}{1/\alpha}=\dfrac1{2\alpha^2}$, so $\langle V\rangle=\dfrac{m\omega^2}{4\alpha^2}$. $E(\alpha)=\dfrac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m}+\dfrac{m\omega^2}{4\alpha^2}$.

(b) $\dfrac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm d\alpha}=\dfrac{\hbar^2\alpha}{m}-\dfrac{m\omega^2}{2\alpha^3}=0\Rightarrow\alpha^4=\dfrac{m^2\omega^2}{2\hbar^2}$, $\alpha^2=\dfrac{m\omega}{\sqrt2\hbar}$. Then $\langle T\rangle=\dfrac{\hbar\omega}{2\sqrt2}=0.354\hbar\omega$ and $\langle V\rangle=\dfrac{m\omega^2\sqrt2\hbar}{4m\omega}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{4}\hbar\omega=0.354\hbar\omega$. $E_\mathrm{min}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\hbar\omega=0.707\,\allowbreak \hbar\omega$, $41\%$ above the exact $0.5\hbar\omega$.

(c) The variational principle guarantees $E_\mathrm{trial}\ge E_0$. The exponential trial function has a cusp at $x=0$ (a kink in $\psi$, a delta function in $\psi''$) whereas the true eigenfunction is smooth, and it decays too slowly at large $x$ compared with the Gaussian tail $e^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}$; neither matches the Gaussian family that contains the exact answer. The energy is nevertheless a reasonable approximation, in line with the principle that errors in the energy are second order in the error of $\psi$.

(d) The virial theorem for a harmonic potential states $\langle T\rangle=\langle V\rangle$ at an eigenstate. For the Gaussian trial function at its optimum, $\tfrac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2m}=\tfrac{m\omega^2}{8\alpha}$ gives exactly equality. For the exponential, minimisation also gives $\langle T\rangle=\langle V\rangle$ ($0.354=0.354$ above): at any variational minimum of a family that can be rescaled ($\psi(x)\to\sqrt s\,\psi(sx)$), the virial theorem is automatically satisfied, so it is a check on the minimisation but not on the accuracy of the shape.

$E_\mathrm{min}=0.707\,\allowbreak \hbar\omega\ge0.5\,\allowbreak \hbar\omega$ (Gaussian trial gives exactly $0.5\,\hbar\omega$)

Problem 12.6Core

For $-(\kappa u')'=f$ on $(0,L)$ with $u(0)=0$ and a prescribed flux (traction) $\kappa u'(L)=q$: (a) derive the weak form; (b) show it is the stationarity condition of $J[u]=\int(\tfrac12\kappa u'^2-fu)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx-qu(L)$; (c) show that the condition $\kappa u'(L)=q$ is a natural boundary condition that need not be imposed on the trial functions, whereas $u(0)=0$ is essential; (d) show that the solution is unique.

Hint

Choose test functions with $v(0)=0$ but $v(L)$ free. Integrate by parts: $\int_0^L(\kappa u')'v=[\kappa u'v]_0^L-\int\kappa u'v'$.

Full solution

(a) Multiply by $v$ ($v(0)=0$) and integrate: $-\int_0^L(\kappa u')'v\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\int fv\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$. By parts: $-[\kappa u'v]_0^L+\int\kappa u'v'=\int fv$. At $x=0$, $v=0$; at $x=L$, $\kappa u'(L)=q$, so $-qv(L)$ appears: $\displaystyle\int_0^L\kappa u'v'\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\int_0^Lfv\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx+q\,\allowbreak v(L)$ for all such $v$.

(b) $\delta J=\int(\kappa u'v'-fv)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx-qv(L)$ for a variation $v$. Setting $\delta J=0$ gives exactly the weak form.

(c) The boundary condition $\kappa u'(L)=q$ arises from the boundary term in the integration by parts; since $v(L)$ is free, the stationarity of $J$ forces it. So in a finite element code the traction is simply added to the load vector and no constraint is applied at $x=L$. The condition $u(0)=0$ must be imposed on the trial and test spaces ($v(0)=0$): it is an essential (Dirichlet) boundary condition. This distinction is general: Dirichlet conditions are imposed on the function space, Neumann conditions come for free.

(d) If $u_1,u_2$ both solve the weak form, then $w=u_1-u_2$ satisfies $\int\kappa w'v'=0$ for all admissible $v$; choose $v=w$: $\int\kappa w'^2=0$, so $w'=0$, and $w(0)=0$ gives $w\equiv0$ (the energy method of MATH 301, Problem 9.12). Existence and uniqueness for this symmetric positive-definite form follow from the Lax–Milgram theorem.

Problem 12.7Core

Use $4$ equal linear elements to solve $-u''=x$ on $(0,1)$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$. (a) Assemble the $3\times3$ system (take $F_i=\int x\varphi_i\,\mathrm dx=hx_i$). (b) Solve for the nodal values and compare with the exact solution $u=(x-x^3)/6$. (c) What are the element stiffness matrix and the connectivity of element 2? (d) How does the error behave between the nodes, if you interpolate linearly, and how would it change on halving $h$?

$K\mathbf u=\mathbf F$ with $\dfrac1h\,\allowbreak \mathrm{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)\mathbf u=h\,\allowbreak (0.25,0.5,0.75)^T$. Multiply by $h$: $\mathrm{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)\mathbf u=h^2(0.25,0.5,0.75)^T$.

(c) Element 2 spans nodes $1$ and $2$ ($x\in[0.25,0.5]$) with local stiffness $\dfrac1h\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4&-4\\-4&4\end{pmatrix}$ (for $\kappa=1$), added into rows and columns $(1,2)$ of the global matrix; each interior node receives $4+4=8$ on the diagonal from its two elements, i.e. $2/h$ after scaling. That is the assembly step common to all finite element codes: loop over elements, compute local matrices, add into the global matrix according to the connectivity.

(d) Between nodes the piecewise-linear approximation differs from the smooth exact function by up to $\tfrac18h^2\max|u''|=\tfrac18(0.0625)(1)=0.0078$ (here $|u''|=x\le1$). Halving $h$ divides this by $4$: second-order convergence of the maximum error, and first order in the gradient $u'$ (the flux is piecewise constant, with error $\propto h$).

Minimise $E(x,y)=\tfrac12(x^2+10y^2)$ by gradient descent from $(10,1)$ with a fixed step $\alpha$. (a) Write the update and the factors by which the $x$- and $y$-errors are multiplied. (b) For which $\alpha$ does it converge? (c) What is the optimal $\alpha$ and the resulting convergence factor, and how many iterations reduce the error by $10^{-6}$? $\nabla E=(x,10y)$, so $x\mapsto(1-\alpha)x$ and $y\mapsto(1-10\alpha)y$. The error factor is the larger of $|1-\alpha|$ and $|1-10\alpha|$.

Full solution

(a) $x_{k+1}=(1-\alpha)x_k$, $y_{k+1}=(1-10\alpha)y_k$. The Hessian is $\mathrm{diag}(1,10)$, $\kappa=10$.

(b) Convergence needs $|1-\alpha|<1$ and $|1-10\alpha|<1$: $0<\alpha<0.2=2/\lambda_\mathrm{max}$. At $\alpha=0.2$ the $y$-error just flips sign each step ($1-10\alpha=-1$) and never decays; for $\alpha>0.2$ it grows (the stiff direction destabilises it). For $\alpha=0.1$: $y$ is killed in one step ($1-1=0$) but $x$ decays only by $0.9$ per step.

Problem 12.9Core

Minimise $E(x,y)=e^{x+y}+x^2+y^2$ by Newton’s method from $(0,0)$. (a) Write the gradient and Hessian. (b) Carry out two Newton steps. (c) The true minimum is at $x=y=-0.28357$; show how the gradient norm falls ($1.41,\allowbreak \ 0.151,\allowbreak \ 1.8\times10^{-3},\allowbreak \ 2.8\times10^{-7}$) and explain the convergence rate. (d) Eigenvalues of the Hessian at the minimum and the condition number.

Hint

With $s=e^{x+y}$: $\nabla E=(s+2x,\ s+2y)$ and $H=\begin{pmatrix}s+2&s\\s&s+2\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ and eigenvalues $2s+2$ and $2$.

Full solution

(a) As in the hint. At $(0,0)$: $s=1$, $\nabla E=(1,1)$, $H=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$.

(b) Step 1: solve $H\mathbf d=\mathbf g$: since $\mathbf g\propto(1,1)$ an eigenvector with eigenvalue $4$, $\mathbf d=\tfrac14(1,1)$; new point $(-0.25,-0.25)$. Step 2: $s=e^{-0.5}=0.60653$, $\mathbf g=(0.60653-0.5)(1,1)=0.10653(1,1)$, eigenvalue of $H$ along $(1,1)$ is $2s+2=3.21306$; $\mathbf d=0.10653/3.21306=0.03316$ each; new point $(-0.28316,-0.28316)$.

(c) Gradient norms: $1.414,\allowbreak \ 0.151,\allowbreak \ 0.0018,\allowbreak \ 2.8\times10^{-7},\allowbreak \ 6\times10^{-15}$. Each is roughly the square of the previous one (times a constant): quadratic convergence (the number of correct digits doubles), as shown in MATH 101 (Problem 1.7). Problem 12.10Challenge

The finite-difference or finite-element matrix for $-u''$ with $n$ interior nodes is $A=\mathrm{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_k=2-2\cos\dfrac{k\pi}{n+1}$, $k=1,\ldots,n$. (a) Show $\kappa=\lambda_\mathrm{max}/\lambda_\mathrm{min}\approx\dfrac{4(n+1)^2}{\pi^2}$. (b) Estimate the iterations needed by steepest descent and by CG to reduce the error by $10^{-6}$ for $n=10,100,1000$. (c) What do the results say about iterative solvers for fine meshes, and what is the role of preconditioning (e.g. multigrid)?

Hint

For small $\theta$, $1-\cos\theta\approx\theta^2/2$. Use $k=1$ for $\lambda_\mathrm{min}\approx(\pi/(n+1))^2$ and $\lambda_\mathrm{max}\approx4$. Iterations: $\ln10^6/\ln[(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)]$ and $\ln10^6/\ln\bigl[(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)\bigr]$.

Full solution

(a) $\lambda_\mathrm{min}=2-2\cos\dfrac\pi{n+1}\approx\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi}{n+1}\Bigr)^2$ and $\lambda_\mathrm{max}=2-2\cos\dfrac{n\pi}{n+1}\to4$. So $\kappa\approx\dfrac{4(n+1)^2}{\pi^2}$: it grows as the square of the number of mesh points (the largest eigenvalue $\sim4/h^2$ and the smallest $\sim\pi^2$ in physical units, so $\kappa\sim L^2/h^2$).

(b) $n=10$: $\kappa=48$; steepest descent $\approx334$ iterations, CG $\approx48$. $n=100$: $\kappa=4134$; steepest descent $\approx28\,500$, CG $\approx444$. $n=1000$: $\kappa=4.1\times10^5$; steepest descent $\approx2.8\times10^6$, CG $\approx4400$. Steepest-descent iterations scale as $n^2$ and CG as $n$.

(c) Even CG needs a number of iterations proportional to the number of mesh points per dimension, and each iteration costs one sparse matrix–vector product, so the total work grows faster than the number of unknowns for fine 2D and 3D meshes. Preconditioning replaces $A\mathbf u=\mathbf f$ by $P^{-1}A\mathbf u=P^{-1}\mathbf f$ with $P\approx A$ cheap to invert, so that $\kappa(P^{-1}A)$ is small. Multigrid is the extreme example: it attacks smooth error components on coarse grids, where they are not slow, and achieves iteration counts independent of $n$ (optimal $O(n)$ total work). The same reasoning (condition number controls iterations) applies to a geometry relaxation with stiff bonds and soft modes, where a good preconditioner from an approximate force-constant matrix can cut the iterations by a large factor.

(a) Compute the Metropolis acceptance probability $e^{-\Delta E/k_\mathrm{B}T}$ at $300$, $1000$ and $2000$ K. (b) Why is a quench to $300$ K unlikely to find the global minimum, and what does annealing do about it? (c) A geometric cooling schedule $T_k=T_0\alpha^k$ with $T_0=2000$ K and $\alpha=0.999$ per step: how many steps does it take to reach $300$ K, and how many attempts are made in the window where the barrier is crossed with probability between $0.5\%$ and $5\%$ per attempt? (d) Name the advantage of basin hopping.

Hint

(c) $T_k=300$: $k=\ln(300/2000)/\ln0.999$. Temperatures where $e^{-\Delta E/kT}=0.05$ and $0.005$: $T=\Delta E/(k_\mathrm{B}\ln20)$ and $\Delta E/(k_\mathrm{B}\ln200)$.

Full solution

(a) $k_\mathrm{B}T=0.02585$, $0.08617$, $0.1723$ eV. $e^{-0.5/0.02585}=3.98\times10^{-9}$; $e^{-5.80}=3.0\times10^{-3}$; $e^{-2.90}=0.055$. At $300$ K a barrier crossing is a one-in-$2.5\times10^8$ event per attempt: effectively never.

(b) A greedy quench from a random start just falls into the nearest local minimum and stays; the global minimum is found only if the starting point is in its basin. Annealing starts hot enough ($T\gtrsim1000$–$2000$ K here) that barriers are crossed routinely and the walker explores widely, with a Boltzmann preference for low energy; as $T$ is lowered the walker spends more time in deeper basins and finally freezes in a low-energy one. With a sufficiently slow schedule it finds the global minimum (a logarithmic schedule $T_k\gtrsim\Delta E_\mathrm{max}/(k_\mathrm{B}\ln k)$ is guaranteed to; geometric ones are the practical approximation).

(c) $k=\dfrac{\ln(0.15)}{\ln(0.999)}=\dfrac{-1.897}{-0.0010005}=1896$ steps from $2000$ K to $300$ K. Temperature where acceptance is $5\%$: $T=\dfrac{0.5}{8.617\times10^{-5}\times\ln20}=\dfrac{0.5}{2.582\times10^{-4}}=1937$ K; where it is $0.5\%$: $T=\dfrac{0.5}{8.617\times10^{-5}\times\ln200}=\dfrac{0.5}{4.566\times10^{-4}}=1095$ K. Steps between $1937$ K and $1095$ K: $\dfrac{\ln(1095/1937)}{\ln0.999}=\dfrac{-0.570}{-0.0010005}=570$ steps. In that window the system has to cross barriers while the preference for low energy builds; if the cooling is too fast there (fewer attempts than needed to cross), the walker is trapped in whichever basin it occupies. Slowing the cooling in the critical temperature window (where $C_V$ peaks) is the standard refinement.

(d) Basin hopping works on the energy of the locally relaxed structure, making the landscape a set of flat plateaus so that barriers of the original surface are bypassed; it needs fewer steps for clusters and molecules where relaxations are cheap, and the temperature-like parameter then needs only to cover the spread of minimum energies, not barrier heights.

acceptances $4\times10^{-9}$, $3\times10^{-3}$, $0.055$; 1896 steps to 300 K; crossing window $\approx570$ steps

Problem 12.12Challenge

An elastic beam of length $L$ with bending stiffness $EI$ under a uniform load $q$ has energy $J[w]=\int_0^L\bigl(\tfrac12EI\,w''^2-qw\bigr)\mathrm dx$, $w$ the deflection. (a) Derive the Euler–Lagrange equation $EI\,w''''=q$ for this second-order functional. (b) For a cantilever (clamped at $x=0$: $w=w'=0$; free at $x=L$) find the natural boundary conditions that emerge at the free end. (c) Solve and find the tip deflection. (d) For a steel beam ($E=200$ GPa) of square section $10\times10$ mm ($I=bh^3/12$), $L=1$ m and $q=10$ N/m, evaluate it.

Hint

First variation: $\int(EIw''\eta''-q\eta)\,\mathrm dx$; integrate the first term by parts twice. Boundary terms: $[EIw''\eta']_0^L-[(EIw'')'\eta]_0^L$.

Full solution

(a) $\delta J=\int_0^L(EIw''\eta''-q\eta)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$. Integrate by parts twice: $\int EIw''\eta''=[EIw''\eta']_0^L-[(EIw'')'\eta]_0^L+\int(EIw'')''\eta$. So $\delta J=\int\bigl[(EIw'')''-q\bigr]\eta\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx+[EIw''\eta']_0^L-[(EIw'')'\eta]_0^L$. For arbitrary $\eta$ in the interior: $EIw''''=q$.

(b) At $x=0$ the clamp fixes $w=0$ and $w'=0$, so $\eta(0)=\eta'(0)=0$ and the boundary terms vanish there. At $x=L$, $\eta$ and $\eta'$ are free, so their coefficients must vanish: $EIw''(L)=0$ (zero bending moment) and $(EIw'')'(L)=0$ (zero shear force). These are the natural boundary conditions: the free end carries no moment or shear, and they emerge from the variational principle without being imposed.

(c) $w''''=q/EI$: $w=\dfrac{q}{24EI}x^4+c_3x^3+c_2x^2+c_1x+c_0$. $w(0)=w'(0)=0\Rightarrow c_0=c_1=0$. $w''(L)=0$: $\dfrac{q}{2EI}L^2+6c_3L+2c_2=0$. $w'''(L)=0$: $\dfrac{q}{EI}L+6c_3=0\Rightarrow c_3=-\dfrac{qL}{6EI}$; then $2c_2=-\dfrac{qL^2}{2EI}+\dfrac{qL^2}{EI}=\dfrac{qL^2}{2EI}$, so $c_2=\dfrac{qL^2}{4EI}$. Hence $w(x)=\dfrac{q}{24EI}\bigl(x^4-4Lx^3+6L^2x^2\bigr)=\dfrac{qx^2}{24EI}(6L^2-4Lx+x^2)$. Tip: $w(L)=\dfrac{q}{24EI}(1-4+6)L^4=\dfrac{qL^4}{8EI}$.

(d) $I=\dfrac{(0.01)(0.01)^3}{12}=8.33\times10^{-10}$ m$^4$; $EI=200\times10^9\times8.33\times10^{-10}=166.7$ N m$^2$. $w(L)=\dfrac{10\times1^4}{8\times166.7}=7.5\times10^{-3}$ m $=7.5$ mm. The $L^4$ dependence makes long thin beams (and MEMS cantilevers, AFM tips) extremely compliant; doubling the length multiplies the deflection by $16$, while doubling the thickness divides it by $8$ (through $I\propto h^3$). Finite elements for beams use cubic Hermite basis functions, since the weak form involves $w''$ and the trial functions need continuous slopes.

$EIw''''=q$; natural BCs $w''=w'''=0$ at the free end; $w_\mathrm{tip}=qL^4/8EI=7.5$ mm

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Euler-Lagrange

Find the extremal of $\int_0^1(y^{\prime2}+y^2)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$ with $y(0)=0$, $y(1)=1$.

Solution

$L=y^{\prime2}+y^2$ gives $2y^{\prime\prime}-2y=0$, so $y=A\sinh x+B\cosh x$. $y(0)=0$ gives $B=0$ and $y(1)=1$ gives $A=1/\sinh1$: $y=\sinh x/\sinh1$.

Quiz 2Beltrami

State the first integral of $L=\tfrac12\kappa c^{\prime2}+f(c)$ and what it says at an interface with $c^\prime\to0$ far away.

Solution

$L-c^\prime\,\allowbreak \partial L/\partial c^\prime=f-\tfrac12\kappa c^{\prime2}=\text{const}=0$, so $\tfrac12\kappa c^{\prime2}=f(c)$: the gradient energy equals the bulk energy at every point of the interface.

Quiz 3Free end

For $J=\int_0^L(\tfrac12\kappa u^{\prime2}-fu)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$ with $u(0)=0$ and a free end, give the natural boundary condition and the tip displacement.

Solution

$u^\prime(L)=0$; $u=(f/\kappa)(Lx-x^2/2)$ and $u(L)=fL^2/(2\kappa)$.

Quiz 4Variational estimate

A trial wavefunction gives $E=5.000\,\hbar^2/(mL^2)$ for a particle in a box. The exact value is $4.935$. Is this consistent? What can you conclude?

Solution

Yes: the variational principle guarantees $E[\psi]\ge E_0$, and the estimate is $1.3\%$ high. Any trial energy below $4.935$ would signal an error.

Quiz 5Finite elements

Two springs in series, $k_1=200$ and $k_2=400$, with a force $P=10$ at the free end of the second. Find the end displacement.

Solution

$u_2=P/k_1+P/k_2=0.05+0.025=0.075$.

Quiz 6Optimisation

A Hessian has eigenvalues $1$ and $100$. Estimate the iteration counts of gradient descent and conjugate gradients for a $10^{-6}$ error reduction.

Solution

Gradient descent: factor $99/101$, about $690$ steps. Conjugate gradients: factor $9/11$, about $69$ steps. The saving comes from $\sqrt\kappa$ replacing $\kappa$.
