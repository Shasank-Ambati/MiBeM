Vector calculus: fields, fluxes and conservation laws

Diffusion, heat flow, elasticity, electrostatics and fluid flow are all expressed in the language of fields: a temperature, a concentration, a stress or a velocity at every point of space. Vector calculus supplies the three operators that describe how fields vary (gradient, divergence and curl), the integrals that add them up over curves, surfaces and volumes, and the theorems that connect the two. Mastering it is what lets you derive the diffusion equation yourself instead of memorising it, and it is the language of the phase-field and finite element methods in later topics.

Lecture 1. Vector fields#

A vector field $\mathbf F(x,y,z)=(F_x,F_y,F_z)$ assigns a vector to each point. Think of arrows attached to space. Four fields recur throughout this series:

  • the force field $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$ derived from a potential energy;
  • the flux of a conserved quantity, such as the diffusion flux $\mathbf J$ (atoms per unit area per unit time) or the heat flux $\mathbf q$;
  • the velocity field $\mathbf v(\mathbf r)$ in a flowing liquid, or the displacement field $\mathbf u(\mathbf r)$ in a deformed solid;
  • the electric field $\mathbf E$, and the gradient $\nabla\phi$ of any scalar field.

We use the position vector $\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$, its length $r=|\mathbf r|$ and the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r/r$. The field $\mathbf F=\mathbf r$ points radially outward and grows with distance; $\mathbf F=\mathbf r/r^3=\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$ is the inverse-square field of a point source (a point charge, or a point source of atoms). Field lines are curves tangent to the field: they show the direction of flow.

The vector algebra you need is brief. The dot product $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta$ projects one vector on another, and the cross product $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ has magnitude $|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta$ and direction perpendicular to both. Component forms and their properties are developed in MATH 103.

Lecture 2. Line integrals and conservative fields#

The line integral#

The work done by a force $\mathbf F$ moving a particle along a curve $C$ is the sum of $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r$ over small steps. Parametrise the curve by $\mathbf r(t)$, $a\le t\le b$; then $$W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=\int_a^b\mathbf F\bigl(\mathbf r(t)\bigr)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf r}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathrm{d}t .$$ Reversing the direction of travel flips the sign. For a closed curve the integral is the circulation $\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r$.

Conservative fields and potentials#

Gradient theorem and conservative fields

If $\mathbf F=\nabla\phi$ then for any path from $A$ to $B$, $\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=\phi(B)-\phi(A)$. The integral depends only on the endpoints, so every closed-loop integral is zero. Such a field is called conservative. Conversely, on a region without holes (simply connected), $\mathbf F$ is conservative if and only if $\nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0$ (section 5).

This is the mathematical form of energy conservation: if $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$, the work done as an atom moves from $A$ to $B$ is $U(A)-U(B)$ whatever route it takes, and no net work is done around a closed loop. All interatomic forces derived from an energy are conservative. A non-conservative force, such as friction or a driven flow, would allow a particle to gain energy around a loop; in a simulation that is a symptom of a bug or of an applied drive.

Worked example 2.1: a conservative field

Compute $\int_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r$ for $\mathbf F=(y,\,x)$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ along (i) the straight line and (ii) the parabola $y=x^2$.

(i) $\mathbf r=(t,t)$, $\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=(1,1)\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}t$, $\mathbf F=(t,t)$: $\int_0^1(t+t)\,\mathrm{d}t=1$. (ii) $\mathbf r=(t,t^2)$, $\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=(1,2t)\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}t$, $\mathbf F=(t^2,t)$: $\int_0^1(t^2+2t^2)\,\mathrm{d}t=1$. Both give $1$ because $\mathbf F=\nabla(xy)$ and $xy$ goes from $0$ to $1$. Problem 4 shows what happens for a non-conservative field.

Worked example 2.2: work along a helix

Find $\int_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf r$ for $\mathbf F=(-y,\,x,\,1)$ along $\mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,t)$, $0\le t\le2\pi$.

$\mathrm d\mathbf r=(-\sin t,\cos t,1)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dt$ and $\mathbf F=(-\sin t,\cos t,1)$ on the curve, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf r=(\sin^2t+\cos^2t+1)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dt=2\,\allowbreak \mathrm dt$ and the integral is $4\pi$. The field has $\nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,0,2)\neq\mathbf0$, so it is not conservative and the result depends on the path.

Lecture 3. Surface integrals and flux#

The flux of a vector field $\mathbf F$ through a surface $S$ with unit normal $\mathbf n$ is $$\Phi=\iint_S\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm{d}S .$$ Only the component of $\mathbf F$ perpendicular to the surface counts. If $\mathbf F=\mathbf J$ is a particle flux (particles per area per time), then $\Phi$ is the number of particles crossing $S$ per unit time; for heat flux $\mathbf q$ it is the heat flow rate in watts. For a closed surface, $\mathbf n$ is taken to point outward, so $\Phi>0$ means a net flow out.

For a sphere of radius $R$ centred on the origin, $\mathbf n=\hat{\mathbf r}$ and $\mathrm{d}S=R^2\sin\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\phi$, the total area being $4\pi R^2$. For a flat region the area element is just $\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y$. In general, if the surface is parametrised as $\mathbf r(u,v)$ then $\mathbf n\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}S=\dfrac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial u}\times\dfrac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial v}\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}u\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}v$.

Worked example 3.1: flux through a sphere

Find the flux of $\mathbf F=\mathbf r/r^3$ (inverse-square field) through a sphere of radius $R$.

On the sphere $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=\dfrac{\hat{\mathbf r}}{R^2}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}=\dfrac1{R^2}$, which is constant, so $\Phi=\dfrac1{R^2}\cdot4\pi R^2=4\pi$. The flux does not depend on $R$: the same total passes through every concentric sphere, as it must if nothing is created or destroyed between them. This is Gauss’s law, and the reason a point source produces an inverse-square field.

Worked example 3.2: heat flow through a tilted plane

A uniform heat flux $\mathbf q=(0,0,-5\times10^4)$ W/m$^2$ crosses a flat area $A=2$ cm$^2$ whose unit normal makes $60^\circ$ with the $z$-axis. Find the heat flow rate through it.

$Q=\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n\,A$ with $\mathbf n=(\sin60^\circ,0,\cos60^\circ)$: $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n=-5\times10^4\times0.5=-2.5\times10^4$ W/m$^2$, so $Q=-2.5\times10^4\times2\times10^{-4}=-5$ W. Only the component normal to the surface counts; the minus sign says heat crosses in the direction opposite to $\mathbf n$. Tilting the plane to $90^\circ$ would give zero flux.

Lecture 4. Divergence#

Definition (divergence)

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}.$$ It is a scalar: the net outflow of $\mathbf F$ per unit volume at a point.

Why does that formula measure outflow? Take a tiny box of sides $\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z$ centred at a point. The flux out through the two faces perpendicular to $x$ is $\bigl[F_x(x+\tfrac12\Delta x)-F_x(x-\tfrac12\Delta x)\bigr]\Delta y\,\allowbreak \Delta z\approx\dfrac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z$. Adding the $y$ and $z$ faces and dividing by the volume $\Delta V$ gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$. A positive divergence marks a source (more leaves than enters), a negative one a sink, and zero divergence a solenoidal (incompressible, source-free) field such as the flow of an incompressible liquid.

Useful results: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf r=3$; $\nabla\cdot\bigl(\phi\mathbf F\bigr)=\phi\,\allowbreak \nabla\cdot\mathbf F+\mathbf F\cdot\nabla\phi$ (product rule).

Worked example 4.1: the inverse-square field has no divergence away from the source

Show $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf r/r^3)=0$ for $\mathbf r\neq0$.

Use the product rule with $\phi=r^{-3}$: $\nabla\cdot(r^{-3}\mathbf r)=r^{-3}\,\allowbreak \nabla\cdot\mathbf r+\mathbf r\cdot\nabla(r^{-3})$. Here $\nabla\cdot\mathbf r=3$ and $\nabla(r^{-3})=-3r^{-4}\hat{\mathbf r}=-3r^{-5}\mathbf r$. So the result is $3r^{-3}-3r^{-5}\,\allowbreak \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r=3r^{-3}-3r^{-3}=0$. All the flux $4\pi$ found in Example 3.1 therefore emerges from the single point $r=0$, where the field is singular.

Worked example 4.2: divergence of a radial field

Show $\nabla\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}=2/r$ in three dimensions, and find $n$ for which $\nabla\cdot(r^n\mathbf r)=0$.

$\hat{\mathbf r}=r^{-1}\mathbf r$, so $\nabla\cdot(r^{-1}\mathbf r)=r^{-1}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf r)+\mathbf r\cdot\nabla r^{-1}=\dfrac3r-\dfrac1{r^2}\,\allowbreak \mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}=\dfrac2r$. In general $\nabla\cdot(r^n\mathbf r)=3r^n+nr^n=(n+3)\,\allowbreak r^n$, which vanishes (for $r\ne0$) only for $n=-3$: the inverse-square field. The $2/r$ for the unit vector field reflects the way lines of $\hat{\mathbf r}$ spread out over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$.

Lecture 5. Curl#

Definition (curl)

$$\nabla\times\mathbf F=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{\mathbf x}&\hat{\mathbf y}&\hat{\mathbf z}\\\partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\F_x&F_y&F_z\end{vmatrix}=\Bigl(\partial_yF_z-\partial_zF_y,\ \partial_zF_x-\partial_xF_z,\ \partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x\Bigr).$$ It is a vector measuring the local rotation of the field: its direction is the axis about which the field circulates, and its magnitude is the circulation per unit area around that axis.

For a rigid rotation with angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$, the velocity is $\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf v=2\boldsymbol\omega$. Two identities hold for any smooth fields and explain much of the structure of physics:

$$\nabla\times(\nabla\phi)=\mathbf 0,\qquad\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0 .$$

The first says gradient fields have no rotation, so a curl-free field can be written as a gradient (a potential exists): this is the test for a conservative field. The second says a field that is a curl has no sources. Both follow from the commuting of mixed partial derivatives. In elasticity, the curl of the displacement gradient measures dislocation density (Problem 11).

Worked example 5.1: testing for a potential

Is $\mathbf F=(2xy,\ x^2+3y^2)$ conservative? If so, find $\phi$ with $\mathbf F=\nabla\phi$.

In two dimensions the only curl component is $\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x=2x-2x=0$, so yes. Integrate: $\phi_x=2xy\Rightarrow\phi=x^2y+g(y)$. Then $\phi_y=x^2+g'(y)$ must equal $x^2+3y^2$, so $g'=3y^2$ and $g=y^3$. Thus $\phi=x^2y+y^3$ (plus any constant).

Lecture 6. The Laplacian#

The divergence of a gradient defines the Laplacian: $$\nabla^2f=\nabla\cdot\nabla f=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial z^2}.$$ It tells you how the value at a point compares with its surroundings: where $\nabla^2f>0$, $f$ is lower than the average of its neighbours (a local dip), and where $\nabla^2f<0$ it is higher (a bump). Concretely, on a grid of spacing $h$ in two dimensions, Taylor expansion shows $$\frac{f(x+h,y)+f(x-h,y)+f(x,y+h)+f(x,y-h)}{4}-f(x,y)=\frac{h^2}{4}\nabla^2f+O(h^4)$$ (Problem 8). That is why diffusion, which drives each point toward the average of its neighbours, is governed by $\nabla^2$, and why the same five-point stencil appears in every grid solver. Functions satisfying $\nabla^2f=0$ (harmonic functions) have no local maxima or minima: they equal the average of their surroundings everywhere. Steady-state diffusion and heat flow with no sources are harmonic problems.

Worked example 6.1: the Laplacian in spherical form

Check $\nabla^2(r^2)=6$ and $\nabla^2(1/r)=0$ with $\nabla^2f=r^{-2}(r^2f')'$.

For $f=r^2$: $r^{-2}(r^2\cdot2r)'=r^{-2}\cdot6r^2=6$, matching $\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\partial_z^2$ of $x^2+y^2+z^2$. For $f=1/r$: $r^2f'=-1$ is constant, so its derivative is $0$. The condition for a harmonic radial function is $r^2f'=\text{constant}$, i.e. $f=A+B/r$, the steady-state profile around a sphere.

Lecture 7. The integral theorems#

Divergence theorem (Gauss)

For a region $V$ with closed boundary surface $\partial V$ and outward unit normal $\mathbf n$, $$\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,\mathrm{d}V=\oiint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm{d}S .$$ The total production of the field inside equals the net flux out through the boundary.

Stokes’ theorem

For a surface $S$ with boundary curve $C$ (oriented by the right-hand rule relative to $\mathbf n$), $$\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm{d}S=\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r .$$ The total rotation over a surface equals the circulation around its rim. In the plane this is Green’s theorem, $\oint(F_x\,\mathrm{d}x+F_y\,\mathrm{d}y)=\iint(\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x)\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}A$.

The divergence theorem is the statement that fluxes through internal faces between neighbouring small boxes cancel in pairs, so summing the boxes' net outflows leaves only the outer boundary. Stokes’ theorem works the same way with circulation around small loops. Together with the gradient theorem of section 2, all three say: integrating a derivative over a region equals the quantity itself evaluated on the boundary, a higher-dimensional version of the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Worked example 7.1: area from a boundary integral (Green’s theorem)

Use Green’s theorem to find the area of the ellipse $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$.

Take $\mathbf F=\tfrac12(-y,x)$, whose curl is $1$. Then $\text{area}=\iint1\,\allowbreak \mathrm dA=\oint\tfrac12(x\,\mathrm dy-y\,\mathrm dx)=\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}(ab\cos^2t+ab\sin^2t)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dt=\pi ab$. The same trick, summed over polygon edges, is how image-analysis software computes the area of a grain from its traced boundary.

Lecture 8. From conservation laws to equations#

Here is the most important use of these theorems in this series. Let $c(\mathbf r,t)$ be the concentration of a conserved species (atoms per unit volume) and $\mathbf J$ its flux. For any fixed volume $V$ with no sources inside, the amount in $V$ changes only because of what flows through the boundary: $$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\iiint_Vc\,\mathrm{d}V=-\oiint_{\partial V}\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm{d}S=-\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,\mathrm{d}V,$$ using the divergence theorem. Move everything to one side: $\iiint_V\bigl(\partial_tc+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J\bigr)\mathrm{d}V=0$. This holds for every volume, however small, so the integrand itself must vanish:

$$\boxed{\ \frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0\ }\qquad\text{(continuity equation)}.$$

If a reaction creates the species at a rate $R$ per unit volume, the right-hand side becomes $+R$. The equation holds for any conserved quantity: mass, charge, energy (with $\mathbf J=\mathbf q$ the heat flux), probability. It says nothing yet about how the material moves; that requires a constitutive law. Fick’s first law states that atoms flow down the concentration gradient, $\mathbf J=-D\nabla c$ (with $D$ the diffusion coefficient), and substituting gives Fick’s second law: $$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot(D\nabla c)\ \xrightarrow{\ D\ \text{const}\ }\ \frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=D\,\nabla^2c .$$ For heat, $\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla T$ and energy density $\rho c_p T$ give $\rho c_p\,\allowbreak \partial_tT=\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla T)$. In the phase-field method (Phase-field modelling) the flux is driven instead by the gradient of the chemical potential, $\mathbf J=-M\nabla\mu$, but the continuity equation is exactly the same. Notice the logic: the structure ($\partial_tc+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$) is exact, from conservation alone; only the closure ($\mathbf J=-D\nabla c$) is a model.

Worked example 8.2: Joule heating of a wire

A wire (slab approximation) $-a<x<a$ generates heat $q=10^8$ W/m$^3$ uniformly and its surfaces are held at $T_0$. Find the temperature profile and the peak rise for $\kappa=400$ W m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, $a=1$ mm.

Steady state of the heat equation with a source, $\kappa T''=-q$. Integrating twice with $T(\pm a)=T_0$ (even in $x$): $T=T_0+\dfrac{q}{2\kappa}(a^2-x^2)$. The peak rise at the centre is $\dfrac{qa^2}{2\kappa}=\dfrac{10^8\times10^{-6}}{800}=0.125$ K. The Laplacian turns a uniform source into a parabolic profile; for a cylinder the same argument gives $T=T_0+\tfrac{q}{4\kappa}(a^2-r^2)$.

Summary of the unit#

The gradient turns potentials into forces and concentrations into fluxes. The divergence measures sources and sinks, and combined with the divergence theorem converts “conservation in every volume” into the local continuity equation. The curl detects rotation and obstructs the existence of a potential. The Laplacian measures local deviation from the average and drives smoothing processes. These ideas set up the PDEs of MATH 301. First, though, we need the linear algebra that underlies tensors, vibrations and numerical methods.

Key takeaways
  • A vector field assigns a vector to every point; the gradient turns a scalar potential into a conservative vector field, and a field is conservative exactly when its curl vanishes (on a simply connected region).
  • Divergence measures net outflow per unit volume (sources and sinks); curl measures local rotation; the Laplacian $\nabla^2 f=\nabla\cdot\nabla f$ measures how much $f$ differs from its neighbours' average.
  • The divergence theorem $\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,\mathrm{d}V=\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}S$ converts a statement about a volume into one about its boundary, and vice versa; Stokes' theorem does the same for circulation.
  • Conservation of a quantity plus the divergence theorem gives the continuity equation $\partial_t c+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}=0$; with Fick's law $\mathbf{J}=-D\nabla c$ this is the diffusion equation $\partial_t c=\nabla\cdot(D\nabla c)$.
  • In curvilinear coordinates, operators gain geometric factors, e.g. $\nabla^2f=\frac1{r^2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_rf)$ for a spherically symmetric function, which gives the $1/r$ steady-state solutions.

Problem 3.1Basic

For $\mathbf F=(x^2y,\ yz,\ xz^2)$ compute $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf F$. For $\phi=x^2+yz$ compute $\nabla\phi$ and $\nabla^2\phi$.

Hint

Apply the definitions component by component; for the curl, be careful with the order of the subtractions.

Full solution

$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\partial_x(x^2y)+\partial_y(yz)+\partial_z(xz^2)=2xy+z+2xz$.

Curl components: $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_x=\partial_yF_z-\partial_zF_y=\partial_y(xz^2)-\partial_z(yz)=0-y=-y$; $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_y=\partial_zF_x-\partial_xF_z=\partial_z(x^2y)-\partial_x(xz^2)=0-z^2=-z^2$; $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x=\partial_x(yz)-\partial_y(x^2y)=0-x^2=-x^2$. So $\nabla\times\mathbf F=(-y,-z^2,-x^2)$.

$\nabla\phi=(2x,\ z,\ y)$ and $\nabla^2\phi=2+0+0=2$.

Problem 3.2Basic

With $r=|\mathbf r|$, prove: (a) $\nabla r=\hat{\mathbf r}$; (b) $\nabla r^n=nr^{n-2}\mathbf r$; (c) $\nabla^2(r^2)=6$; (d) $\nabla^2(1/r)=0$ for $r\ne0$ (use the spherical formula, or the product rule with $\nabla\cdot\nabla$).

Hint

Start from $\partial r/\partial x=x/r$. For (d), $\nabla(1/r)=-\mathbf r/r^3$ and Example 4.1 gives its divergence.

Full solution

(a) $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$, so $\partial r/\partial x=x/r$ and similarly for $y,z$: $\nabla r=\mathbf r/r=\hat{\mathbf r}$.

(b) Chain rule: $\nabla r^n=nr^{n-1}\nabla r=nr^{n-1}\mathbf r/r=nr^{n-2}\mathbf r$.

(c) $r^2=x^2+y^2+z^2$, so $\nabla^2r^2=2+2+2=6$. (Check with (b): $\nabla r^2=2\mathbf r$ and $\nabla\cdot(2\mathbf r)=6$.) ✓

(d) By (b) with $n=-1$, $\nabla(1/r)=-r^{-3}\mathbf r$. Then $\nabla^2(1/r)=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf r/r^3)=0$ by Example 4.1. (With the spherical formula: $\dfrac1{r^2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\bigl(r^2\cdot(-r^{-2})\bigr)=\dfrac{1}{r^2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}(-1)=0$.) The potential $1/r$ of a point source is harmonic everywhere except at the source.

Problem 3.3Core

Show that $\mathbf F=(2xy+z^3,\ x^2,\ 3xz^2)$ is conservative, find a potential $\phi$ with $\mathbf F=\nabla\phi$, and compute the work done moving a particle from $(0,0,0)$ to $(1,2,3)$ along any path.

Hint

Check that all three curl components vanish. Then integrate $F_x$ with respect to $x$, and fix the unknown functions of $y,z$ using the other components.

Full solution

Curl: $\partial_yF_z-\partial_zF_y=0-0=0$; $\partial_zF_x-\partial_xF_z=3z^2-3z^2=0$; $\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x=2x-2x=0$. So $\nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf0$ and, since $\mathbb R^3$ has no holes, $\mathbf F$ is conservative.

Integrate $\phi_x=2xy+z^3$: $\phi=x^2y+xz^3+g(y,z)$. Then $\phi_y=x^2+g_y$ must equal $x^2$, so $g_y=0$ and $g=g(z)$. Then $\phi_z=3xz^2+g'(z)$ must equal $3xz^2$, so $g'=0$. Therefore $\phi=x^2y+xz^3$.

Work $=\phi(1,2,3)-\phi(0,0,0)=(1)(2)+(1)(27)=29$ (in the units of $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r$), independent of path.

$\phi=x^2y+xz^3$; $W=29$

Problem 3.4Core

The field $\mathbf F=(-y,\ x)$ is a rigid rotation. (a) Compute $\int\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ along the straight line, and along the path $(0,0)\to(1,0)\to(1,1)$. Is $\mathbf F$ conservative? (b) Compute the circulation around the circle $x^2+y^2=R^2$ directly, and check it with Green’s theorem.

Hint

For (b) parametrise $\mathbf r=(R\cos t,R\sin t)$.

Full solution

(a) Straight line $\mathbf r=(t,t)$: $\mathbf F=(-t,t)$, $\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=(1,1)\mathrm{d}t$, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=0$ and the integral is $0$. Along the $x$-axis leg ($y=0$, $\mathrm{d}y=0$): $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=-y\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x=0$. Along $x=1$ ($\mathrm{d}x=0$): $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=x\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}y=\mathrm{d}y$, giving $\int_0^1\mathrm{d}y=1$. The two paths give $0$ and $1$, so $\mathbf F$ is not conservative (indeed $\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x=1-(-1)=2\ne0$).

(b) With $\mathbf r=(R\cos t,R\sin t)$: $\mathbf F=(-R\sin t,R\cos t)$ and $\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=(-R\sin t,R\cos t)\mathrm{d}t$, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=R^2\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}t$ and $\oint=2\pi R^2$. Green: $\iint(\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x)\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}A=2\cdot\pi R^2$. ✓ A field driving a particle round a loop does net work each time: this is how a non-conservative drive, such as an applied shear flow, pumps energy into a system.

Problem 3.5Core

Verify the divergence theorem for $\mathbf F=\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$ and the ball of radius $R$: compute the surface flux and the volume integral of the divergence, and compare. Then use the theorem to show that the volume of a region is $V=\tfrac13\oint\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}S$.

Hint

On the sphere, $\mathbf n=\hat{\mathbf r}$, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=r=R$. The volume of a ball is $\tfrac43\pi R^3$.

Full solution

Surface: $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}=R$ on the sphere, so $\Phi=R\cdot4\pi R^2=4\pi R^3$. Volume: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf r=3$, so $\iiint3\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}V=3\cdot\tfrac43\pi R^3=4\pi R^3$. Equal. ✓

For any region, $\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}V=3V=\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}S$, so $V=\tfrac13\oint\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}S$. This formula is used in practice to compute the volume enclosed by a triangulated surface (for example the volume of a pore or a precipitate from a surface mesh): each triangle contributes one-third of its area times the distance of its plane from the origin.

Problem 3.6Basic

Verify the divergence theorem for $\mathbf F=(x^2,y^2,z^2)$ and the unit cube $0\le x,y,z\le1$.

Hint

Six faces; only the faces at $x=1$, $y=1$, $z=1$ contribute, since $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=0$ or $-x^2=0$ on the others.

Full solution

Volume side: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=2x+2y+2z$, and $\iiint(2x+2y+2z)\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}V=2\bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac12\bigr)=3$ (each term integrates to $\tfrac12$ over the unit cube, times the unit extent of the other two directions).

Surface side: on the face $x=1$, $\mathbf n=\hat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=x^2=1$, giving flux $1\cdot1=1$. On the face $x=0$, $\mathbf n=-\hat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=-x^2=0$. The same for $y$ and $z$. The total is $1+1+1=3$. ✓

Problem 3.7Core

A thin steel plate of thickness $L=1.0$ mm separates a carburising gas from a decarburising gas. The carbon concentration is held at $c_1=5.0$ kg/m$^3$ on one face and $c_2=0$ on the other, and the carbon diffusion coefficient is $D=8\times10^{-12}$ m$^2$/s (constant). (a) Starting from the continuity equation and Fick’s law, show that at steady state $c$ is linear in $x$. (b) Find the steady flux $J$ and the mass of carbon passing through $1$ m$^2$ of plate in one hour.

Hint

Steady state means $\partial_tc=0$, so $\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}x=0$ in one dimension: the flux is uniform.

Full solution

(a) In one dimension, $\partial_tc+\partial_xJ=0$ with $J=-D\,\partial_xc$. Steady state: $\partial_xJ=0$, so $J$ is constant, and with constant $D$, $\partial_xc=-J/D$ is constant: $c(x)$ is linear, $c(x)=c_1+(c_2-c_1)\,x/L$.

(b) $J=-D\,\allowbreak \dfrac{c_2-c_1}{L}=D\,\allowbreak \dfrac{c_1-c_2}{L}=\dfrac{(8\times10^{-12})(5.0)}{1.0\times10^{-3}}=4.0\times10^{-8}$ kg m$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$, flowing from high to low concentration. In one hour: $4.0\times10^{-8}\times3600=1.44\times10^{-4}$ kg $=0.144$ g per m$^2$. A thicker plate transmits less in inverse proportion to $L$: permeation barriers work by increasing $L$ or decreasing $D$.

$J=4.0\times10^{-8}$ kg m⁻² s⁻¹; 0.144 g/m² per hour

Problem 3.8Core

(a) Using Taylor series, show that on a square grid of spacing $h$, the average of the four nearest neighbours of a point exceeds the value at the point by $\tfrac14h^2\nabla^2f+O(h^4)$. (b) Deduce that a solution of $\nabla^2f=0$ equals the average of its neighbours, and explain why such a function cannot have an interior maximum. (c) What discrete update rule for $f$ follows from the diffusion equation $\partial_tf=D\nabla^2f$ using a forward Euler step $\Delta t$?

Hint

Add the expansions of $f(x\pm h,y)$ and $f(x,y\pm h)$; the first-derivative terms cancel in pairs.

Full solution

(a) $f(x\pm h,y)=f\pm hf_x+\tfrac12h^2f_{xx}\pm\tfrac16h^3f_{xxx}+\tfrac1{24}h^4f_{xxxx}$, so $f(x+h,y)+f(x-h,y)=2f+h^2f_{xx}+O(h^4)$. Likewise for $y$. Summing the four neighbours: $4f+h^2(f_{xx}+f_{yy})+O(h^4)$. Dividing by $4$ and subtracting $f$: $\tfrac14h^2\nabla^2f+O(h^4)$.

(b) If $\nabla^2f=0$, the point value equals the average of its neighbours (to this order, exactly for harmonic functions by the mean value property). A point cannot be strictly larger than all its neighbours while equal to their average; so there is no interior maximum (or minimum), and extremes occur on the boundary. This is the maximum principle: steady-state temperature in a source-free body is hottest and coldest at its surface.

(c) $\partial_tf\approx(f^{n+1}-f^n)/\Delta t$ and $\nabla^2f\approx\dfrac{4}{h^2}(\bar f_\mathrm{nbrs}-f)$, so $f^{n+1}=f^n+\dfrac{4D\,\Delta t}{h^2}\bigl(\bar f_\mathrm{nbrs}-f^n\bigr)$: each point moves toward its neighbours’ average by a fraction $4D\Delta t/h^2$. For this to remain stable the fraction must not exceed $1$, i.e. $\Delta t\le h^2/(4D)$, the 2D stability limit of the explicit scheme (see MATH 301).

Problem 3.9Core

Show that $c(x,t)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\,\allowbreak \exp\!\Bigl(-\dfrac{x^2}{4Dt}\Bigr)$ satisfies $\partial_tc=D\,\partial_x^2c$, that $\int_{-\infty}^\infty c\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x=1$ for all $t>0$, and that the variance is $\langle x^2\rangle=2Dt$. For $D=1.0\times10^{-12}$ m$^2$/s and $t=1$ hour, find the diffusion distance $\sqrt{2Dt}$.

Hint

Let $s=4Dt$. Compute $c_t$ and $c_{xx}$ with the product and chain rules. For the variance use the Gaussian moments from MATH 101 (Problem 1.11).

Full solution

Write $c=(\pi s)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/s}$ with $s=4Dt$, so $\mathrm{d}s/\mathrm{d}t=4D$.

$\partial_tc=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\allowbreak \partial_sc=4D\Bigl[-\dfrac{1}{2s}+\dfrac{x^2}{s^2}\Bigr]c$. And $\partial_xc=-\dfrac{2x}{s}c$, $\partial_x^2c=\Bigl[-\dfrac2s+\dfrac{4x^2}{s^2}\Bigr]c$, so $D\,\allowbreak \partial_x^2c=4D\Bigl[-\dfrac1{2s}+\dfrac{x^2}{s^2}\Bigr]c$. These agree. ✓

Normalisation: $\int e^{-x^2/s}\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\pi s}$, so $\int c\,\mathrm{d}x=1$ at every $t$ (conservation of the diffused amount). Variance: $\langle x^2\rangle=\dfrac12s=2Dt$ (a Gaussian $e^{-x^2/s}=e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ has $\sigma^2=s/2$).

Numbers: $2Dt=2(10^{-12})(3600)=7.2\times10^{-9}$ m$^2$, so $\sqrt{2Dt}=8.5\times10^{-5}$ m $=85\ \mu$m. This is the rule of thumb “distance $\sim\sqrt{Dt}$”: diffusion distance grows only as the square root of time, so doubling the distance needs four times the time.

$\sigma^2=2Dt$; $\sqrt{2Dt}\approx85\ \mu$m

Problem 3.10Core

For $\mathbf F=(-y,\ x,\ z)$ verify Stokes’ theorem on the flat disc $x^2+y^2\le R^2$, $z=0$, with $\mathbf n=\hat{\mathbf z}$.

Hint

Compute $\nabla\times\mathbf F$ first. The boundary circle is $(R\cos t,R\sin t,0)$ traversed anticlockwise.

Full solution

$\nabla\times\mathbf F=(\partial_yF_z-\partial_zF_y,\ \partial_zF_x-\partial_xF_z,\ \partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x)=(0,0,1-(-1))=(0,0,2)$. Surface integral: $\iint(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\hat{\mathbf z}\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}A=2\pi R^2$.

Line integral: on the circle $\mathbf F=(-R\sin t,R\cos t,0)$ and $\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=(-R\sin t,R\cos t,0)\mathrm{d}t$, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf r=R^2\mathrm{d}t$ and $\oint=2\pi R^2$. ✓ (The $z$-component of $\mathbf F$ does not contribute on the flat disc.)

Problem 3.11Challenge

A screw dislocation along the $z$-axis in an isotropic crystal produces the displacement $u_z=\dfrac{b}{2\pi}\,\theta$ (with $\theta$ the polar angle about the axis, $u_x=u_y=0$), where $b$ is the Burgers vector. (a) Show that $\nabla u_z=\dfrac{b}{2\pi r}\,\allowbreak \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=\dfrac{b}{2\pi}\Bigl(\dfrac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\ \dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\Bigr)$. (b) Show this field has zero curl for $r\ne0$. (c) Compute $\oint\nabla u_z\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf l$ around a circle of radius $\rho$ about the axis. Why does this not contradict “$\nabla\times\nabla f=0$ implies a zero closed integral”? (d) For $b=0.25$ nm, find the strain $\gamma_{\theta z}=\tfrac{b}{2\pi\rho}$ at $\rho=2$ nm.

Hint

$\theta=\arctan(y/x)$, so $\partial_x\theta=-y/(x^2+y^2)$ and $\partial_y\theta=x/(x^2+y^2)$. For (c) use $\mathbf r=\rho(\cos t,\sin t)$.

Full solution

(a) $\partial_x\theta=\dfrac{-y}{x^2+y^2}$, $\partial_y\theta=\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}$, so $\nabla u_z=\dfrac{b}{2\pi}\Bigl(\dfrac{-y}{r^2},\dfrac{x}{r^2}\Bigr)$. Since $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=(-y,x)/r$, this equals $\dfrac{b}{2\pi r}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$.

(b) With $F_x=-y/r^2$ and $F_y=x/r^2$ ($r^2=x^2+y^2$): $\partial_xF_y=\dfrac{r^2-2x^2}{r^4}=\dfrac{y^2-x^2}{r^4}$ and $\partial_yF_x=-\dfrac{r^2-2y^2}{r^4}=\dfrac{y^2-x^2}{r^4}$. So $\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x=0$ for $r\neq0$.

(c) On the circle, $\nabla u_z\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf l=\dfrac{b}{2\pi\rho}\,\allowbreak \rho\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}t=\dfrac{b}{2\pi}\mathrm{d}t$, so $\oint=b$ for every $\rho$. No contradiction: the identity $\oint\nabla f\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf l=0$ requires $f$ to be single-valued, and Stokes needs the curl to be defined throughout the surface. Here $\theta$ is multivalued (it increases by $2\pi$ going around the axis) and the field is singular on the axis, which is not part of a simply connected region. The curl vanishes everywhere except on the line $r=0$, where it is concentrated: $\nabla\times\nabla u_z=b\,\allowbreak \delta^{(2)}(x,y)\hat{\mathbf z}$. This is precisely the definition of a dislocation: a line carrying a Burgers vector $b=\oint\nabla u\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf l$ (the closure failure of a circuit round it).

(d) $\gamma_{\theta z}=\dfrac{b}{2\pi\rho}=\dfrac{0.25}{2\pi\cdot2}=0.0199\approx2.0\%$. The strain falls as $1/\rho$: the long-range elastic field of a dislocation is why dislocations interact strongly over large distances, and why the core (where this blows up) needs atomistic treatment.

$\oint\nabla u_z\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf l=b$; strain $\approx2.0\%$ at 2 nm

Problem 3.12Challenge

Steady heat conduction through a spherical shell $a\le r\le b$ with constant conductivity $\kappa$, with $T(a)=T_1$ and $T(b)=T_2$. (a) Show that steady state requires $\nabla^2T=0$ and solve for $T(r)$ assuming spherical symmetry. (b) Show that the total heat flow $Q=4\pi r^2q_r$ does not depend on $r$ and find it. (c) Evaluate for a spherical particle with a $1$ cm inner radius, a $2$ cm outer radius, $\kappa=1.0$ W m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ and $T_1-T_2=100$ K. (d) Repeat the derivation for a long cylindrical shell and show that the heat flow per unit length is $2\pi\kappa(T_1-T_2)/\ln(b/a)$.

Hint

Steady state with no source: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0$ with $\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla T$. In the spherical Laplacian, $r^2T'$ must be constant.

Full solution

(a) Steady state: $\partial_tT=0$ so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla^2T=0$. With spherical symmetry, $\dfrac1{r^2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\bigl(r^2T'\bigr)=0$, so $r^2T'=-B$ is constant, $T'=-B/r^2$ and $T=A+B/r$. The boundary conditions give $A+B/a=T_1$, $A+B/b=T_2$, so $B\bigl(\tfrac1a-\tfrac1b\bigr)=T_1-T_2$, i.e. $B=\dfrac{ab\,(T_1-T_2)}{b-a}$.

(b) $q_r=-\kappa T'=\kappa B/r^2$, so $Q=4\pi r^2q_r=4\pi\kappa B=\dfrac{4\pi\kappa\,ab\,(T_1-T_2)}{b-a}$, independent of $r$ as required by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0$ (the same heat crosses every sphere).

(c) $Q=4\pi(1.0)\dfrac{(0.01)(0.02)}{0.01}(100)=4\pi(0.02)(100)=25.1$ W.

(d) In cylindrical symmetry, $\dfrac1r\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}(rT')=0$, so $rT'=-B$ and $T=A+B'\ln r$, with $B'=\dfrac{T_2-T_1}{\ln(b/a)}$. The heat per unit length is $2\pi rq_r=-2\pi\kappa\,\allowbreak rT'=-2\pi\kappa B'=\dfrac{2\pi\kappa(T_1-T_2)}{\ln(b/a)}$. The same argument gives the steady permeation of hydrogen through a pipe wall (with $D$, $c$ in place of $\kappa$, $T$).

$T=A+B/r$; $Q=4\pi\kappa ab(T_1-T_2)/(b-a)=25.1$ W

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Operators

For $\mathbf F=(yz,\,xz,\,xy)$ compute $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf F$, and find a potential if one exists.

Solution

$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0+0+0=0$. Curl: $(x-x,\ y-y,\ z-z)=\mathbf0$. So $\mathbf F$ is conservative with $\phi=xyz$ ($\nabla\phi=(yz,xz,xy)$).

Quiz 2Identities

For which $n$ is $r^n\mathbf r$ divergence-free for $r\neq0$, and what physical field is it?

Solution

$\nabla\cdot(r^n\mathbf r)=(n+3)r^n$, zero for $n=-3$: $\mathbf r/r^3$, the inverse-square field of a point source (Coulomb or a point source of atoms).

Quiz 3Line integral

Evaluate $\int\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf r$ for $\mathbf F=(2xy,\,x^2)$ along $y=x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.

Solution

With $x=t$, $y=t^2$: $\mathbf F=(2t^3,t^2)$, $\mathrm d\mathbf r=(1,2t)\mathrm dt$, so $\int_0^1(2t^3+2t^3)\mathrm dt=1$. Check: $\mathbf F=\nabla(x^2y)$ and $x^2y$ goes from $0$ to $1$.

Quiz 4Divergence theorem

Find the outward flux of $\mathbf F=(x,y,z)$ through the surface of the cube $-1\le x,y,z\le1$.

Solution

$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3$ and the volume is $8$: flux $=24$. (Directly: each face has area $4$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=1$, six faces: $24$.)

Quiz 5Laplace

Show that $f=e^x\sin y$ is harmonic, and explain what that implies about its extrema.

Solution

$f_{xx}=e^x\sin y$ and $f_{yy}=-e^x\sin y$, so $\nabla^2f=0$. By the maximum principle $f$ has no interior maximum or minimum in any region: extreme values lie on the boundary.

Quiz 6Diffusion

A species diffuses from a surface through a film of thickness $L=10\ \mu$m with $D=10^{-14}$ m$^2$/s. Estimate the time to equilibrate and the steady flux for surface concentration $c_0$ and zero on the far side.

Solution

Time scale $\sim L^2/D=10^{-10}/10^{-14}=10^4$ s (about three hours; the slowest-mode time constant is $L^2/\pi^2D=1.0\times10^3$ s). In steady state $c$ is linear and $J=Dc_0/L=10^{-14}c_0/10^{-5}=10^{-9}c_0$ (m/s times $c_0$), flowing towards the low-concentration side.
