Numerical methods essentials

Every simulation is a numerical approximation to a mathematical model. This page covers the ideas that decide whether the numbers a program prints can be trusted: how computers do arithmetic, how errors arise and grow, how to choose steps and grids, and what scales badly.

1. Finite-precision arithmetic#

Computers store real numbers as floating-point values: a sign, an exponent and a fixed number of significant bits. Most scientific code uses IEEE 754 double precision: 64 bits, of which 52 are the stored fraction (53 significant bits with the implicit leading one), giving about 16 decimal digits and a range of roughly 10−308 to 10308. The spacing of numbers near 1 is the machine epsilon, εmach = 2−52 ≈ 2.2 × 10−16; single precision has about 7 digits (ε ≈ 1.2 × 10−7), which is much faster on GPUs but needs care.

Three consequences appear constantly:

  • Most decimals are not exact. 0.1 has no finite binary expansion, so 0.1 + 0.2 evaluates to 0.30000000000000004 and equality tests between floating-point numbers should use a tolerance (for example numpy.isclose).
  • Catastrophic cancellation. Subtracting nearly equal numbers loses leading digits: (1 + 10−16) − 1 evaluates to 0. Formulas that subtract close quantities (small energy differences, variances computed as ⟨x²⟩ − ⟨x⟩²) should be rearranged.
  • Order of summation matters. Adding a tiny number to a large one drops it; summing millions of terms accumulates error, so parallel and serial runs can differ in the last digits. Algorithms such as pairwise or compensated (Kahan) summation reduce the error.

A result should therefore never be trusted beyond about 16 digits, and usually far fewer after many operations. Overflow and underflow (exponents leaving the representable range) are rare in physical units but common in exponentials such as exp(−E/kBT) at large arguments, where working with logarithms avoids them.

2. Errors, order and stability#

Four kinds of error separate the model from the number on the screen:

ErrorSourceHow to reduce or test it
Modelling errorThe equations or potential do not describe reality exactlyValidation against experiment
Discretisation (truncation) errorReplacing derivatives, integrals and continuous fields by finite steps and gridsRefine the step or mesh and watch the result converge
Round-off errorFinite-precision arithmeticReformulate; use higher precision; check sensitivity
Iteration errorStopping an iterative solver before it has convergedTighten tolerances; monitor residuals

A method has order of accuracy p if its discretisation error scales as hp with the step h. Halving h then cuts the error by 2p, and the order can be measured from two runs: p ≈ log(eh/eh/2)/log 2. If the measured order is lower than the theory says, there is usually a bug or a non-smooth feature. Richardson extrapolation combines runs at two step sizes to estimate the converged value and the remaining error.

Accuracy is not enough: two further properties matter. Stability means errors do not grow without bound as the calculation proceeds (an explicit scheme with too large a time step does not converge to anything). Conditioning is a property of the problem itself: a problem is ill-conditioned if small changes in the input cause large changes in the answer. For a linear system Ax = b, the condition number κ(A) = ‖A‖‖A−1‖ bounds the amplification of relative errors, and one loses roughly log10κ digits. A well-posed problem needs a solution that exists, is unique and depends continuously on the data.

3. Differentiation and integration#

Finite differences#

Taylor series (see mathematics for simulation) give difference formulas for derivatives with known error:

forward: f′(x) ≈ [f(x+h) − f(x)]/h, error ≈ ½ h f″ (first order)
central: f′(x) ≈ [f(x+h) − f(x−h)]/2h, error ≈ ⅙ h² f′′′ (second order)

Reducing h shrinks this truncation error, but the numerator involves subtracting nearly equal numbers, so round-off error grows as εmach/h. The total error therefore has a minimum at an intermediate step: about √εmach ≈ 10−8 for forward differences and εmach1/3 ≈ 6 × 10−6 for central differences in double precision. Integration#

Numerical quadrature replaces an integral by a weighted sum. The trapezoid rule is second order, Simpson’s rule fourth order, and Gauss–Legendre quadrature with n points integrates polynomials up to degree 2n − 1 exactly; the latter is what finite-element codes use inside each element. For high-dimensional integrals (such as ensemble averages over 3N coordinates) grids fail, but Monte Carlo integration converges as σ/√N regardless of dimension (see Monte Carlo methods).

4. Roots and optimisation#

Solving f(x) = 0#

  • Bisection halves a bracketing interval each step; slow (linear convergence) but guaranteed if the function changes sign.
  • Newton’s method, xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f′(xn), roughly squares the error at each step near a simple root (quadratic convergence: 3 digits, then 6, then 12), but needs a good starting guess and fails where f′ is near zero. In many dimensions the derivative becomes the Jacobian matrix and each step solves a linear system; this is the Newton–Raphson iteration of nonlinear finite elements.
  • Secant and Brent methods avoid derivatives; Brent combines bisection’s safety with fast convergence and is the usual library choice.

Minimising a function#

Finding structures (relaxation to a minimum of the energy), fitting parameters and training models are all optimisation. Gradient descent steps along −∇f; for an ill-conditioned landscape (a long narrow valley) it zigzags and converges slowly at a rate set by the condition number. Conjugate gradients and quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, and its limited-memory form L-BFGS) use information from previous steps and converge much faster; for atomic relaxation, the damped-dynamics method FIRE is popular because it is robust and cheap. All of them find a local minimum. Global searches (basin hopping, simulated annealing, evolutionary algorithms, random structure search) run many local optimisations from different starting points.

5. Linear algebra in practice#

Most numerical methods eventually reduce to a linear system or an eigenvalue problem, so the cost of linear algebra decides what is feasible.

Solve, sparseSparse direct solversDepends on structure; much less than n3Finite-element and finite-difference matrices are sparse

The n3 growth of dense algorithms is why conventional DFT is limited to hundreds or a few thousand atoms: doubling the system multiplies the cost of the dense steps by eight.

6. Integrating ODEs#

To advance dy/dt = f(y, t) in time:

  • Explicit Euler, yn+1 = yn + h f(yn), is first order and unstable for oscillations (it adds energy to a harmonic oscillator at every step), so it is a teaching tool, not a production one.
  • Runge–Kutta methods, in particular the classic fourth-order RK4 and adaptive embedded pairs (such as Dormand–Prince, used by scipy.integrate.solve_ivp), give high accuracy for smooth problems.
  • Verlet and leapfrog integrators are second order and symplectic: they preserve the structure of Hamiltonian dynamics, so the energy error stays bounded over very long runs. They are the standard for molecular dynamics.
  • Implicit methods (backward Euler, BDF) solve an equation at every step but are stable for much larger steps, which is essential for stiff problems with widely separated time scales, such as carbon diffusing millions of times faster than manganese.

For the test equation dy/dt = −λy, explicit Euler is stable only if hλ ≤ 2, so the largest stable step is set by the fastest process in the system, however little that process matters to the answer.

7. Discretising PDEs#

A PDE is turned into algebra by sampling space on a grid or mesh (see diffusion and finite elements). The main families:

  • Finite differences replace derivatives by differences on a regular grid: simple and fast, best for simple geometry.
  • Finite volumes balance fluxes across the faces of cells, so conserved quantities are conserved exactly; the standard for fluid flow and transport.
  • Finite elements use a weak form and piecewise polynomial functions on unstructured meshes; the standard for solid mechanics and complex geometry.
  • Spectral methods expand in Fourier or polynomial modes; extremely accurate for smooth periodic problems (see Fourier analysis).

Time stepping adds stability limits. For explicit diffusion the dimensionless number r = DΔt/Δx² must not exceed 1/2 in one dimension (1/4 in two, 1/6 in three); for wave and advection problems the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition requires that the numerical domain of dependence contains the physical one, cΔt/Δx ≤ 1 with c the wave speed. Implicit schemes remove these limits at the price of solving a linear system each step, and the Crank–Nicolson scheme is second order in time. Always run a mesh-convergence and a time-step convergence study; and test against a problem with a known analytic solution (the method of manufactured solutions constructs one for any equation).

8. Random numbers#

Computers produce pseudo-random numbers with deterministic algorithms: the Mersenne Twister (period 219937 − 1) and the PCG family (NumPy’s default) are good for scientific use. Fixing the seed makes a run reproducible, which is essential for debugging and for reporting results; different seeds provide the independent replicas used for error bars. For parallel runs, give each process its own independent stream (for example with NumPy’s SeedSequence.spawn), never the same seed.

Other distributions come from uniform numbers. The inverse-transform method maps a uniform value u through the inverse of the cumulative distribution (an exponential waiting time is −ln(u)/R, the step of kinetic Monte Carlo); the Box–Muller transform produces Gaussian pairs from two uniform values; rejection sampling draws from a simple envelope and keeps points under the target curve.

9. Cost, parallelism and scaling#

Before a large calculation, estimate its cost from how it scales with size: a pair-force loop without neighbour lists scales as N2, with cell lists as N; the FFT as N log N; dense diagonalisation as N3. A code may be limited by arithmetic (compute-bound) or by moving data from memory (memory-bound, typical of sparse and grid-based codes), and optimising the wrong one wastes effort. Vectorised library calls (NumPy, BLAS and LAPACK) are far faster than explicit loops in interpreted languages.

Parallel machines combine threads within a node (OpenMP), message passing between nodes (MPI) and GPUs. Two scaling tests describe how well a code uses them: strong scaling (fixed problem, more processors) and weak scaling (problem grows with the processor count). Amdahl’s law limits strong scaling: if a fraction p of the work parallelises perfectly,

S(N) = 1 / [ (1 − p) + p/N ]    →   1/(1 − p) as N → ∞

so a code that is 95% parallel can never run more than 20 times faster, however many processors are used. Measure with a profiler before optimising, test on a small case first, and note that communication and input/output often dominate at large processor counts.

Key takeaways
  • Computers use finite-precision arithmetic: double precision keeps about 16 digits, and subtracting nearly equal numbers destroys most of them.
  • Every numerical answer has modelling, discretisation, round-off and iteration error; convergence tests separate them.
  • Stability and conditioning matter as much as accuracy: an explicit scheme can blow up, and an ill-conditioned problem amplifies small errors.
  • Newton's method converges quadratically near a root; optimisers find local, not global, minima.
  • Cost scaling decides what is feasible: dense linear algebra grows as N cubed, and Amdahl's law limits parallel speed-up.

10. Check your understanding#

Why does making the step h ever smaller eventually make a finite-difference derivative worse?

The truncation error falls as a power of h, but the numerator subtracts nearly equal values, so round-off error grows like εmach/h. Their sum has a minimum at an intermediate h, and shrinking h beyond it increases the total error.

A solver gives an error of 10−3 at h = 0.1 and 2.5 × 10−4 at h = 0.05. What is its order?

The error fell by a factor of 4 when h halved, so p = log24 = 2: second order.

A code is 95% parallelisable. What is the best possible speed-up, and what speed-up on 64 processors?

The limit is 1/(1 − 0.95) = 20. On 64 processors, S = 1/(0.05 + 0.95/64) ≈ 15.4.
