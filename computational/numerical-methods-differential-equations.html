Numerical methods II: differential equations, optimisation and Fourier methods

Part I (COMP 201) treated problems that have no time or space in them: arithmetic, equations, linear systems, interpolation and integrals. The simulations of this curriculum are about change in time and variation in space, and this second part supplies the corresponding numerical tools: integrators for ordinary differential equations (reaction kinetics, particle motion), finite-difference discretisations of partial differential equations (diffusion, heat conduction, advection, electrostatics), optimisation (relaxing a structure, fitting a model), eigenvalue algorithms (vibrations, electronic structure), the fast Fourier transform, and the practicalities of making a program fast. The thread running through all of them is the same as in Part I: every method has an order of accuracy, a stability condition and a cost, and the three must be understood together. Lecture 1. Ordinary differential equations: Runge–Kutta methods#

An initial-value problem asks for the function $\mathbf y(t)$ that satisfies $\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(t,\mathbf y)$ with $\mathbf y(0)=\mathbf y_0$. Chemical kinetics ($\mathbf y$ = concentrations), the motion of atoms (positions and velocities) and the method of lines for partial differential equations (Lecture 3) all take this form. A numerical method advances $\mathbf y$ from $t_n$ to $t_{n+1}=t_n+h$. Its local error is the error made in one step, $O(h^{p+1})$ for a method of order $p$; after $T/h$ steps the global error is $O(h^p)$. The explicit Euler method of COMP 102, $y_{n+1}=y_n+hf(t_n,y_n)$, has $p=1$ and is almost never accurate enough: to gain one digit one must take ten times more steps.

Runge–Kutta methods reach higher order by evaluating $\mathbf f$ several times per step, at intermediate points, and combining the results so that the Taylor expansion of the numerical solution matches the exact one to higher order. The second-order midpoint method takes a half Euler step, evaluates the slope there and uses it for the full step: $$k_1=f(t_n,y_n),\quad y_{n+1}=y_n+h\,f\!\left(t_n+\tfrac h2,\,y_n+\tfrac h2k_1\right).$$ The classical fourth-order method (RK4) uses four evaluations, $$k_1=f(t_n,y_n),\;k_2=f\!\left(t_n+\tfrac h2,y_n+\tfrac h2k_1\right),\;k_3=f\!\left(t_n+\tfrac h2,y_n+\tfrac h2k_2\right),\;k_4=f(t_n+h,y_n+hk_3),$$ $$y_{n+1}=y_n+\tfrac h6\,(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4),$$ a weighted average of slopes at the start, twice in the middle and at the end of the step (Simpson’s rule for the integral of the slope). The cost of a step is the number of evaluations of $\mathbf f$ (one for Euler, two for the midpoint method, four for RK4), which usually dominates. The right comparison is therefore accuracy per evaluation. The test problem has the exact solution $y=\tfrac65\mathrm e^{-2t}+\tfrac15(2\sin t-\cos t)$ for $y^{\prime}=-2y+\sin t$, $y(0)=1$:

                return y

Measuring the order of a code in this way, with a problem whose answer is known, is the standard test that a time integrator has been implemented correctly: a method that should be fourth order but shows ratios of $2$ has a bug.

Long-time behaviour: accuracy is not enough

For a conservative system such as the harmonic oscillator $\ddot x=-x$ the exact solution conserves the energy $E=\tfrac12(x^2+v^2)$. A high-order Runge–Kutta method is far more accurate over a short time than velocity Verlet (the integrator of molecular dynamics, COMP 102) but its error is of a different kind:

2. long-time energy of the harmonic oscillator x'' = -x (E = 1/2 at t = 0), h = 0.1, T = 1000
               RK4          E at t = 200, 400, ..., 1000: 0.499986 0.499972 0.499958 0.499945 0.499931
               velocity Verlet E at the same times: 0.499140 0.498927 0.499948 0.499391 0.498790
               RK4 loses energy steadily (artificial damping); Verlet oscillates around the exact value but does not drift

Over $1000$ time units ($160$ periods) with $h=0.1$ the energy of the RK4 solution decreases monotonically, by $6.9\times10^{-8}$ per time unit; the energy of the Verlet solution fluctuates between $0.4988$ and $0.4999$ (a deviation of up to $1.2\times10^{-3}$) but has no trend. Over this short run RK4 is better, but the RK4 drift is a secular error: it grows with time, and if the linear trend continued, after $7\times10^{6}$ time units, a typical length of an MD run measured in oscillation periods, half the energy would be gone, while Verlet is still at $0.4988\pm0.001$. Verlet is symplectic: it exactly conserves a nearby Hamiltonian (the shadow Hamiltonian), so the energy cannot drift (PHYS 101 and CMS 502). The lesson is a general one: choose the integrator for the property that matters over the time you simulate. For a short dissipative problem (reaction kinetics, relaxation) use a high-order method with step control; for a long Hamiltonian problem use a symplectic one.

Adaptive step-size control

The right step is not the same everywhere: small where the solution changes quickly, large where it is smooth. Embedded Runge–Kutta pairs compute two estimates of different order from the same evaluations (the Dormand–Prince pair of scipy.integrate.RK45 has orders $5$ and $4$ with $6$ new evaluations per step). Their difference estimates the local error $\mathrm{err}$; the step is accepted if $\mathrm{err}\le\mathrm{tol}$ (with an absolute tolerance atol and a relative one rtol combined as $\mathrm{atol}+\mathrm{rtol}\,|y|$) and the next step is chosen as $h_\mathrm{new}=0.9\,h\,(\mathrm{tol}/\mathrm{err})^{1/5}$. For the same equation as above:

                  1e-03        9        56   9.902e-04

                  1e-07       41       260   7.750e-08

                  1e-11      241      1460   3.217e-11

The maximum error against the exact solution follows the tolerance closely ($9.9\times10^{-4}$ for $10^{-3}$, $6.4\times10^{-6}$ for $10^{-5}$, down to $3.2\times10^{-11}$ for $10^{-11}$), and the work grows slowly: the number of steps increases by a factor of $27$ ($9\to241$) when the tolerance falls by $10^8$, i.e. as $\mathrm{tol}^{-0.18}\approx\mathrm{tol}^{-1/5}$, as expected for a fifth-order method. Two warnings. The tolerance controls the local error per step, not the global error: the agreement here is a property of this easy problem, and for an unstable problem the global error can be much larger than rtol. And the tolerances are relative to the scale of the variables: with concentrations of order $10^{-10}$ and the default atol of $10^{-6}$ the solver treats them as zero. Always set atol consistently with the units, and always confirm that halving the tolerance does not change the result you care about.

Lecture 2. Stiffness and implicit methods#

Consider the simplest decaying problem $y^{\prime}=-\lambda y$, $\lambda>0$, whose solution decays to zero monotonically. Explicit Euler gives $y_{n+1}=(1-\lambda h)y_n$: the numerical solution decays only if $|1-\lambda h|<1$, i.e. $h<2/\lambda$. For larger steps the solution oscillates with growing amplitude, though the exact solution is smooth and tiny: stability, not accuracy, limits the step. The implicit (backward) Euler method uses the slope at the end of the step, $y_{n+1}=y_n+hf(t_{n+1},y_{n+1})$, which for this problem gives $y_{n+1}=y_n/(1+\lambda h)$, with magnification factor below $1$ for every $h>0$: the method is unconditionally (A-)stable. The price is that each step requires solving an equation for $y_{n+1}$: a nonlinear system in general, handled by Newton’s method (COMP 201, Lecture 2) with the Jacobian $\partial f/\partial y$ and a linear solve (COMP 201, Lecture 3) at each iteration.

At $h=0.05$ ($\lambda h=2.5$) explicit Euler produces $y(1)=3.3\times10^3$ instead of $1.9\times10^{-22}$; at exactly $h=0.04$ it oscillates between $\pm1$ forever ($y(1)=-1$); below the limit it decays (in the right direction, but note it is not accurate: $3.6\times10^{-63}$ at $h=0.021$ against $1.9\times10^{-22}$: it does not need to be, when the solution is already negligible). Implicit Euler gives a decaying result for every step.

What is stiffness?

A problem is stiff when it contains components that decay or oscillate on a time scale much shorter than the time scale on which the solution of interest evolves. The fast components are present in the equations, so an explicit method must resolve them for stability ($h<2/\lambda_\mathrm{max}$) even though they are long dead in the solution. For a system $\dot{\mathbf y}=J\mathbf y$ the ratio of the largest to smallest decay rate of $J$ is the stiffness ratio. Stiffness is very common in materials simulation: reaction networks with rate constants that differ by many orders of magnitude (point defects, precipitation, oxidation); diffusion on a fine mesh (the fastest mode of the discretised operator decays at rate $4D/h^2$, which grows as the mesh is refined); and the Cahn–Hilliard equation of phase-field models, whose fourth-order operator gives rates proportional to $h^{-4}$ (the reason why the spinodal simulation of LAB 506 takes several minutes with a time step controller). The standard test problem is the system of Robertson (1966), a model of three-species chemical kinetics: $$\dot A=-0.04\,A+10^4BC,\qquad\dot B=0.04\,A-10^4BC-3\times10^7B^2,\qquad\dot C=3\times10^7B^2,$$ with rate constants that differ by nine orders of magnitude. The program integrates it with the explicit RK45 and with the implicit methods of SciPy (Radau, a fifth-order implicit Runge–Kutta method; BDF, backward differentiation formulas; LSODA, which switches automatically between a stiff and a non-stiff method), supplying the Jacobian to the first two:

print("\n4. stiffness: Robertson's chemical-kinetics problem, t from 0 to 1e5 (rate constants differ by 9 orders of magnitude; RK45 is only run to t = 1e3, where it already needs about two minutes)")
            def rob(t, y):
                return [-0.04 * y[0] + 1e4 * y[1] * y[2], 0.04 * y[0] - 1e4 * y[1] * y[2] - 3e7 * y[1]**2, 3e7 * y[1]**2]
            def rob_jac(t, y):
                return [[-0.04, 1e4 * y[2], 1e4 * y[1]], [0.04, -1e4 * y[2] - 6e7 * y[1], -1e4 * y[1]], [0, 6e7 * y[1], 0]]
            print("   method    steps   f evals   Jacobians   time (s)    y(1e5) = (A, B, C)")

Practical advice
  • Non-stiff problems and moderate accuracy: RK45 (default of solve_ivp); higher accuracy: DOP853.
  • Stiff problems: Radau or BDF (with a Jacobian if you can supply it), or LSODA when you do not know. For very large sparse systems from PDEs, supply the sparsity pattern (jac_sparsity).
  • Hamiltonian systems integrated for a long time: symplectic methods (velocity Verlet and its relatives), with a fixed time step.
  • If an explicit method has to use a step far below what accuracy requires, you are paying for stability; implicit methods are the way to stop paying.

Lecture 3. Finite differences for diffusion: consistency, stability, convergence#

The diffusion (heat) equation $\partial_tu=D\,\partial_x^2u$ describes the spreading of heat, of solute atoms in a solid (Fick’s second law, CMS 504) and of many other fields. The finite-difference method replaces the continuous variables by values on a grid, $u_j^n\approx u(x_j,t_n)$ with $x_j=jh$ ($h$ the grid spacing) and $t_n=n\tau$ ($\tau$ the time step), and the derivatives by differences. A Taylor expansion shows that $$\frac{u_{j-1}-2u_j+u_{j+1}}{h^2}=\partial_x^2u+\frac{h^2}{12}\partial_x^4u+O(h^4),$$ a second-order approximation, and replacing $\partial_tu$ by the forward difference $(u_j^{n+1}-u_j^n)/\tau$ gives the explicit (FTCS: forward time, centred space) scheme $$u_j^{n+1}=u_j^n+r\left(u_{j+1}^n-2u_j^n+u_{j-1}^n\right),\qquad r=\frac{D\tau}{h^2}.$$ It is trivial to program (a vectorised line of NumPy), needs no linear solve, and has an accuracy $O(\tau+h^2)$. Three properties decide whether a scheme is useful. It is consistent if the difference equation tends to the differential equation as $h,\tau\to0$ (checked with Taylor series); it is stable if rounding and truncation errors do not grow without bound; it is convergent if the numerical solution tends to the true solution. The Lax equivalence theorem says that for a well-posed linear problem and a consistent scheme, stability is necessary and sufficient for convergence: so everything reduces to checking stability.

Von Neumann stability analysis

For a linear scheme on a uniform grid, errors can be decomposed into Fourier modes $u_j^n=g^n\mathrm e^{\mathrm ikjh}$; each mode is multiplied by an amplification factor $g(k)$ per step, and the scheme is stable if $|g(k)|\le1$ for all wave numbers $k$. Inserting the mode in FTCS: $g=1+r(\mathrm e^{\mathrm ikh}-2+\mathrm e^{-\mathrm ikh})=1-4r\sin^2(kh/2)$. The worst case is the shortest wavelength, $kh=\pi$ (the grid mode that alternates in sign, wavelength $2h$): $g=1-4r$, and $|1-4r|\le1$ needs $$r=\frac{D\tau}{h^2}\le\frac12\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\tau\le\frac{h^2}{2D}.$$ This condition makes physical sense: in one step, information moves by $\sqrt{2D\tau}$, which must not exceed one grid spacing. The cost is severe: halving $h$ to improve the spatial resolution divides the allowed time step by four, so the total cost of a computation to a fixed time rises by a factor of eight in one dimension and $2^{d+2}$ in $d$ dimensions. We solve $u_t=u_{xx}$ ($D=1$) on $[0,1]$ with $u=0$ at both ends and $u(x,0)=\sin\pi x$, whose exact solution is $\mathrm e^{-\pi^2t}\sin\pi x$, with $50$ intervals:

Note that $r=0.51$ looks fine at $t=0.1$: the amplification of the unstable mode is $|1-4r|=1.04$ per step, which is only $1.04^{490}\approx2\times10^{8}$ after $490$ steps, applied to a rounding error of $10^{-16}$, and would show up if the run were three times longer. An instability that has not yet appeared is the most dangerous kind: always check a scheme’s stability condition analytically rather than by running it for a short time.

Implicit schemes and the θ-method

The remedy is to evaluate the spatial difference at the new time level (backward Euler) or at the average of the two levels (Crank–Nicolson). Both are special cases of the $\theta$-scheme, $$\frac{u^{n+1}-u^n}{\tau}=D\,\mathcal L\left[\theta u^{n+1}+(1-\theta)u^n\right],$$ with $\mathcal L$ the matrix of the second difference; $\theta=0$ is FTCS, $\theta=1$ backward Euler, $\theta=\tfrac12$ Crank–Nicolson. Each step requires solving the tridiagonal (in 1D) or sparse (in 2D, 3D) system $(I-\theta\tau D\mathcal L)u^{n+1}=(I+(1-\theta)\tau D\mathcal L)u^n$, the kind of problem treated in COMP 201 Lecture 5; the matrix is the same at every step, so one factorisation is computed and reused. The amplification factor is $g=[1-4(1-\theta)r\sin^2(kh/2)]/[1+4\theta r\sin^2(kh/2)]$: for $\theta\ge\tfrac12$ it satisfies $|g|\le1$ for every $r$, so there is no stability restriction. Crank–Nicolson is second order in time, backward Euler first order, and the accuracy is then the only limit on $\tau$. The convergence in time at fixed fine spacing ($h=1/400$) is:

print("\n2. accuracy in time at fixed dx = 1/400: error at t = 0.1 versus the time step")

Method of lines

Discretising space only gives the ordinary differential system $\dot{\mathbf u}=D\mathcal L\mathbf u$ of Lecture 1, which any integrator can solve. The eigenvalues of $D\mathcal L$ lie between $-\pi^2D$ (the slowest mode) and about $-4D/h^2$ (the grid mode), so the stiffness ratio is $\approx(2/\pi h)^2$, which is $6.5\times10^4$ for $h=1/400$: the heat equation on a fine grid is a stiff problem, and the stability limit $r\le\tfrac12$ is the explicit-method stability limit $h_t<2/|\lambda_\mathrm{max}|$ of Lecture 2. The FiPy calculations in LAB 506 do exactly this with backward Euler and sparse linear solvers, and the error-function comparison that lab makes for carburising steel is the same test as the one above.

Lecture 4. Boundary-value problems and advection#

The Poisson equation

Steady heat conduction, electrostatics, the pressure in a flow, the stress function in elasticity and the long-range part of the electrostatic energy of a crystal all lead to Poisson’s equation $-\nabla^2u=f$. In two dimensions on a square with spacing $h$ the second differences in $x$ and $y$ are added, giving the five-point stencil $$\frac{4u_{i,j}-u_{i+1,j}-u_{i-1,j}-u_{i,j+1}-u_{i,j-1}}{h^2}=f_{i,j},$$ one equation for each interior grid point, with the known boundary values moved to the right-hand side. The matrix is the Kronecker sum of two one-dimensional matrices, $A=(I\otimes T+T\otimes I)/h^2$ with $T=\mathrm{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$; it is sparse (five entries per row), symmetric and positive definite, and exactly the matrix whose solution by sparse LU and by conjugate gradients was compared in COMP 201 Lecture 5. The manufactured solution $u=\sin\pi x\sin\pi y$ (for which $f=2\pi^2\sin\pi x\sin\pi y$) tests the discretisation:

Advection: a different kind of error

The advection equation $\partial_tu+a\,\partial_xu=0$ moves a profile with speed $a$ without changing its shape, and is the model for transport by a flow (convection of solute in a melt, of heat in a moving medium). Its discretisation shows errors that diffusion does not have. Let $\nu=a\tau/h$ be the Courant number and consider three schemes on a periodic domain with $N=200$ points:

  • Upwind (backward space difference for $a>0$): $u_j^{n+1}=u_j^n-\nu(u_j^n-u_{j-1}^n)$; first order, stable for $0\le\nu\le1$.
  • Central (FTCS): $u_j^{n+1}=u_j^n-\tfrac\nu2(u_{j+1}^n-u_{j-1}^n)$; second order in space but with $g=1-\mathrm i\nu\sin kh$, $|g|>1$ for every $\nu>0$: unconditionally unstable (the intuitive centred scheme is useless with forward Euler).
  • Lax–Wendroff: adds a second-difference term, $u_j^{n+1}=u_j^n-\tfrac\nu2(u_{j+1}^n-u_{j-1}^n)+\tfrac{\nu^2}2(u_{j+1}^n-2u_j^n+u_{j-1}^n)$; second order in space and time, stable for $\nu\le1$.

The condition $\nu\le1$ is the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition: the numerical domain of dependence of a point must include the physical one, i.e. the signal must not travel more than one grid cell per step. A Gaussian pulse of height $1$ and width $0.05$ is moved once around the domain ($200/0.8=250$ steps):

print("\n4. advection u_t + a u_x = 0, periodic, a Gaussian pulse of height 1 moved once around the domain (N = 200, CFL = a dt/dx = 0.8)")

The upwind scheme is monotone (no negative values) but the peak has decreased from $1$ to $0.745$ and the pulse has widened. A Taylor expansion shows why: the scheme actually solves $\partial_tu+a\partial_xu=\tfrac12ah(1-\nu)\,\partial_x^2u+\dots$, the advection equation plus a diffusion term with the numerical diffusion coefficient $D_\mathrm{num}=\tfrac12ah(1-\nu)=\tfrac12(0.005)(0.2)=5\times10^{-4}$. A Gaussian of width $w$ that diffuses for $T=1$ has the new peak $w/\sqrt{w^2+4D_\mathrm{num}T}=0.05/\sqrt{0.0025+0.002}=0.745$, in agreement with the simulation ($0.7453$). The scheme introduces a diffusivity that no one asked for; this is why first-order upwind schemes smear fronts and why the apparent diffusion coefficient in a transport simulation must be compared with the numerical one. Lax–Wendroff has a much smaller error ($L^2$ error $0.011$ against $0.059$ and peak $0.988$) but creates a small dispersive error: different wavelengths travel at different numerical speeds and trailing oscillations appear (the minimum $-0.0008$ is negative although the true solution is not). The central scheme explodes to $3\times10^{10}$. Modern schemes (flux limiters, WENO) combine the accuracy of the second order scheme in smooth regions with the monotonicity of the upwind scheme at steep fronts.

The four words to keep in mind

Consistency (the scheme approximates the equation), stability (errors do not grow), order (the rate at which the error falls when the grid is refined) and cost (how the work grows with refinement). Good numerical practice is to establish the first three with a manufactured or known exact solution before computing anything whose answer is not known. The error of a PDE solution has two parts, spatial and temporal, which must be refined together; a refinement study in which only one is changed levels off at the other’s error (as the Crank–Nicolson column did).

Lecture 5. Optimisation#

To optimise is to find the point $\mathbf x$ that minimises a function $f(\mathbf x)$ (a maximum of $f$ is a minimum of $-f$). In computational materials science $f$ is the total energy as a function of the atomic positions (geometry optimisation, finding the equilibrium structure and the cell), the misfit between a model and data (fitting, as in MATH 205 and the potentials of CMS 507), or a free energy as a function of composition and structure. A point $\mathbf x^\ast$ is a local minimum if the gradient vanishes, $\nabla f(\mathbf x^\ast)=0$, and the Hessian matrix $H_{ij}=\partial^2f/\partial x_i\partial x_j$ is positive definite there. All the practical methods below find local minima; whether the minimum found is the global one is a separate and much harder question (end of this lecture).

Steepest descent and the role of the condition number

The negative gradient is the direction of fastest decrease, so the simplest method is steepest descent, $\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k-\alpha_k\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)$, with the step length $\alpha_k$ chosen by a one-dimensional minimisation (line search). Its speed is governed by the Hessian. For the quadratic $f=\tfrac12(x^2+\kappa y^2)$ (a valley whose curvatures differ by the factor $\kappa$, the condition number of $H$), the error of steepest descent with exact line search is multiplied by $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ per step: when $\kappa=10^4$ it zigzags across the narrow valley, making progress only $0.02\%$ per step. The conjugate-gradient method (the same algorithm as in COMP 201 Lecture 5, here used for optimisation) chooses each new direction conjugate to the previous ones, and minimises an $n$-variable quadratic in at most $n$ steps. The experiment, with $f=\tfrac12(x^2+\kappa y^2)$ started at $(\kappa,1)$:

"""Optimisation: gradient descent, conjugate gradients, Newton, BFGS; and Lennard-Jones clusters."""

            from scipy.optimize import minimize

                while np.linalg.norm(x) > 1e-6 and n < 10**6:

                    x = x - alpha * g; n += 1

                return n
            for k in (1, 10, 100, 1000, 10000):
                print(f"   {k:12d}        {sd_iterations(k):10d}            {cg_iterations(k):6d}")

Newton and quasi-Newton methods

Near a minimum, $f$ is approximately quadratic, $f(\mathbf x+\mathbf p)\approx f+\mathbf g^T\mathbf p+\tfrac12\mathbf p^TH\mathbf p$, and the minimum of this model is at the Newton step $\mathbf p=-H^{-1}\mathbf g$: Newton’s method for $\nabla f=0$ (COMP 201 Lecture 2), with quadratic convergence but at the cost of the Hessian ($n^2$ entries) and a linear solve ($n^3$). Quasi-Newton methods avoid the Hessian: the BFGS method builds an approximation of $H^{-1}$ from the changes in position and gradient during the iteration, at a cost of $O(n^2)$ per step, and converges superlinearly; the limited-memory L-BFGS keeps only the last $m\approx10$ pairs of vectors, at $O(mn)$ cost and memory, and is the standard choice for large $n$. Comparison on the Rosenbrock function, the classic curved valley $f=(1-x)^2+100(y-x^2)^2$ with the minimum at $(1,1)$, started at $(-1.2,1)$:

The derivative-free Nelder–Mead simplex method needs $249$ function evaluations but no gradient. Analytic forces, which are available in every atomistic code, are what make geometry optimisation practical.

Local minima: the Lennard-Jones clusters

Global optimisation methods combine local optimisation with moves that escape from the basin of attraction: basin hopping (random displacements followed by local minimisation, accepted by a Metropolis criterion), simulated annealing and parallel tempering (Monte Carlo at decreasing temperature, CMS 503), genetic algorithms and particle-swarm methods (the basis of crystal-structure-prediction programs such as USPEX and CALYPSO), and random structure searching (AIRSS). None guarantees the global minimum; each should be repeated from different seeds and the lowest structures compared.

Practical rules for optimisation
  • Use gradients: analytic if possible, automatic differentiation otherwise; check them against finite differences once (COMP 201 Lecture 7).
  • Scale the variables so that their effects on $f$ are comparable (a poorly scaled problem has a huge Hessian condition number).
  • Decide the convergence criterion in physical terms: for a geometry optimisation, a maximum force below $0.01$–$0.05$ eV/Å and a stress below $0.1$ GPa; and re-optimise with a tighter criterion before computing second derivatives (phonons need very tight forces).
  • Check that the result is a minimum, not a saddle point: compute the Hessian (phonons) and look for imaginary frequencies (LAB 301).
  • Run from several starting points. A single optimisation is a single sample.

Lecture 6. Eigenvalue problems#

The equation $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$ appears throughout the subject: the normal modes and frequencies of a crystal ($D\mathbf e=\omega^2\mathbf e$ for the dynamical matrix $D$; PHYS 302, LAB 301), the energy levels of electrons ($H\psi=E\psi$, with $H$ a matrix in a basis; PHYS 201, CMS 501), the principal axes of a stress or inertia tensor, the principal components of a descriptor set, the stability of a numerical scheme. In practice one meets two cases: all eigenvalues of a small or medium dense matrix, and a few (the lowest, or those near a given value) of a large sparse matrix; different algorithms serve each. For symmetric (Hermitian) matrices, which cover almost all physical applications, the eigenvalues are real, the eigenvectors can be chosen orthogonal, and the problem is well conditioned: a perturbation of size $\delta$ of the matrix moves the eigenvalues by at most $\delta$ (Weyl’s theorem). For non-symmetric matrices neither is true.

Power iteration and shift-and-invert

Repeated multiplication of a vector by $A$ amplifies the component along the eigenvector of largest $|\lambda|$: $\mathbf v_{k+1}=A\mathbf v_k/\|A\mathbf v_k\|$ converges to the dominant eigenvector, with the error multiplied by $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ per step (and the Rayleigh quotient $\mathbf v^TA\mathbf v$, for a symmetric matrix, by its square). It is the algorithm behind PageRank and the simplest iterative eigensolver, and it needs only matrix–vector products. Its weakness is the dependence on the eigenvalue ratio:

"""Eigenvalue problems: power iteration, QR algorithm, tight-binding chains, sparse eigensolvers."""
            import numpy as np, scipy.sparse as sp, scipy.sparse.linalg as spl, scipy.linalg as sla, time

            rng = np.random.default_rng(5)

Shift-and-invert applies power iteration to $(A-\sigma I)^{-1}$, whose largest eigenvalue $1/(\lambda_i-\sigma)$ belongs to the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ nearest the shift $\sigma$, and the ratio can be made as good as one likes by moving $\sigma$ closer. It needs a linear solve per iteration (a sparse factorisation computed once). With $\sigma$ updated to the current Rayleigh quotient at each step, the convergence is cubic (Rayleigh quotient iteration).

The QR algorithm

For all eigenvalues of a dense matrix the standard method is the QR algorithm: factor $A_k=Q_kR_k$ (COMP 201 Lecture 4) and form $A_{k+1}=R_kQ_k=Q_k^TA_kQ_k$, a similarity transformation that preserves the eigenvalues; the sequence converges to a triangular (for symmetric matrices, diagonal) matrix with the eigenvalues on the diagonal, ordered by size. Two improvements make it practical: the matrix is first reduced to tridiagonal form by Householder reflections ($\tfrac43n^3$ operations) and each iteration then costs $O(n)$; and a shift (subtract $\sigma I$ before factoring, add it back after, with $\sigma$ near the last diagonal element) together with deflation (as soon as the last row has converged, reduce the problem to size $n-1$) gives cubic or quadratic convergence. For a random symmetric $6\times6$ matrix:

Large sparse matrices: Lanczos and Arnoldi

When $n$ is large and only a few eigenpairs are needed (the lowest levels of an electronic Hamiltonian, the lowest vibrational modes), a dense solver wastes both memory and time. Krylov methods build an orthonormal basis of the space $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf v,A\mathbf v,A^2\mathbf v,\dots\}$ and find the best eigenvalue approximations within it (for symmetric matrices, the Lanczos algorithm; in general, Arnoldi). Extreme eigenvalues converge first; interior ones converge fast with shift-and-invert. SciPy’s scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh (symmetric) and eigs (general) wrap ARPACK. The test is the lowest six eigenvalues of the 2D Laplacian on an $m\times m$ grid, whose exact lowest eigenvalue in the continuum is $2\pi^2$:

Application: tight-binding models

The simplest electronic-structure model puts one orbital on each atom and a hopping amplitude $-t$ between neighbours: $H=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}(c_i^\dagger c_j+\mathrm{h.c.})$, a matrix with $-t$ for every bond. Lecture 7. The fast Fourier transform#

A sampled signal $x_0,\dots,x_{N-1}$ can be written as a sum of $N$ complex exponentials, and the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) gives the amplitudes, $\hat x_k=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_n\mathrm e^{-2\pi\mathrm ikn/N}$, with the inverse $x_n=\tfrac1N\sum_k\hat x_k\mathrm e^{2\pi\mathrm ikn/N}$ (MATH 301). Evaluated as a matrix–vector product, the DFT costs $N^2$ operations. The fast Fourier transform (Cooley and Tukey, 1965, after Gauss) splits the sum into the even- and odd-indexed terms, each a DFT of length $N/2$, and repeats: the cost falls to $O(N\log N)$. It is one of the most important algorithms ever devised, and it is everywhere in materials simulation: plane-wave DFT codes use it to move wave functions between real and reciprocal space, particle-mesh Ewald sums use it for the long-range electrostatics in molecular dynamics, diffraction patterns and structure factors are Fourier transforms of the density, vibrational spectra are Fourier transforms of velocity autocorrelation functions, and phase-field codes can be written in spectral form. The speed-up is dramatic:

Spectral differentiation

In Fourier space the derivative is a multiplication: $\widehat{u^{\prime}}_k=\mathrm ik\,\hat u_k$. For a smooth periodic function this gives the derivative to a precision that finite differences cannot approach with the same number of points, because the error of the FFT derivative decreases faster than any power of the number of points (exponentially for analytic functions): 

2. spectral differentiation of a periodic function f = exp(sin x) on [0, 2 pi): error of f' against exact; finite differences for comparison
                  N     central difference (2nd order)   4th-order difference      FFT (spectral)
                  8            3.402e-01                   1.384e-01             4.318e-03
                 16            9.359e-02                   1.575e-02             1.762e-07
                 32            2.583e-02                   1.132e-03             2.887e-15
                 64            6.512e-03                   7.590e-05             1.066e-14

For $f=\mathrm e^{\sin x}$ the second-order central difference needs $64$ points to get $6.5\times10^{-3}$ and the fourth-order difference $7.6\times10^{-5}$, while the FFT with $32$ points is already at rounding level ($2.9\times10^{-15}$) and is wrong by $4\times10^{-3}$ with only $8$ points. That is why periodic problems (a crystal in a periodic box, a microstructure with periodic boundaries) are often solved pseudospectrally: derivatives in Fourier space, nonlinear products in real space. The price is the periodicity assumption (a non-periodic function gives Gibbs oscillations and algebraic convergence) and the global coupling (a local change affects every point).

Diffusion in Fourier space

For the heat equation with periodic boundary conditions, each Fourier mode evolves independently, $\hat u_k(t)=\hat u_k(0)\mathrm e^{-Dk^2t}$: the solution at any time $t$ is obtained from one forward transform, a multiplication and one inverse transform, without time stepping and without any stability limit. A Gaussian pulse plus a sinusoid on a periodic domain, integrated to $t=0.5$ with $D=0.01$:

The explicit finite-difference solution of the same problem needs $410$ steps (the stability limit) and differs from the spectral solution by $1.9\times10^{-4}$; the difference is the finite-difference error (the spectral result is exact for the resolved modes). The conserved total, $\int u\,\mathrm dx$, is identical ($0.088623$) as it must be: the $k=0$ mode is untouched. The same trick treats the linear part of any equation in phase-field models (the Cahn–Hilliard equation is solved in this “semi-implicit spectral” way in many codes, CMS 505).

Aliasing and leakage

Sampling at a rate $f_s$ can represent frequencies only up to the Nyquist frequency $f_s/2$; a higher frequency masquerades as a lower one (aliasing). A $70$ Hz sine sampled at $100$ Hz is indistinguishable from a $30$ Hz sine:

4. aliasing: a signal sin(2 pi f t) with f = 70 Hz sampled at 100 Hz (Nyquist frequency 50 Hz)
               the largest peak appears at 30.0 Hz (= |70 - 100|), amplitude 1.000
               a true 30 Hz signal sampled the same way gives a peak at 30.0 Hz: the two cannot be distinguished from the samples

The peak is at $30$ Hz ($=|70-100|$) with the full amplitude, and a real $30$ Hz signal gives the same samples (up to the sign of the phase): nothing in the data tells them apart. The molecular-dynamics consequence is direct: if the velocities are saved every $\Delta t_\mathrm{out}$, atomic motions faster than $1/(2\Delta t_\mathrm{out})$ appear at the wrong frequency in the spectrum; to see a bond vibration of $100$ THz one must save at least every $5$ fs and preferably every $1$ fs. The second effect is spectral leakage: the DFT assumes the record is one period of a periodic signal, and when the record does not contain an integer number of periods the discontinuity at the ends spreads the line over many bins:

5. leakage and windows: a sine of frequency 10.5 cycles per record (not an integer) sampled over N = 256 points
               rectangular window: spectrum 30 bins from the peak is 7.43e-03 of the peak, main-lobe width (bins above 0.1 of peak): 12
               Hann        window: spectrum 30 bins from the peak is 1.15e-05 of the peak, main-lobe width (bins above 0.1 of peak): 4
               for an integer 10 cycles per record the spectrum is a single line: other bins are below 5.4e-16

For a sine of $10.5$ cycles in the record the rectangular window leaves a spectrum that is still $7.4\times10^{-3}$ of the peak $30$ bins away, and the main lobe is $12$ bins wide; with a Hann window (a smooth bell-shaped weighting) the far leakage is $1.2\times10^{-5}$ and the lobe $4$ bins wide. For a sine of exactly $10$ cycles the spectrum is a single line (the other bins are at rounding level, $5\times10^{-16}$). So when a spectrum is estimated from a finite simulation, apply a window, or use the autocorrelation function (which decays to zero) as the input: that is how vibrational densities of states are computed from molecular-dynamics trajectories, with a frequency resolution of $1/T$: a $10$ ps run gives $0.1$ THz $=3.3$ cm$^{-1}$.

Lecture 8. Performance, parallelism and random numbers#

A correct program that takes a month is useless; a fast program that is wrong is worse. The rules of optimisation are: first make it correct, then measure where the time goes (never guess), then fix the bottleneck, then measure again. The experiment below computes one quantity, the sum of the distances between all pairs of $N$ random points in three dimensions ($N^2/2$ pairs: $4.5$ million for $N=3000$), in three ways: loops in pure Python, NumPy array operations, and the same loops compiled to machine code by Numba (an @njit decorator):

"""Performance: loops versus vectorisation versus compiled code; memory-bound versus compute-bound; Amdahl; random streams."""
            import numpy as np, time, cProfile, pstats, io
            from numba import njit

            def timeit(f, *a, rep=3):
                best = 1e99
                for _ in range(rep):
                    t0 = time.perf_counter(); r = f(*a); best = min(best, time.perf_counter() - t0)
                return best, r

            print("1. the sum of all pair distances of N random points in 3D: the same O(N^2) work written three ways")
            def pairs_python(X):
                n = len(X); s = 0.0
                for i in range(n):
                    for j in range(i + 1, n):
                        dx = X[i][0] - X[j][0]; dy = X[i][1] - X[j][1]; dz = X[i][2] - X[j][2]
                        s += (dx * dx + dy * dy + dz * dz) ** 0.5
                return s
            def pairs_numpy(X):
                d = X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]
                return 0.5 * np.sqrt((d**2).sum(-1)).sum()
            @njit(cache=False)
            def pairs_numba(X):
                n = X.shape[0]; s = 0.0

                    for j in range(i + 1, n):

                        s += (dx * dx + dy * dy + dz * dz) ** 0.5
                return s

            pairs_numba(rng.random((10, 3)))          # compile once, outside the timing
            print("      N     pure Python     NumPy (N x N x 3 array)     Numba (compiled loops)    results agree")

The vector update $y=ax+y$ does two floating-point operations for every $24$ bytes read or written; the matrix product does $2n^3$ operations on $3n^2$ numbers and reuses each number $\sim n$ times from the cache. The first is limited by the speed of the memory (at least $3.3$ GB/s here; it reaches only $0.27$ GFlop/s), the second by the processor ($180$ GFlop/s): a factor of $657$ between the two on the same hardware. Most of the codes of this curriculum (finite differences, finite elements, molecular dynamics with neighbour lists) are memory-bound, which is why a new processor with ten times more arithmetic power does not make them ten times faster, why single precision (half the memory traffic) speeds them up, and why algorithms that raise the arithmetic intensity (blocking, kernel fusion, dense matrix products inside a plane-wave code) matter.

If a fraction $s$ of the work is serial (cannot be divided among processors), the speed-up on $p$ processors is $S=1/(s+(1-s)/p)$ and the maximum possible is $1/s$ however many processors are used. A code that is $1\%$ serial runs only $39$ times faster on $64$ processors and $91$ times faster on $1024$, never more than $100$; with $10\%$ serial work, $1024$ processors give $9.9$. Real codes also lose time to communication and load imbalance, so one distinguishes strong scaling (a fixed problem on more processors: limited by Amdahl) from weak scaling (a problem that grows with the number of processors: usually limited by communication, and the reason why large molecular-dynamics runs scale well to thousands of processors while a $1000$-atom run does not).

Monte Carlo and stochastic dynamics rely on pseudo-random generators, deterministic sequences that pass statistical tests. Three rules. (1) Seed explicitly and record the seed, so that a surprising result can be reproduced (default_rng(42) gives the same numbers every time). (2) Use a modern generator (NumPy’s default PCG64, or Philox or Xoshiro); the old linear-congruential rand() of C libraries has short periods and correlations. (3) Use independent streams for parallel runs or replicas: SeedSequence.spawn creates child generators with provably non-overlapping states; seeding replica $i$ with $\mathrm{seed}+i$ is acceptable for some generators and can produce correlated streams with others. The four child streams above have correlations of $0.002$, the size expected from $10^5$ samples ($1/\sqrt N=0.003$). A sanity check you should always run on a Monte Carlo code is exactly this kind: independent runs must agree within their statistical errors.

Key takeaways
  • Runge-Kutta methods of order p have a global error proportional to h^p: for y' = -2y + sin t the errors at t = 5 fall by factors 2.0, 4.1 and 16.4 per halving of h for Euler, midpoint and RK4 (2.6e-3, 3.5e-5 and 1.4e-8 at h = 0.031), and adaptive embedded pairs such as RK45 choose the step to meet a tolerance (9 steps for 1e-3, 241 steps for 1e-11).
  • A system is stiff when stability, not accuracy, limits the step of an explicit method: for y' = -50 y explicit Euler is stable only for h < 0.04 (h = 0.05 gives 3.3e3 instead of 1.9e-22), while implicit Euler is stable for every h; on Robertson's chemical kinetics RK45 needs 1.7 million steps and 103 s to reach t = 1000 where the implicit Radau method needs 116 steps and 0.08 s.
  • For the heat equation the explicit scheme is stable only if r = D dt/dx^2 <= 1/2 (max|u| = 0.373 for r <= 0.51 and 4e18 for r = 0.55), backward Euler and Crank-Nicolson are stable for all r with errors proportional to dt (ratio 1.98) and dt^2 (ratio about 4), and the second-order central scheme for Poisson's equation reduces the error by 3.97 per halving of h.
  • Advection exposes the other errors of a discretisation: with CFL 0.8 the first-order upwind scheme smears a pulse of height 1 to 0.745 (numerical diffusion), Lax-Wendroff keeps 0.988 but produces negative values (dispersion), and the unstable central scheme grows to 3e10.
  • Optimisation: steepest descent needs a number of iterations proportional to the condition number (81, 922, 10 362 and 115 130 for k = 10 to 10 000) while conjugate gradients solve a two-variable quadratic in 2 steps; on the Rosenbrock function BFGS needs 34 iterations and plain gradient descent 32 076, and random-start BFGS finds the global minimum of a 7-atom Lennard-Jones cluster from 25 percent of 200 starts, of a 13-atom cluster from 5.5 percent and of a 19-atom cluster from 1 start in 200.
  • Eigenvalues: power iteration converges at the rate (lambda2/lambda1)^2 per step (7932 iterations for a ratio of 0.999), shifted QR with deflation finds all eigenvalues of a 6 x 6 matrix in 17 sweeps against 205 unshifted, Lanczos (eigsh) finds the lowest six eigenvalues of a 14 400-unknown Laplacian in 0.2 s, and the chain and ring tight-binding eigenvalues -2 cos(k pi/(N+1)) and the benzene levels (-2, -1, -1, 1, 1, 2) are reproduced to 1e-15.
  • The FFT turns an O(N^2) transform into O(N log N) (a speed-up of 33 800 at N = 8192), makes derivatives of smooth periodic functions exponentially accurate (error 2.9e-15 with 32 points against 2.6e-2 for second-order differences), solves diffusion exactly mode by mode, and requires care with aliasing (a 70 Hz signal sampled at 100 Hz appears at 30 Hz) and windowing.
  • Performance: for a sum over 4.5 million atom pairs compiled loops (Numba) are 80 times faster than pure Python and 34 times faster than a vectorised NumPy version (0.013 s against 1.02 s and 0.44 s), a memory-bound kernel runs at 0.27 GFlop/s while a matrix product reaches 180 GFlop/s on the same machine, Amdahl's law limits a code with 1 percent serial work to a speed-up of 100, and independent random streams for parallel runs must come from SeedSequence.spawn.

(b) $2mn\times8=2\cdot10\cdot3\times10^5\cdot8=4.8\times10^7$ bytes $=48$ MB and $O(mn)$ operations per step: trivial. (c) Test on a small representative case and count force evaluations, since the force is the dominant cost: nonlinear CG is simple and robust for smooth surfaces but needs a good line search; L-BFGS usually needs the fewest force calls on smooth problems; FIRE (damped molecular dynamics with an adaptive step) is the most robust on rough or noisy surfaces and for systems near a saddle, and is what many codes (LAMMPS min_style fire, ASE) offer as default. Whatever the choice, the final convergence criterion (force threshold) should be the same for a fair comparison, and the result must be checked for a minimum with a Hessian calculation (phonons).

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Core

Explain in your own words why the explicit scheme for the heat equation has a stability limit but the implicit schemes do not, and what the practical cost of the implicit scheme is.

Solution

Explicit: $u^{n+1}$ is computed from $u^n$ alone; the amplification factor of the shortest grid mode is $1-4r$, with magnitude above $1$ for $r=D\tau/h^2>1/2$, so rounding errors in that mode double each step; physically a disturbance must not move farther than one cell per step. Implicit: $u^{n+1}$ is defined by a linear system involving all cells, which damps all modes ($|g|=1/(1+4r\sin^2)\le1$ for any $r$). The price is a sparse linear solve per step (tridiagonal in 1D, a sparse or iterative solve in 2D–3D), and accuracy still demands $\tau$ small compared with the evolution time of the solution.

Quiz 2Core

Define stiffness and give two examples from materials modelling. What symptom tells you that an explicit adaptive solver is being used on a stiff problem?

Solution

A problem is stiff if it contains modes that decay much faster than the time scale of interest, so that the stability of explicit methods, not accuracy, restricts the step. Examples: reaction networks with widely different rate constants (point-defect kinetics, oxidation); diffusion on a fine mesh (fastest mode $4D/h^2$); the Cahn–Hilliard equation ($h^{-4}$). Symptom: the adaptive solver takes an enormous number of tiny steps although the solution is smooth (Robertson: $1.7$ million steps for $t=1000$) and the step does not grow when the tolerance is loosened. The cure is an implicit method (Radau, BDF, LSODA).

Quiz 3Core

You minimise an energy with BFGS and obtain a structure with $|\mathbf F|_\mathrm{max}<10^{-4}$ eV/Å. List two checks before you trust it as the ground state.

Solution

(1) That it is a minimum and not a saddle point: compute the phonons or the Hessian and look for imaginary frequencies; if there are any, displace along the unstable mode and re-relax. (2) That it is the lowest minimum you can find: repeat from several different starting structures (random displacements, other candidate structures, a short annealing run) and compare energies; a local optimiser never tells you that a lower minimum exists. Also check that the energy and structure do not change when the convergence criteria of the underlying energy calculation (cutoff, k points) are tightened.

Quiz 4Core

A periodic function sampled at $N=64$ points is differentiated by finite differences and by FFT. When do you prefer each?

Solution

The FFT derivative is exponentially accurate for smooth, periodic data (error $10^{-14}$ for $\mathrm e^{\sin x}$ with $32$ points) and costs $O(N\log N)$; use it for periodic problems such as a periodic box and for smooth fields. Finite differences are local (cost $O(N)$, parallelise easily, handle non-periodic boundaries, variable grids and discontinuous coefficients) and their error is algebraic ($h^2$ or $h^4$); use them when the function is not smooth, not periodic, or when locality is needed. For a non-periodic function the FFT derivative has Gibbs oscillations near the boundaries.

Quiz 5Core

A graduate student reports that their parallel Monte Carlo code gives different results with $1$ and with $32$ processors, although all runs use the same seed. Name two possible causes.

Solution

(1) The random-number streams on different processors are not the same as the single stream: with a single seed each processor either draws a different part of one stream (the order depends on the number of processors and scheduling) or the same stream is repeated on all (correlated replicas). A different decomposition of the work then gives statistically equivalent but not identical numbers; differences within the statistical error are expected. (2) A parallel reduction (a sum over processors) adds floating-point numbers in a different order, so the results differ in the last digits; in a chaotic dynamics these differences grow exponentially. What must hold is statistical agreement, not bitwise equality: compare averages with error bars, using independent streams from SeedSequence.spawn.

Quiz 6Challenge

You need to estimate the vibrational density of states of a metal from a molecular-dynamics run, a free energy surface from many short runs and the structure factor of a polycrystal from a $2000^3$ grid. Name, for each, the numerical tool of this course that does most of the work and one pitfall.

Solution

(1) Vibrational DOS: FFT of the velocity autocorrelation function. Pitfalls: aliasing from too sparse saving (Nyquist), leakage (use the autocorrelation or a window), resolution $1/T$. (2) Free-energy surface from short runs: analysis of a histogram or a fit, solved by least squares (QR/SVD) or an optimiser; pitfalls: strong correlation between samples (the statistical error needs the autocorrelation time), ill-conditioned fits of high-degree polynomials. (3) Structure factor on a $2000^3=8\times10^9$ grid: a three-dimensional FFT, $O(N\log N)$ in the $8\times10^9$ points; the memory ($64$ GB for doubles, $128$ GB complex) is the problem, not the time: use single precision or a distributed FFT, a smaller grid, or a smaller region of reciprocal space.
