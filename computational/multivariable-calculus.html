Calculus II: several variables and optimisation

Real systems depend on many variables at once: the energy of a crystal depends on every atomic coordinate, a free energy on temperature, pressure and composition, a temperature field on position and time. This module extends single-variable calculus to functions of several variables. You will learn to differentiate them, to find and classify their minima and saddle points, to optimise under constraints, and to integrate over regions of space, using the same ideas that sit inside every force calculation, structure optimiser and thermodynamic derivation in this series.

Lecture 1. Functions of several variables#

A scalar field assigns a number to every point: $f(x,y)$, $f(x,y,z)$, or $f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$. Examples you will meet: the potential energy $U(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N)$ of $N$ atoms (a function of $3N$ variables), the temperature $T(x,y,z,t)$ in a part, the Gibbs energy $G(T,P,x)$ of a phase, a concentration field $c(\mathbf r,t)$.

Functions of two variables are drawn as surfaces $z=f(x,y)$ or, more usefully, as contour maps: curves of constant $f$ (level curves, or level surfaces in 3D). A contour plot of a potential energy surface (PES) shows valleys (stable states), passes between valleys (saddles) and hills. High-dimensional functions cannot be drawn; we study them through the derivatives below, which work in any number of dimensions.

Lecture 2. Partial derivatives#

Definition and notation#

Definition (partial derivative)

The partial derivative of $f(x,y)$ with respect to $x$ is the ordinary derivative with $y$ held constant: $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,y)-f(x,y)}{h},$$ and similarly for $y$. Alternative notations: $f_x$, $\partial_xf$.

In practice: to compute $\partial f/\partial x$, treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate as usual. Example: for $f=x^2y+e^{xy}$, $f_x=2xy+y\,e^{xy}$ and $f_y=x^2+x\,e^{xy}$.

Mixed partials commute. If $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ are continuous then $$\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\,\partial y}=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\,\partial x}.$$ In the example, $f_{xy}=\partial_y(2xy+ye^{xy})=2x+e^{xy}+xy\,\allowbreak e^{xy}$ and $f_{yx}=\partial_x(x^2+xe^{xy})=2x+e^{xy}+xy\,\allowbreak e^{xy}$. This innocent fact is the source of the Maxwell relations of thermodynamics (section 3).

The total differential#

The linear approximation extends directly: for small changes, $f(x+\mathrm{d}x,\,y+\mathrm{d}y)-f(x,y)\approx\mathrm{d}f$, where $$\mathrm{d}f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\mathrm{d}y.$$ Each term is the contribution from one variable; the total is their sum. This is the rule for combining errors (they add, weighted by sensitivities) and the origin of the fundamental thermodynamic relations. For example, the Gibbs energy of a closed system obeys $$\mathrm{d}G=-S\,\mathrm{d}T+V\,\mathrm{d}P,\qquad\text{so}\qquad S=-\Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\Bigr)_P,\quad V=\Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\Bigr)_T .$$ In thermodynamics the subscript on a partial derivative names the variables held fixed. It is not decoration: $(\partial U/\partial T)_V=C_V$ but $(\partial U/\partial T)_P=C_P-P(\partial V/\partial T)_P$, which differ. Always state what is held constant.

In materials science: Maxwell relations

Because $G$ is a state function, $\partial^2G/\partial T\,\allowbreak \partial P=\partial^2G/\partial P\,\allowbreak \partial T$. Substituting $S=-G_T$ and $V=G_P$ gives $$\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\Bigr)_T=-\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_P .$$ The left side is hard to measure; the right side is the thermal expansion coefficient (times $V$), which is easy. Problem 12 puts it to work.

Worked example 2.1: sensitivity of the pressure of a gas

For $P=nRT/V$ find $\mathrm dP$ and the relative change of $P$ if $T$ rises by $2\%$ and $V$ by $1\%$.

$\mathrm dP=\dfrac{nR}{V}\mathrm dT-\dfrac{nRT}{V^2}\mathrm dV$. Dividing by $P=nRT/V$ gives the relative form $\dfrac{\mathrm dP}{P}=\dfrac{\mathrm dT}{T}-\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{V}$. With $\mathrm dT/T=0.02$ and $\mathrm dV/V=0.01$: $\mathrm dP/P\approx+0.01$, a $1\%$ rise. Powers and quotients give sums of relative changes, the pattern behind error propagation in M11.

Lecture 3. The chain rule and implicit differentiation#

If $f=f(x,y)$ and both $x$ and $y$ depend on a parameter $t$, then $$\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t}.$$ If instead $x=x(s,t)$ and $y=y(s,t)$, then $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial s}=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial s}$, and similarly for $t$. The pattern is always: sum over every route through which $f$ depends on the variable.

Worked example 3.1: energy along a path

An atom moves along $x(t)=\cos t$, $y(t)=\sin t$ in a potential $U(x,y)=x^2+3y^2$. Find $\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}t$.

$U_x=2x$, $U_y=6y$, $\dot x=-\sin t$, $\dot y=\cos t$. So $$\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t}=2\cos t\,(-\sin t)+6\sin t\cos t=4\sin t\cos t=2\sin2t.$$ Check by substituting first: $U=\cos^2t+3\sin^2t=1+2\sin^2t$, so $\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}t=4\sin t\cos t$. ✓

Implicit and cyclic relations#

If $F(x,y,z)=0$ defines $z$ implicitly as a function of $x$ and $y$, differentiating the identity $F(x,y,z(x,y))=0$ with respect to $x$ at fixed $y$ gives $F_x+F_z\,z_x=0$, so $$\Bigl(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}.$$ Applying this to three variables linked by an equation of state gives the cyclic (triple-product) relation: $$\Bigl(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\Bigr)_z\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\Bigr)_x\Bigl(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y=-1 .$$ It is surprising the first time: the product is $-1$, not $+1$, because the “cancelling” of $\partial x$, $\partial y$, $\partial z$ is not a real fraction cancellation when different variables are held fixed.

Worked example 3.2: the cyclic relation for the ideal gas

Verify the cyclic relation for $PV=nRT$ with variables $(P,V,T)$.

$P=nRT/V\Rightarrow(\partial P/\partial V)_T=-nRT/V^2$. $V=nRT/P\Rightarrow(\partial V/\partial T)_P=nR/P$. $T=PV/nR\Rightarrow(\partial T/\partial P)_V=V/nR$. The product is $$-\frac{nRT}{V^2}\cdot\frac{nR}{P}\cdot\frac{V}{nR}=-\frac{nRT}{PV}=-1 .$$ A practical use: the bulk modulus $K=-V(\partial P/\partial V)_T$ can be found from the (easier to measure) thermal expansion $\alpha$ and the pressure coefficient via $(\partial P/\partial V)_T=-(\partial P/\partial T)_V/(\partial V/\partial T)_P$.

Lecture 4. The gradient, directional derivatives and forces#

The gradient vector#

Definition (gradient)

The gradient of $f(x,y,z)$ is the vector of its partial derivatives: $$\nabla f=\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y},\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\Bigr).$$

The directional derivative of $f$ along a unit vector $\hat{\mathbf u}$ (the slope you feel when walking in direction $\hat{\mathbf u}$) is $$D_{\hat{\mathbf u}}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=|\nabla f|\cos\theta,$$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\hat{\mathbf u}$. From this, three facts follow at once:

  • $\nabla f$ points in the direction of steepest ascent ($\cos\theta=1$), and its length $|\nabla f|$ is the maximum slope.
  • The slope is zero ($\cos\theta=0$) perpendicular to $\nabla f$, so $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the level curve (or level surface) through that point.
  • $-\nabla f$ points steepest downhill. Following it is steepest descent, the simplest geometry optimiser (M12).
In materials science: force is minus the gradient of energy

For a potential energy $U(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N)$, the force on atom $i$ is $$\mathbf F_i=-\nabla_{\mathbf r_i}U=-\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i},\frac{\partial U}{\partial y_i},\frac{\partial U}{\partial z_i}\Bigr).$$ Every molecular dynamics and every relaxation code computes this vector, either analytically or by automatic differentiation of a machine-learned potential. In density functional theory the same quantity is the Hellmann–Feynman force. Diffusion flux $\mathbf J=-D\nabla c$ and heat flux $\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla T$ have the same structure: things flow “down” the gradient.

Worked example 4.1: force from a central pair potential

An atom at position $\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$ relative to another has energy $U(r)$ depending only on the distance $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$. Find the force.

By the chain rule $\partial U/\partial x=U'(r)\,\allowbreak \partial r/\partial x=U'(r)\,\allowbreak x/r$, and likewise for $y,z$. Therefore $$\nabla U=U'(r)\,\frac{\mathbf r}{r}=U'(r)\,\hat{\mathbf r},\qquad\mathbf F=-U'(r)\,\hat{\mathbf r}.$$ The force is along the line joining the atoms, with magnitude $|U'(r)|$: repulsive if $U'<0$ (energy falls as atoms separate), attractive if $U'>0$. All pair potentials in simulations (Lennard-Jones, Morse, Coulomb) work this way.

Why the gradient is perpendicular to level curves#

Let $\mathbf r(t)$ trace a level curve, so $f(\mathbf r(t))=c$ is constant. Differentiating with the chain rule gives $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=0$: the gradient is orthogonal to every tangent of the level curve. Combined with $D_{\hat{\mathbf u}}f=|\nabla f|\cos\theta$, the gradient direction is the one in which $f$ climbs fastest, at the rate $|\nabla f|$.

Worked example 4.2: linear approximation in two variables

Estimate $f(3.1,\,3.9)$ for $f=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ using the gradient at $(3,4)$.

$f(3,4)=5$ and $\nabla f=\bigl(x/f,\,y/f\bigr)=(0.6,\,0.8)$. Then $f(3.1,3.9)\approx5+0.6(0.1)+0.8(-0.1)=4.98$. The exact value is $\sqrt{24.82}=4.9820$. The first-order estimate is good to about $2\times10^{-3}$, an error of second order in the step.

Lecture 5. Stationary points: the Hessian#

Taylor expansion in several variables#

Writing the displacement as a vector $\mathbf h$, the second-order Taylor expansion about $\mathbf x_0$ is $$f(\mathbf x_0+\mathbf h)=f(\mathbf x_0)+\nabla f(\mathbf x_0)\cdot\mathbf h+\tfrac12\,\mathbf h^{T}\mathsf H\,\mathbf h+O(|\mathbf h|^3),$$ where the Hessian matrix collects all second derivatives, $H_{ij}=\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}$. It is symmetric because mixed partials commute. For two variables, $$\mathsf H=\begin{pmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{xy}&f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}.$$ At a stationary (critical) point $\nabla f=0$ the linear term disappears, so the Hessian alone decides the local shape: $f\approx f_0+\tfrac12\mathbf h^T\mathsf H\mathbf h$. This is the multi-dimensional harmonic approximation of M1.

Classification#

The quadratic form $\mathbf h^T\mathsf H\mathbf h$ is positive for every $\mathbf h\neq0$ (the Hessian is positive definite) exactly when all eigenvalues of $\mathsf H$ are positive (see M5). For two variables there is a quick test with the discriminant $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=\det\mathsf H$:

ConditionTypeEigenvalues of $\mathsf H$
$D>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$Local minimumboth positive
$D>0$ and $f_{xx}<0$Local maximumboth negative
$D<0$Saddle pointone positive, one negative
$D=0$Inconclusiveat least one zero
In materials science: minima, saddles and transition states

On a potential energy surface, minima are stable configurations (a crystal, a defect, a molecule). A first-order saddle point (Hessian with exactly one negative eigenvalue) is a pass between two minima and is the transition state of a reaction or a diffusion hop: the energy difference to the nearest minima is the activation energy, which sets the rate through the Arrhenius law. Methods such as the nudged elastic band and dimer method search for exactly these points. A relaxation that ends on a saddle rather than a minimum has an imaginary vibrational frequency (a negative Hessian eigenvalue): the structure is unstable.

Worked example 5.1: a double well

Classify the stationary points of $f(x,y)=x^4-2x^2+y^2$.

$f_x=4x^3-4x=4x(x^2-1)$ and $f_y=2y$. Stationary points: $y=0$ and $x\in\{0,\pm1\}$. The Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}12x^2-4&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, so $D=2(12x^2-4)$.

At $(\pm1,0)$: $D=16>0$ and $f_{xx}=8>0$, so minima with $f=-1$. At $(0,0)$: $D=-8<0$, a saddle with $f=0$, lying between the two minima. The barrier is $f(0,0)-f(\pm1,0)=1$: this is the cartoon of an atom hopping between two sites, with the saddle as the transition state.

Worked example 5.2: classification with the discriminant

Find and classify the stationary points of $f(x,y)=x^3-3xy+y^3$.

$f_x=3x^2-3y=0$ and $f_y=3y^2-3x=0$ give $y=x^2$ and $x=y^2$, so $x=x^4$: $x=0$ or $x=1$. The real stationary points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. The Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}6x&-3\\-3&6y\end{pmatrix}$ with $D=36xy-9$. At $(0,0)$, $D=-9<0$: a saddle. At $(1,1)$, $D=27>0$ and $f_{xx}=6>0$: a minimum, with $f=-1$.

Lecture 6. Constrained optimisation: Lagrange multipliers#

Often you must extremise $f$ subject to a constraint $g(\mathbf x)=0$: minimise energy at fixed composition, maximise entropy at fixed mean energy, minimise surface area at fixed volume. Along the constraint curve, $f$ is stationary when its level curve is tangent to the constraint curve, i.e. when $\nabla f$ and $\nabla g$ are parallel.

Method of Lagrange multipliers

To find extrema of $f$ subject to $g=0$, solve $$\nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g,\qquad g(\mathbf x)=0$$ for $\mathbf x$ and the multiplier $\lambda$. Equivalently, find the stationary points of $\mathcal L=f-\lambda g$ with respect to $\mathbf x$ and $\lambda$. With several constraints $g_k=0$, use one multiplier each: $\nabla f=\sum_k\lambda_k\nabla g_k$.

The multiplier is not just a device: $\lambda$ measures how fast the optimum value changes if the constraint is relaxed, $\lambda=\mathrm{d}f_\mathrm{opt}/\mathrm{d}c$ for $g=c$. In thermodynamics the multipliers of the energy and number constraints become $1/T$ and $\mu/T$.

Worked example 6.1: the Boltzmann distribution

A system has states $i$ with energies $E_i$ and probabilities $p_i$. Maximise the entropy $S=-k_\mathrm{B}\sum_ip_i\ln p_i$ subject to $\sum_ip_i=1$ and $\sum_ip_iE_i=U$.

Form $\mathcal L=-\sum_ip_i\ln p_i-\alpha\bigl(\sum_ip_i-1\bigr)-\beta\bigl(\sum_ip_iE_i-U\bigr)$ (dropping the constant $k_\mathrm{B}$, which does not change where the maximum is). Setting $\partial\mathcal L/\partial p_i=0$: $$-\ln p_i-1-\alpha-\beta E_i=0\ \Longrightarrow\ p_i=e^{-1-\alpha}\,e^{-\beta E_i}.$$ The constant is fixed by normalisation, giving $p_i=e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$ with $Z=\sum_ie^{-\beta E_i}$. The multiplier $\beta$ is fixed by the energy constraint; comparing $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}U=1/T$ shows $\beta=1/(k_\mathrm{B}T)$. The Boltzmann distribution is therefore the least biased probability assignment consistent with a known average energy (the maximum-entropy principle). Problem 9 evaluates it for a three-level system.

Worked example 6.2: the cube minimises surface area

Of all boxes with volume $V$, which has the least surface area? Minimise $A=2(ab+bc+ca)$ subject to $abc=V$.

$\nabla A=2(b+c,\ a+c,\ a+b)$ and $\nabla(abc)=(bc,\ ac,\ ab)$. The conditions $\nabla A=\lambda\nabla(abc)$, after multiplying the three equations by $a$, $b$, $c$ respectively, read $$2a(b+c)=\lambda abc,\quad 2b(a+c)=\lambda abc,\quad 2c(a+b)=\lambda abc,$$ so $a(b+c)=b(a+c)\Rightarrow ac=bc\Rightarrow a=b$, and likewise $b=c$. The optimum is a cube, $a=b=c=V^{1/3}$ with $A=6V^{2/3}$. Problem 8 repeats this with different surface energies on different faces and gets the Wulff shape of a crystal.

Worked example 6.3: a maximum on a circle

Maximise $x+y$ on the circle $x^2+y^2=1$.

$\nabla(x+y)=(1,1)=\lambda(2x,2y)$, so $x=y=\dfrac1{2\lambda}$; the constraint then gives $2x^2=1$, $x=y=\pm\dfrac1{\sqrt2}$. The maximum is $\sqrt2$ at $\bigl(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigr)$ (with $\lambda=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$) and the minimum $-\sqrt2$ at the opposite point. Geometrically the level lines $x+y=c$ touch the circle where the circle’s normal is parallel to $(1,1)$.

Lecture 7. Multiple integrals#

Double and triple integrals#

The integral of $f(x,y)$ over a region $R$ of the plane, $\iint_Rf\,\mathrm{d}A$, is the volume under the surface (more generally a total of “density times area”). For a rectangle it is evaluated as an iterated integral, and the order does not matter (Fubini’s theorem): $$\iint_Rf\,\mathrm{d}A=\int_a^b\!\Bigl(\int_c^df(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Bigr)\mathrm{d}x=\int_c^d\!\Bigl(\int_a^bf(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)\mathrm{d}y .$$ For a non-rectangular region, the inner limits depend on the outer variable. Sketching the region and choosing the order that makes the inner integral easy is the whole art. Triple integrals $\iiint f\,\mathrm{d}V$ work the same way, with the mass of a body $\iiint\rho\,\mathrm{d}V$ and thermal averages over position as the standard uses.

Worked example 7.1: reversing the order

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1e^{y^2}\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}y\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x$.

The inner integral $\int e^{y^2}\mathrm{d}y$ has no elementary form. The region is the triangle $0\le x\le y\le1$. Describe it the other way: $y$ runs from $0$ to $1$ and, for each $y$, $x$ runs from $0$ to $y$: $$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^ye^{y^2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_0^1y\,e^{y^2}\,\mathrm{d}y=\Bigl[\tfrac12e^{y^2}\Bigr]_0^1=\tfrac12(e-1)\approx0.859.$$

Change of variables and the Jacobian#

When the region or integrand has a symmetry, change to better coordinates $(u,v)$ with $x=x(u,v)$, $y=y(u,v)$. Areas scale by the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant: $$\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v,\qquad\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\begin{vmatrix}x_u&x_v\\y_u&y_v\end{vmatrix}.$$ The three coordinate systems you will use most:

SystemCoordinatesVolume element
Polar (2D)$x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$$\mathrm{d}A=r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\theta$
Cylindrical$x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, $z$$\mathrm{d}V=r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}z$
Spherical$x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z=r\cos\theta$$\mathrm{d}V=r^2\sin\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\phi$

(In spherical coordinates, $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ is the polar angle from the $z$-axis and $\phi\in[0,2\pi)$ the azimuth.) For polar coordinates, $\begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}=r$, which is why the extra factor $r$ appears: a ring of radius $r$ has circumference $2\pi r$.

Worked example 7.2: the Gaussian integral

Show $\displaystyle I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x=\sqrt\pi$.

Square it and treat the two factors as integrals over $x$ and $y$: $$I^2=\int\!\!\int_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi\Bigl[-\tfrac12e^{-r^2}\Bigr]_0^\infty=\pi.$$ So $I=\sqrt\pi$ (positive, since the integrand is). Scaling $x\to\sqrt a\,x$ gives $\int e^{-ax^2}\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\pi/a}$, the result used throughout statistical mechanics.

Worked example 7.3: the Maxwell speed distribution

Atoms of mass $m$ at temperature $T$ have velocity probability density $f(\mathbf v)=\bigl(\tfrac{m}{2\pi k_\mathrm{B}T}\bigr)^{3/2}e^{-m|\mathbf v|^2/(2k_\mathrm{B}T)}$. Find the distribution of speeds $v=|\mathbf v|$ and the mean speed.

The density depends only on $v$, so integrate over the angles in spherical velocity coordinates, where $\mathrm{d}^3v=v^2\sin\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}v\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\phi$. The angular integral is $\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\theta\int_0^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi=4\pi$ (the area of the unit sphere), so $$F(v)\,\mathrm{d}v=4\pi\,v^2\Bigl(\frac{m}{2\pi k_\mathrm{B}T}\Bigr)^{3/2}e^{-mv^2/(2k_\mathrm{B}T)}\,\mathrm{d}v .$$ With $b=m/(2k_\mathrm{B}T)$ the integral $\int_0^\infty v^3e^{-bv^2}\mathrm{d}v=\dfrac1{2b^2}$ (substitute $u=v^2$) gives $\langle v\rangle=4\pi(b/\pi)^{3/2}/(2b^2)=\sqrt{8k_\mathrm{B}T/(\pi m)}$. The factor $v^2$ from the volume element is why the distribution vanishes at $v=0$ even though the velocity density is largest there: there are few ways to have almost no speed, and many to have a moderate one.

Worked example 7.4: the volume of a sphere

Use spherical coordinates to find the volume of a ball of radius $R$.

$V=\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^Rr^2\sin\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm dr\,\allowbreak \mathrm d\theta\,\allowbreak \mathrm d\phi=\frac{R^3}3\cdot2\cdot2\pi=\frac43\pi R^3$. The three integrals separate because the integrand is constant and the limits are independent; the Jacobian $r^2\sin\theta$ does all the geometry.

Summary of the unit#

A function of many variables is understood through its gradient (the slope, which gives forces and fluxes) and its Hessian (the curvature, which gives stiffness, stability and transition states). Constraints are handled by Lagrange multipliers, which in statistical mechanics turn into temperature and chemical potential. Integrals over several variables, with the right Jacobian, are how averages over positions or velocities are computed. The next module packages the gradient together with the divergence and curl into vector calculus, which expresses conservation laws and produces the diffusion equation.

Key takeaways
  • A partial derivative holds the other variables fixed; in thermodynamics the subscript says what is held fixed, and it matters, e.g. $(\partial U/\partial T)_V \neq (\partial U/\partial T)_P$.
  • The gradient $\nabla f$ points uphill, is perpendicular to level curves, and has magnitude equal to the steepest slope; a force is the negative gradient of a potential energy, $\mathbf{F}=-\nabla U$.
  • The Hessian matrix of second derivatives classifies stationary points: all positive eigenvalues is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle (a transition state has exactly one negative eigenvalue).
  • Lagrange multipliers solve constrained optimisation, and maximising entropy under a fixed mean energy gives the Boltzmann distribution $p_i\propto e^{-\beta E_i}$.
  • Changing variables in a multiple integral needs the Jacobian ($r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta$ in the plane, $r^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi$ in space); it turns the Gaussian integral and the Maxwell speed distribution into one-line calculations.

For $f(x,y)=x^3y^2-\ln(xy)$ with $x,y>0$: (a) compute $f_x$, $f_y$, $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$, $f_{xy}$; (b) verify $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$; (c) write the total differential $\mathrm{d}f$ at $(1,2)$.

Hint

Write $\ln(xy)=\ln x+\ln y$ before differentiating.

Full solution

Write $f=x^3y^2-\ln x-\ln y$.

(a) $f_x=3x^2y^2-\dfrac1x$, $f_y=2x^3y-\dfrac1y$, $f_{xx}=6xy^2+\dfrac1{x^2}$, $f_{yy}=2x^3+\dfrac1{y^2}$, $f_{xy}=\partial_y(3x^2y^2-1/x)=6x^2y$.

(b) $f_{yx}=\partial_x(2x^3y-1/y)=6x^2y=f_{xy}$. ✓

(c) At $(1,2)$: $f_x=3\cdot4-1=11$ and $f_y=2\cdot2-\tfrac12=3.5$. So $\mathrm{d}f=11\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x+3.5\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}y$.

Problem 2.2Basic

The density of a material is computed as $\rho=m/V$ with $V=abc$ for a measured sample with $a=2.00$, $b=3.00$, $c=1.00$ cm (each $\pm0.01$ cm) and $m=47.0\pm0.1$ g. (a) Compute $\rho$. (b) Use the total differential (relative form) to estimate the worst-case relative error in $\rho$.

Hint

For a product or quotient, $\mathrm{d}\rho/\rho=\mathrm{d}m/m-\mathrm{d}a/a-\mathrm{d}b/b-\mathrm{d}c/c$, since $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho=\sum\mathrm{d}\ln(\text{factors})^{\pm1}$. In the worst case all the errors add in magnitude.

Full solution

(a) $V=2.00\times3.00\times1.00=6.00$ cm$^3$, so $\rho=47.0/6.00=7.83$ g/cm$^3$ (close to iron).

(b) $\ln\rho=\ln m-\ln a-\ln b-\ln c$, so $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\rho}=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}m}{m}-\dfrac{\mathrm{d}a}{a}-\dfrac{\mathrm{d}b}{b}-\dfrac{\mathrm{d}c}{c}$. Worst case (all magnitudes add): $\dfrac{0.1}{47.0}+\dfrac{0.01}{2.00}+\dfrac{0.01}{3.00}+\dfrac{0.01}{1.00}=0.00213+0.00500+0.00333+0.01000=0.0205$. So $\rho=7.83\pm0.16$ g/cm$^3$ (about $2\%$). The smallest dimension dominates the error: measure thin directions more carefully. (In M11 you will see that independent errors normally combine in quadrature, giving a smaller estimate than this worst case.)

$\rho=7.83$ g/cm³, worst-case relative error $\approx2.1\%$

Problem 2.3Basic

Let $u(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ and use polar coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. (a) Find $\partial u/\partial r$ and $\partial u/\partial\theta$ with the chain rule. (b) Check by writing $u$ in terms of $r,\theta$ directly.

Hint

$\partial x/\partial r=\cos\theta$, $\partial y/\partial r=\sin\theta$, $\partial x/\partial\theta=-r\sin\theta$, $\partial y/\partial\theta=r\cos\theta$.

Full solution

(a) $u_x=2x$, $u_y=-2y$. Then $u_r=u_x\cos\theta+u_y\sin\theta=2r\cos^2\theta-2r\sin^2\theta=2r\cos2\theta$, and $u_\theta=u_x(-r\sin\theta)+u_y(r\cos\theta)=-2r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta-2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta=-2r^2\sin2\theta$.

(b) $u=r^2\cos^2\theta-r^2\sin^2\theta=r^2\cos2\theta$, so $u_r=2r\cos2\theta$ and $u_\theta=-2r^2\sin2\theta$. ✓ (This $u$ is also a harmonic function, $\nabla^2u=0$, which you will use in M9.)

Problem 2.4Core

A plate has temperature $T(x,y)=100-x^2-2y^2$ (in °C, with $x,y$ in cm). At the point $(1,1)$: (a) find $\nabla T$ and the direction in which temperature increases fastest; (b) find the rate of change of temperature (°C/cm) when moving in the direction $(1,1)/\sqrt2$; (c) give a direction along which $T$ does not change; (d) if the thermal conductivity is $\kappa=50$ W m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, give the heat flux vector $\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla T$ at $(1,1)$.

Hint

Level curves of $T$ are perpendicular to $\nabla T$.

Full solution

(a) $\nabla T=(-2x,-4y)=(-2,-4)$ at $(1,1)$. Temperature rises fastest in the direction $(-1,-2)/\sqrt5$ (toward the hot centre at the origin), at the rate $|\nabla T|=\sqrt{20}=4.47$ °C/cm.

(b) $D_{\hat u}T=\nabla T\cdot\hat u=(-2,-4)\cdot(1,1)/\sqrt2=-6/\sqrt2=-4.24$ °C/cm: moving away from the centre, the plate cools.

(c) A direction perpendicular to $\nabla T$, for example $(2,-1)/\sqrt5$ (check: $(-2)(2)+(-4)(-1)=0$). This is tangent to the isotherm through $(1,1)$.

(d) Convert to SI first: $\nabla T=(-2,-4)$ °C/cm $=(-200,-400)$ K/m. Then $\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla T=-50\,\allowbreak (-200,-400)=(10\,000,\ 20\,000)$ W/m$^2$, with magnitude $22.4$ kW/m$^2$. It points away from the hot centre, down the temperature gradient, as heat flows from hot to cold.

$\nabla T=(-2,-4)$; $D_u T=-4.24$ °C/cm; $\mathbf q=(1,2)\times10^4$ W/m²

Problem 2.5Core

In reduced units ($\varepsilon=\sigma=1$) two atoms interact via $U(r)=4\bigl(r^{-12}-r^{-6}\bigr)$. The second atom sits at $\mathbf r=(1,1,1)$ relative to the first. (a) Show that the force on it is $\mathbf F=(48r^{-14}-24r^{-8})\,\allowbreak \mathbf r$. (b) Evaluate $\mathbf F$ at $\mathbf r=(1,1,1)$ and say whether it is attractive or repulsive.

Hint

Use Example 4.1: $\mathbf F=-U'(r)\,\mathbf r/r$.

Full solution

(a) $U'(r)=4\bigl(-12r^{-13}+6r^{-7}\bigr)=-48r^{-13}+24r^{-7}$. Then $\mathbf F=-U'(r)\,\allowbreak \mathbf r/r=(48r^{-13}-24r^{-7})\,\allowbreak \mathbf r/r=(48r^{-14}-24r^{-8})\,\allowbreak \mathbf r$.

(b) $r=\sqrt3$, so $r^{-14}=3^{-7}=4.572\times10^{-4}$ and $r^{-8}=3^{-4}=0.012346$. The prefactor is $48(4.572\times10^{-4})-24(0.012346)=0.021948-0.296296=-0.27435$. Hence $\mathbf F=-0.2743\,(1,1,1)$: each component is $-0.274$ and $|\mathbf F|=0.475$. The force on atom 2 points back toward atom 1 (the negative of its position vector), so the interaction is attractive: $r=1.73>r_0=1.12$, beyond the minimum. As a check, $r=1.73$ is also beyond the inflection point $1.24$ found in M1, where the attraction is largest, so it is decaying.

$\mathbf F=-0.274\,(1,1,1)$, magnitude $0.475$, attractive

Problem 2.6Core

Find and classify all stationary points of $f(x,y)=x^4+y^4-2x^2-2y^2$. If this were a potential energy surface, which points are stable states and which are transition states? What is the lowest barrier between two minima?

Hint

The function separates into $g(x)+g(y)$ with $g(t)=t^4-2t^2$, so the Hessian is diagonal.

Full solution

$f_x=4x^3-4x=4x(x-1)(x+1)$ and $f_y=4y(y-1)(y+1)$, so stationary points have $x,y\in\{-1,0,1\}$: nine points. The Hessian is diagonal with $f_{xx}=12x^2-4$ and $f_{yy}=12y^2-4$, so each eigenvalue is $+8$ if the coordinate is $\pm1$ and $-4$ if it is $0$.

PointsHessian eigenvaluesType$f$
$(\pm1,\pm1)$ (4 points)$+8,+8$minimum$-2$
$(\pm1,0)$, $(0,\pm1)$ (4 points)$+8,-4$first-order saddle$-1$
$(0,0)$$-4,-4$maximum$0$

The four minima are the stable states; the four first-order saddles (exactly one negative eigenvalue) are transition states, each connecting two neighbouring minima, for instance $(1,1)\to(1,-1)$ via $(1,0)$. The lowest barrier is $f(\text{saddle})-f(\text{min})=-1-(-2)=1$. The maximum at the origin (two negative eigenvalues, an “index-2 saddle” in chemical language) is not a transition state: no lowest-energy path between minima passes through it. (The surface is a model of an atom or ion hopping between four equivalent sites.)

Problem 2.7Core

(a) Maximise $f(x,y)=xy$ subject to $x+2y=8$ using a Lagrange multiplier, and interpret $\lambda$. (b) Find the point on the line $x+2y=8$ closest to the origin using the same method.

Hint

For (b) minimise $x^2+y^2$. Check (a) by substituting $x=8-2y$.

Full solution

(a) $\nabla f=(y,x)$, $\nabla g=(1,2)$ with $g=x+2y-8$. Then $y=\lambda$, $x=2\lambda$, and the constraint gives $2\lambda+2\lambda=8$, so $\lambda=2$, $x=4$, $y=2$, $f=8$. Check: with $x=8-2y$, $f=8y-2y^2$ has a maximum at $y=2$. ✓ Interpretation: if the constraint is relaxed to $x+2y=8+\delta$, the maximum becomes $f_\mathrm{max}(c)=c^2/8$, whose derivative at $c=8$ is $c/4=2=\lambda$: the multiplier is the sensitivity of the optimum to the constraint.

(b) Minimise $x^2+y^2$: $(2x,2y)=\lambda(1,2)$, so $x=\lambda/2$, $y=\lambda$. Constraint: $\lambda/2+2\lambda=8\Rightarrow\lambda=3.2$, giving $(x,y)=(1.6,3.2)$ and distance $\sqrt{1.6^2+3.2^2}=3.58$. This agrees with the point-to-line distance formula $8/\sqrt{1^2+2^2}=3.578$. ✓

(a) $(4,2)$, $f=8$, $\lambda=2$; (b) $(1.6,3.2)$, distance $3.58$

Problem 2.8Challenge

A crystal particle is a rectangular box with side lengths $a,b,c$ and fixed volume $V=abc$. The pair of faces perpendicular to the $a$-direction (each of area $bc$) has surface energy $\gamma_a$ per unit area, and similarly $\gamma_b$, $\gamma_c$. The total surface energy is $E=2\bigl(\gamma_abc+\gamma_bac+\gamma_cab\bigr)$. Minimise $E$ at fixed $V$ and show that $a:b:c=\gamma_a:\gamma_b:\gamma_c$ (the Wulff construction: the distance between a pair of faces is proportional to their surface energy). Find the minimum energy for $\gamma_a=1,\gamma_b=2,\gamma_c=4$ J/m$^2$ and $V=8\times10^{-27}$ m$^3$.

Hint

Multiply the three stationarity equations by $a$, $b$, $c$ respectively and compare. Let $X=\gamma_abc$, $Y=\gamma_bac$, $Z=\gamma_cab$.

Full solution

$\mathcal L=E-\lambda(abc-V)$. The conditions: $$2(\gamma_bc+\gamma_cb)=\lambda bc,\qquad2(\gamma_ac+\gamma_ca)=\lambda ac,\qquad2(\gamma_ab+\gamma_ba)=\lambda ab .$$ Multiply the first by $a$, the second by $b$, the third by $c$ and set $X=\gamma_abc$, $Y=\gamma_bac$, $Z=\gamma_cab$: they become $2(Y+Z)=\lambda abc$, $2(X+Z)=\lambda abc$, $2(X+Y)=\lambda abc$. Hence $Y+Z=X+Z=X+Y$, so $X=Y=Z$.

$X=Y$ means $\gamma_abc=\gamma_bac$, i.e. $\gamma_ab=\gamma_ba$, so $a/\gamma_a=b/\gamma_b$; likewise $b/\gamma_b=c/\gamma_c$. Therefore $a:b:c=\gamma_a:\gamma_b:\gamma_c$: the box is longest between the faces with the highest energy, which are therefore the smallest in area. (Low-energy faces dominate the shape.)

Numbers: let $a=s\gamma_a$, etc.: $a=s,\ b=2s,\ c=4s$. Then $V=8s^3=8\times10^{-27}$, so $s=10^{-9}$ m and $(a,b,c)=(1,2,4)$ nm. Energy: $E=2\bigl(1\cdot(2\cdot4)+2\cdot(1\cdot4)+4\cdot(1\cdot2)\bigr)\times10^{-18}=2(8+8+8)\times10^{-18}=4.8\times10^{-17}$ J. (Equal $X=Y=Z$ terms, as the theory predicts.)

$a:b:c=\gamma_a:\gamma_b:\gamma_c$; $(a,b,c)=(1,2,4)$ nm; $E_\mathrm{min}=4.8\times10^{-17}$ J

Problem 2.9Challenge

A system has three states with energies $E=0,1,2$ (in units where $k_\mathrm{B}T$ will be measured in the same units). (a) Use the Boltzmann form $p_i\propto x^{E_i}$ with $x=e^{-\beta}$ and find $x$, $\beta$ and the three probabilities if the mean energy is $\langle E\rangle=0.5$. (b) What would the mean energy be if the three states were equally likely, and what temperature (value of $\beta$) does that correspond to?

Hint

$\langle E\rangle=(x+2x^2)/(1+x+x^2)$. Set this equal to $0.5$ and solve the resulting quadratic for $x>0$.

Full solution

(a) With $Z=1+x+x^2$: $\langle E\rangle=\dfrac{0\cdot1+1\cdot x+2x^2}{1+x+x^2}=0.5$. Cross-multiplying: $x+2x^2=0.5+0.5x+0.5x^2\Rightarrow1.5x^2+0.5x-0.5=0\Rightarrow3x^2+x-1=0$, so $x=\dfrac{-1+\sqrt{13}}{6}=0.4343$ (the positive root). Then $\beta=-\ln x=0.834$ (so $k_\mathrm{B}T=1/\beta=1.20$ in these units). $Z=1+0.4343+0.1886=1.6229$ and $p_0=0.616$, $p_1=0.268$, $p_2=0.116$ (sum $1.000$). Check: $\langle E\rangle=0.268+2(0.116)=0.500$. ✓

(b) Equal probabilities have $\langle E\rangle=(0+1+2)/3=1$, which corresponds to $x=1$, i.e. $\beta=0$: infinite temperature, the maximum-entropy state with no energy constraint. Mean energies below $1$ need $\beta>0$ (positive temperature); a mean energy above $1$ would need $\beta<0$ (a population inversion, negative temperature).

$x=0.434$, $\beta=0.834$; $p=(0.616,0.268,0.116)$; equal probabilities at $\beta=0$

Problem 2.10Core

(a) Evaluate $\displaystyle\iint_Rxy\,\mathrm{d}A$ where $R$ is the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$. (b) Use polar coordinates to evaluate $\displaystyle\iint_{x^2+y^2\le R_0^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}A$ and find its limit as $R_0\to\infty$.

Hint

(a) The triangle is $0\le y\le x\le1$. (b) Remember the Jacobian $r$.

Full solution

(a) $\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^xxy\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}y\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x=\int_0^1x\cdot\frac{x^2}2\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x=\int_0^1\frac{x^3}{2}\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}x=\frac18$.

(b) $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{R_0}e^{-r^2}r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}r\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}\theta=2\pi\cdot\tfrac12\bigl(1-e^{-R_0^2}\bigr)=\pi\bigl(1-e^{-R_0^2}\bigr)$. As $R_0\to\infty$ this tends to $\pi$, the result of Example 7.2. The integrand is the same Gaussian, but in polar coordinates it has the elementary antiderivative $-\tfrac12e^{-r^2}$ thanks to the extra factor $r$.

(a) $1/8$; (b) $\pi(1-e^{-R_0^2})\to\pi$

Problem 2.11Core

For argon gas ($m=39.95$ u $=6.634\times10^{-26}$ kg) at $T=300$ K, use the Maxwell speed distribution of Example 7.3 to compute (a) the mean speed $\langle v\rangle$, (b) the most probable speed $v_\mathrm{p}$ (the maximum of $F(v)$), and (c) the root-mean-square speed $v_\mathrm{rms}=\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}$. Use $\int_0^\infty v^4e^{-bv^2}\mathrm{d}v=\dfrac{3\sqrt\pi}{8b^{5/2}}$. ($k_\mathrm{B}=1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K.)

Hint

For (b) set $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}v=0$: $F\propto v^2e^{-bv^2}$. For (c), $\langle v^2\rangle=4\pi(b/\pi)^{3/2}\int_0^\infty v^4e^{-bv^2}\mathrm{d}v$.

Full solution

Let $b=m/(2k_\mathrm{B}T)$ and $k_\mathrm{B}T=4.143\times10^{-21}$ J.

(a) From Example 7.3, $\langle v\rangle=\sqrt{\dfrac{8k_\mathrm{B}T}{\pi m}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{8(4.143\times10^{-21})}{\pi(6.634\times10^{-26})}}=\sqrt{1.590\times10^5}=399$ m/s.

(b) $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}v}\bigl(v^2e^{-bv^2}\bigr)=(2v-2bv^3)e^{-bv^2}=0\Rightarrow v_\mathrm{p}=1/\sqrt b=\sqrt{\dfrac{2k_\mathrm{B}T}{m}}=\sqrt{1.249\times10^5}=353$ m/s.

(c) $\langle v^2\rangle=4\pi\Bigl(\dfrac b\pi\Bigr)^{3/2}\dfrac{3\sqrt\pi}{8b^{5/2}}=\dfrac{3}{2b}=\dfrac{3k_\mathrm{B}T}{m}$, so $v_\mathrm{rms}=\sqrt{\dfrac{3k_\mathrm{B}T}{m}}=\sqrt{1.873\times10^5}=433$ m/s. This agrees with the equipartition result $\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle=\tfrac32k_\mathrm{B}T$.

The ordering $v_\mathrm{p}<\langle v\rangle<v_\mathrm{rms}$ (ratios $1:1.128:1.225$) reflects the tail towards high speeds. In molecular dynamics, the initial velocities of atoms are drawn from exactly this distribution.

$\langle v\rangle=399$, $v_\mathrm{p}=353$, $v_\mathrm{rms}=433$ m/s

Problem 2.12Challenge

(a) From $\mathrm{d}G=-S\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}T+V\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}P$ derive the Maxwell relation $(\partial S/\partial P)_T=-(\partial V/\partial T)_P$. (b) For one mole of ideal gas, find $S(T,P)-S(T,P_0)$ by integrating the relation along an isotherm. (c) A solid has volumetric thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha=\dfrac1V(\partial V/\partial T)_P=3.0\times10^{-5}$ K$^{-1}$ and molar volume $7.1\times10^{-6}$ m$^3$/mol (iron-like). By how much does its molar entropy change when the pressure is raised by $1$ GPa at constant $T$?

Hint

(a) $S=-G_T$ and $V=G_P$; then $S_P=-G_{TP}$ and $V_T=G_{PT}$. (c) Treat $V$ and $\alpha$ as constant over the pressure range.

Full solution

(a) Reading off the differential: $S=-(\partial G/\partial T)_P$ and $V=(\partial G/\partial P)_T$. Then $(\partial S/\partial P)_T=-\dfrac{\partial^2G}{\partial P\,\partial T}$ and $(\partial V/\partial T)_P=\dfrac{\partial^2G}{\partial T\,\partial P}$. Mixed partials commute, so $(\partial S/\partial P)_T=-(\partial V/\partial T)_P$.

(b) For one mole, $V=RT/P$ and $(\partial V/\partial T)_P=R/P$. So $(\partial S/\partial P)_T=-R/P$, and integrating at constant $T$: $S(T,P)-S(T,P_0)=-R\ln(P/P_0)$. Compressing a gas lowers its entropy, as expected.

(c) $(\partial S/\partial P)_T=-V\alpha=-(7.1\times10^{-6})(3.0\times10^{-5})=-2.13\times10^{-10}$ J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$ Pa$^{-1}$. Over $1$ GPa ($10^9$ Pa): $\Delta S=-0.213$ J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$. This is tiny compared with the molar entropy of iron (about $27$ J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$): pressure changes the entropy of a solid very little, which is why the $P\Delta V$ and $-TS$ contributions to $G$ matter so differently for solids and gases, and why CALPHAD models (Thermodynamics and CALPHAD) often neglect pressure for condensed phases at ambient conditions.

(b) $-R\ln(P/P_0)$; (c) $\Delta S\approx-0.21$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Gradient

$f(x,y)=xe^y+y^2$. Find $\nabla f$ at $(1,0)$ and the directional derivative along $(3,4)/5$.

Solution

$\nabla f=(e^y,\ xe^y+2y)=(1,\ 1)$ at $(1,0)$. $D_{\hat u}f=(1,1)\cdot(0.6,0.8)=1.4$.

Quiz 2Stationary points

Classify the stationary points of $f=x^3-12x+y^2$.

Solution

$\nabla f=(3x^2-12,\ 2y)=0$ at $(\pm2,0)$. $H=\mathrm{diag}(6x,2)$: at $(2,0)$ both eigenvalues positive, a minimum with $f=-16$; at $(-2,0)$ eigenvalues $-12$ and $2$, a saddle point with $f=16$.

Quiz 3Constraint

Minimise $x^2+y^2$ subject to $2x+y=5$.

Solution

$(2x,2y)=\lambda(2,1)$ gives $x=\lambda$, $y=\lambda/2$; the constraint gives $\tfrac52\lambda=5$, $\lambda=2$. Minimum at $(2,1)$ with value $5$ (distance $\sqrt5$ from the origin).

Quiz 4Double integral

Evaluate $\iint(x+y)\,\mathrm dA$ over the triangle with vertices $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$.

Solution

$\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x}(x+y)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dy\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\int_0^1\Bigl[x(1-x)+\tfrac12(1-x)^2\Bigr]\mathrm dx=\int_0^1\tfrac12(1-x^2)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\tfrac13$.

Quiz 5Polar coordinates

Evaluate $\iint_{x^2+y^2\le4}(x^2+y^2)\,\allowbreak \mathrm dA$.

Solution

$\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2r^2\cdot r\,\allowbreak \mathrm dr\,\allowbreak \mathrm d\theta=2\pi\cdot\tfrac{2^4}4=8\pi$ (the extra $r$ is the Jacobian).

Quiz 6Chain rule

Let $z=x^2y$ with $x=t^2$, $y=t^3$. Find $\mathrm dz/\mathrm dt$ in two ways.

Solution

Chain rule: $2xy\cdot2t+x^2\cdot3t^2=2t^5\cdot2t+t^4\cdot3t^2=7t^6$. Direct substitution: $z=t^7$, $\mathrm dz/\mathrm dt=7t^6$. ✓
