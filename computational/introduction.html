Introduction to computational materials science

Computational materials science uses physical models and numerical methods to predict, explain and design the structure, processing and properties of materials. No single model covers every scale, so the field is a toolbox of methods, each valid over a limited range of length, time and chemistry.

1. Scope and purpose#

The central problem of materials science is the processing–structure–properties–performance chain: how a material is made determines its structure at every scale, structure determines properties, and properties determine how it performs in service. Computation can be applied to any link in this chain. In the forward direction it predicts properties from a given structure or composition. In the inverse direction it searches for compositions and processes that deliver a target property.

What simulation adds to experiment

  • Access to what cannot be measured: atomic-scale mechanisms, short-lived intermediates, internal fields during deformation, temperature or composition in places no probe can reach.
  • Speed and cost: thousands of virtual compositions can be screened before a single melt is made.
  • Isolation of variables: a simulation can change one parameter, such as one solute or one boundary type, while holding everything else fixed, which real samples rarely allow.
  • Interpretation: models give a mechanism that explains why a measured trend occurs, which is what allows extrapolation beyond tested conditions.

What simulation cannot do

Every model contains approximations and needs inputs, and the output is no better than either. Models are usually most trustworthy for trends and for ranking candidates, and least trustworthy for absolute numbers on complex real material, which has defects, impurities, residual stress and a processing history the model may not represent. A prediction is a hypothesis to be tested, not a measurement.

What to know first#

The series assumes undergraduate-level familiarity with a handful of subjects. If any feel rusty, the curriculum has a course for each, with the simulation use in mind: mathematics (calculus, linear algebra, vector calculus, differential equations, Fourier analysis, probability, statistics), physics (mechanics, electrostatics, quantum mechanics, thermodynamics, units), materials science essentials (bonding, structure, defects, transformations, mechanical behaviour), numerical methods (precision, stability, solvers, scaling) and programming and workflow (command line, Python, git, clusters). Integrated computational materials engineering

ICME is the practice of linking models across scales, together with experiments and databases, to shorten the path from composition and process to component performance. The US Materials Genome Initiative (launched 2011) pushed the related idea of combining computation, experiment and data to cut the time needed to bring new materials to use.

2. Length and time scales#

Material behaviour arises from structure at many scales at once: electrons bond atoms, atoms form defects, defects interact to form microstructure, and microstructure controls the bulk response. Cost grows steeply as a model resolves finer detail, so a method covers only a window of scales. The table summarises the windows; values are order-of-magnitude and depend on hardware and implementation.

Two gaps matter in practice. First, atomistic time scales (up to microseconds) are many orders of magnitude shorter than the time scales of diffusion-controlled processing (seconds to hours). Second, atomistic strain rates are typically 107 s−1 or higher, against about 10−3 s−1 in a tensile test. Bridging these gaps is a main reason for coarse-grained, mesoscale and accelerated-dynamics methods.

3. Method families#

3.1 Density functional theory (DFT)#

Basis. The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (1964) show that the ground-state energy of an interacting electron system is a unique functional of the electron density, and the Kohn–Sham approach (1965) replaces the many-body problem by non-interacting electrons moving in an effective potential:

[ −½∇² + vext(r) + vH(r) + vxc(r) ] ψi(r) = εi ψi(r),   n(r) = Σi |ψi(r)|²

Here vext is the nuclear potential, vH the classical electron–electron (Hartree) term, and vxc the exchange–correlation potential, which contains all the remaining many-body physics and is the one quantity that must be approximated. The equations are solved self-consistently because n(r) enters the potential that determines it.

Approximations to choose. The local density approximation (LDA) uses the exchange–correlation energy of a uniform electron gas; generalised gradient approximations (GGA, for example PBE) add dependence on the density gradient; meta-GGAs (for example SCAN) add kinetic-energy density; hybrid functionals (for example HSE06) mix in part of exact exchange at higher cost. Other practical choices are the pseudopotential or PAW dataset, the plane-wave cutoff energy, the k-point mesh for Brillouin-zone sampling, smearing for metals, spin polarisation, and corrections such as DFT+U for localised d and f states or van der Waals terms for layered and molecular materials.

Outputs. Total energies (hence formation energies, defect energies, surface and interface energies, migration barriers by nudged elastic band), forces and stresses (hence relaxed structures, elastic constants, phonons), electronic density of states and band structure, magnetic moments.

Cost and limits. Conventional Kohn–Sham DFT scales roughly with the cube of the system size, which limits cells to hundreds or a few thousand atoms. Standard GGA tends to underestimate band gaps of semiconductors and insulators, and treats strongly correlated systems poorly. Results are normally at 0 K and for perfect periodic cells, so finite-temperature and long-time behaviour need additional steps. Magnetism can decide the answer: LDA famously predicts the wrong ground-state structure of iron, which GGA corrects.

3.2 Molecular dynamics (MD)#

Basis. Atoms are treated as classical particles that move according to Newton’s equations under forces derived from an interatomic potential U:

mi d²ri/dt² = Fi = −∇i U(r1, …, rN)

The equations are integrated in small time steps (typically about 1 fs, shorter if light atoms such as hydrogen are involved; the step must resolve the fastest vibration). Statistical-mechanical ensembles are imposed with thermostats and barostats: NVE (constant energy), NVT (constant temperature) and NPT (constant temperature and pressure). Properties are time or ensemble averages over the trajectory.

Interatomic potentials are the decisive input. Lennard–Jones and Morse are simple pair potentials. The embedded-atom method (EAM, Daw and Baskes, 1984) and modified EAM include an environment-dependent term and are standard for metals. Stillinger–Weber and Tersoff describe directional covalent bonding (silicon, carbon). ReaxFF allows bond breaking and forming. A growing class of machine-learned potentials (for example Gaussian approximation, moment tensor and neural-network potentials) is fitted to DFT data and approaches DFT accuracy at far lower cost, within the chemical and structural range they were trained on.

Outputs. Diffusion coefficients from mean-squared displacement, thermal expansion, melting point, free-energy differences, elastic and thermal properties, dislocation and crack mechanisms, grain boundary structure and energy, radiation damage cascades.

Limits. The result is only as good as the potential, which may be wrong outside the data it was fitted to. Time scales stop at microseconds and strain rates are extreme. Electrons are absent, so electronic and magnetic effects are missing unless built into the potential.

3.3 Monte Carlo methods#

Monte Carlo (MC) samples configurations randomly instead of following physical trajectories. In Metropolis MC (1953), a trial change from state a to state b is accepted with probability

P(a→b) = min[ 1, exp( −(Eb − Ea) / kBT ) ]

so that the configurations visited follow the Boltzmann distribution. Because moves need not be physical, MC can equilibrate quantities that MD reaches too slowly, such as chemical ordering, segregation of solutes to interfaces, or composition in multicomponent alloys (swap moves). Kinetic Monte Carlo (kMC) instead picks events, such as an atom hopping into a vacancy, with rates from transition-state theory, and advances a physically meaningful clock; it reaches seconds or longer but needs a catalogue of possible events and their barriers, often taken from DFT. Lattice models such as the Ising model and cluster expansions, fitted to DFT energies, provide the energy function for many alloy MC studies.

3.4 CALPHAD#

Basis. The CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagrams) method models the molar Gibbs energy of every phase as a function of temperature, pressure and composition, and finds equilibrium by minimising the total Gibbs energy of the system. A typical solution-phase model has the form

Gm = Gref + Gideal + Gexcess + Gmag,   Gideal = RT Σ xi ln xi

where the excess term is commonly written as a Redlich–Kister polynomial in composition, and phases with distinct sublattices (carbides, intermetallics, interstitial solutions) use the compound energy formalism. Model parameters are fitted simultaneously to phase-diagram data, thermochemical measurements and first-principles results, and collected into thermodynamic databases (often distributed as .tdb files). Binary and ternary descriptions are extrapolated to multicomponent systems.

Outputs. Phase diagrams and isopleths, equilibrium phase amounts and compositions, solidus and liquidus, solvus lines, transformation temperatures, activities, driving forces for precipitation, and (with the Scheil–Gulliver model) solidification paths under limited diffusion.

Limits. It is a bulk equilibrium method with no geometry, no interfaces and no time unless coupled to a kinetic model. Accuracy depends on how well the database covers the composition and temperature region of interest, which is a common source of error for unusual compositions. Coupling with diffusion mobility databases allows simulation of diffusion-controlled transformations in one dimension and supplies the thermodynamic input to phase-field models.

3.5 Phase-field modelling#

Basis. Interfaces are represented as diffuse transition layers, so the interface need not be tracked explicitly. One or more continuous order parameters φ(r, t) distinguish phases or grains, and the free energy of the whole microstructure is a functional

F = ∫ [ f(φ, c, T) + ½ κφ|∇φ|² + ½ κc|∇c|² + fel ] dV

Evolution follows by relaxation toward lower free energy. For a non-conserved order parameter (grain orientation, phase identity) this is the Allen–Cahn equation, ∂φ/∂t = −L δF/δφ. For a conserved quantity such as composition it is the Cahn–Hilliard equation, ∂c/∂t = ∇·(M∇δF/δc). Elastic, magnetic and electrostatic energies can be added to the functional.

Typical applications. Dendritic and cellular solidification, grain growth, spinodal decomposition and precipitation, martensitic and diffusional solid-state transformations, ferroelectric domain evolution, fracture and corrosion fronts.

Practical points. The numerical grid must resolve the interface width, which makes large domains and long times expensive; thin-interface formulations relax this. Inputs are free-energy densities (often from CALPHAD), mobilities, interface energies and anisotropies, many of which are poorly known and must be taken from atomistic work or calibrated.

3.6 Dislocation dynamics and crystal plasticity#

Discrete dislocation dynamics (DDD) follows individual dislocation lines, moving each under the Peach–Koehler force from applied stress and from other dislocations, with rules for reactions such as junction formation and cross-slip. It reaches micrometre-scale volumes and addresses hardening and size effects.

Crystal plasticity treats each grain as a single crystal that deforms by slip on defined systems. Slip on system α begins when the resolved shear stress, τα = mα·σ·nα (Schmid’s law), reaches a critical value, and the shear rate follows a flow rule with hardening laws. Implemented in a finite-element or spectral solver (crystal plasticity finite element method, CPFEM; spectral solvers such as DAMASK), it predicts texture evolution, anisotropy, grain-level strain partitioning and the response of polycrystals and multiphase aggregates, using a representative volume element (RVE) built from measured or synthetic microstructure.

3.7 Continuum methods#

Continuum models treat the material as a smooth medium and solve conservation laws as partial differential equations: heat conduction, mass and momentum balance (fluid flow), species transport, and mechanical equilibrium with a constitutive law linking stress to strain and strain rate. The finite element method (FEM) divides the domain into elements and solves a weak form of the equations; the finite volume method is common in computational fluid dynamics (CFD). They are used for casting and welding thermal fields, forging, rolling and forming, heat treatment distortion, residual stress, fatigue and fracture assessment, and component-level design.

Accuracy rests on the constitutive law and on boundary conditions, such as heat-transfer coefficients and friction, which are often the dominant uncertainty. Mesh convergence and time-step control are essential.

3.8 Machine learning#

Machine learning (ML) fits flexible models to data instead of deriving them from physics. In materials work it appears in four roles: (i) property prediction from composition or structure descriptors, (ii) interatomic potentials fitted to DFT, (iii) surrogate models that replace expensive simulations, and (iv) inverse design and active learning, where the model proposes the next experiment or calculation (for example by Bayesian optimisation).

Key issues are data quantity and quality (materials datasets are small and biased toward successful experiments), the choice of descriptors or learned representations, the reliability of predictions outside the training domain (extrapolation), uncertainty estimates, and interpretability. Proper cross-validation matters: random splits of related compounds leak information, and splitting by composition family or by time is a stricter and more honest test. Physics-informed models, which embed symmetries or known constraints, generally need less data.

4. Linking scales#

Multiscale modelling connects the methods above. Two coupling strategies are common.

  • Hierarchical (sequential, parameter passing): a finer model computes a quantity that is passed as a parameter to a coarser model. Example: DFT gives defect and interface energies; MD or kMC gives diffusivities and mobilities; CALPHAD supplies free energies; a phase-field model then uses all of these to predict microstructure; the microstructure feeds a crystal-plasticity or continuum model for properties.
  • Concurrent: different regions or levels run together and exchange information during the run, as in quantum-mechanics/molecular-mechanics (QM/MM) or atomistic/continuum coupling around a crack tip. These are harder to set up and less common.

Information also flows downward: a continuum thermal model provides the temperature history that a microstructure model needs. Along every interface of the chain, errors propagate, so each link should be validated on its own before the chain is trusted.

5. A typical workflow#

  1. Define the question in measurable terms: which property, which condition, what accuracy is needed.
  2. Select the model from the smallest feature and longest time that matter (section 7).
  3. Prepare inputs: structure or geometry, composition, potential or database, boundary and initial conditions, material parameters.
  4. Test convergence of numerical parameters before production runs (section 10).
  5. Run on a workstation or cluster; script it so that it is repeatable.
  6. Post-process and visualise; compute uncertainties, not only a single number.
  7. Validate against independent experiment or a higher-level calculation (section 6).
  8. Document versions, input files and random seeds so the work can be reproduced.

6. Verification, validation and uncertainty#

These three terms are often confused.

Verification
Are the equations solved correctly? Checks against analytical solutions, benchmark problems, mesh and time-step refinement, and code tests.
Validation
Are the right equations being solved? Comparison of predictions with experiments that were not used to fit the model.
Uncertainty quantification
How confident should one be in the number? Propagates uncertainty in inputs, parameters and numerical error to the output, and distinguishes systematic error (a poor model or potential) from statistical error (finite sampling).

Typical sources of error include the exchange–correlation functional in DFT, the potential in MD, database coverage in CALPHAD, the neglect of defects and impurities, finite-size and finite-time effects, discretisation error, and poorly known input parameters. A model that reproduces its own training data has been fitted, not validated.

7. Choosing a method#

Start from the question, not the tool. Three questions usually narrow the choice quickly:

  1. What is the smallest feature that controls the answer? Bond energies and electronic effects point to DFT; defect cores and interfaces point to atomistics; grains and precipitates point to mesoscale; whole components point to continuum models.
  2. What is the longest time that matters? Microsecond limits rule out direct MD for diffusional processing; use kMC, phase-field or a continuum model.
  3. Is the answer an equilibrium state or a rate? Equilibrium is often settled by thermodynamics alone, which is cheap; rates need a kinetic model.
QuestionReasonable first method
Which phases are stable at this composition and temperature?CALPHAD
How strongly does a solute bind to a defect or interface?DFT
What is the diffusion coefficient or boundary mobility of an element?MD, kMC or DFT-derived barriers; or experimental data
How does a dislocation or crack behave in this lattice?MD (with a validated potential)
How does the microstructure evolve during heat treatment or solidification?Phase-field, with CALPHAD input
How does a polycrystal deform and develop texture?Crystal plasticity
What temperature, stress and distortion arise in the process or part?FEM or CFD
Which of thousands of candidates should be tried first?High-throughput DFT or machine learning

Often the cheapest useful step is a thermodynamic calculation, because it reveals which phases can exist before heavier modelling is attempted.

9. Databases and data#

  • Materials Project, AFLOW, OQMD and JARVIS: large collections of DFT-computed structures and properties.
  • NOMAD: repository and archive for computational materials data from many codes.
  • Crystallographic databases: Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD, licensed), Crystallography Open Database (COD, open), Pearson’s Crystal Data.
  • Thermodynamic and phase-diagram data: CALPHAD databases (commercial and some open), the ASM Alloy Phase Diagram Database, and NIST resources.
  • Interatomic potentials: the NIST Interatomic Potentials Repository and OpenKIM, which also provides tests for potentials.

Computed values in public databases carry the approximations of their method (typically GGA), so treat them as consistent comparisons rather than exact numbers.

10. Good practice#

  • Converge before you compute. Vary the plane-wave cutoff and k-point mesh in DFT, the cell size and time step in MD, the grid spacing and time step in phase-field, and the mesh in FEM, until the quantity of interest stops changing within the accuracy you need.
  • Check the size effects. Periodic cells that are too small interact with their own images; MD boxes must be large compared with the defect or correlation length.
  • Equilibrate and sample enough. Discard the start-up transient; report averages with error bars from independent runs or block averaging.
  • Mind units and conventions. Codes use different unit systems (for example LAMMPS “metal” units) and different sign and normalisation conventions.
  • Start simple. Reproduce a known result, such as a lattice parameter, an elastic constant or a textbook phase boundary, before attempting a novel one.
  • Keep it reproducible. Store inputs, versions and seeds under version control, and archive the data that supports a published figure.
Key takeaways
  • Methods are specialised by length and time scale; no single model covers every scale, so multiscale linking is the norm.
  • Start from the question: the smallest feature that matters, the longest time that matters, and whether the answer is an equilibrium or a rate.
  • A simulation is a prediction to be tested. Verification, validation and uncertainty quantification are three different checks.
  • The cheapest useful step is often a thermodynamic (CALPHAD) calculation, before heavier modelling is attempted.
  • Inputs and approximations bound accuracy: trends and rankings are usually more reliable than absolute numbers.

11. Check your understanding#

Which method would you reach for first to estimate the vacancy formation energy of copper, and why?

Density functional theory: the quantity is an energy difference between a small cell with and without the defect, which needs electronic accuracy rather than a long trajectory. If a validated embedded-atom potential exists, molecular dynamics or static relaxation with that potential gives a much cheaper first estimate that can be checked against a few DFT calculations.

Why can plain molecular dynamics not follow the homogenisation of a segregated alloy?

Homogenisation is diffusion over micrometres lasting seconds to hours, while molecular dynamics reaches tens of nanometres and nanoseconds to microseconds. A kinetic Monte Carlo, phase-field or continuum diffusion model covers the relevant scales, with diffusivities and thermodynamics supplied by finer-scale methods.

What is the difference between verification and validation?

Verification asks whether the equations are solved correctly (convergence tests, benchmarks, code checks). Validation asks whether the right equations and inputs are being used, by comparing predictions with independent experiments that were not used to fit the model.

12. Glossary#

Ab initio
“From first principles”: methods that use no fitted parameters for the material of interest, such as DFT.
Ensemble
The set of conditions held fixed in a statistical-mechanics simulation (NVE, NVT, NPT).
Gibbs energy
The thermodynamic potential that is minimised at equilibrium at constant temperature and pressure.
Interatomic potential
A function giving the energy of a set of atoms from their positions, used in classical simulations.
Order parameter
A field that distinguishes one phase or grain orientation from another in phase-field models.
Pseudopotential
An effective potential that replaces core electrons in DFT so that only valence electrons are treated explicitly.
Representative volume element (RVE)
A volume of microstructure large enough that its averaged response represents the bulk material.
Surrogate model
A fast approximate model, often machine-learned, fitted to the output of an expensive simulation.
Transition-state theory
A theory that gives the rate of a thermally activated event from the energy barrier between two states.
