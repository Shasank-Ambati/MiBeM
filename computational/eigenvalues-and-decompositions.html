Linear algebra II: eigenvalues and decompositions

If one idea from linear algebra reaches into every corner of computational materials science, it is the eigenvalue problem. Atomic vibrations are eigenvectors of a force-constant matrix; electron energies are eigenvalues of a Hamiltonian; principal stresses are eigenvalues of the stress tensor; stability of a structure is the sign of Hessian eigenvalues; the dominant directions in a dataset are eigenvectors of a covariance matrix. This course develops the theory and then shows how each of these follows from the same few facts.

Lecture 1. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors#

Definition (eigenpair)

For a square matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $\mathbf v$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if $$A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v .$$ Applying $A$ to $\mathbf v$ leaves its direction unchanged and only scales it by $\lambda$ (flipping it if $\lambda<0$).

The characteristic polynomial#

Rearrange to $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf0$. A nonzero solution exists only if $A-\lambda I$ is singular, so $$\det(A-\lambda I)=0,$$ the characteristic equation, a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\lambda$ with $n$ roots (counted with multiplicity, possibly complex). For each root, solve $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf0$ for the eigenvector, which is determined only up to a scalar multiple; usually one normalises it to length $1$. Two checks are always worth making: $$\sum_i\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_iA_{ii},\qquad\prod_i\lambda_i=\det A .$$ For a $2\times2$ matrix, $\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\,\allowbreak \lambda+\det A=0$, so $\lambda=\tfrac12\bigl(\operatorname{tr}A\pm\sqrt{(\operatorname{tr}A)^2-4\det A}\bigr)$.

Worked example 1.1: a $2\times2$ eigenproblem

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.

$\operatorname{tr}A=4$, $\det A=3$, so $\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=0$ gives $\lambda=1,3$. For $\lambda=3$: $(A-3I)\mathbf v=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf v=\mathbf0$ gives $v_1=v_2$, so $\mathbf v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$. For $\lambda=1$: $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf v=\mathbf0$ gives $v_2=-v_1$, so $\mathbf v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$. The two eigenvectors are orthogonal. Geometrically $A$ stretches by $3$ along the diagonal $(1,1)$ and by $1$ along $(1,-1)$: the unit circle becomes an ellipse with those axes.

Matrices need not have real eigenvalues: a rotation by $\theta$ has $\lambda=e^{\pm i\theta}$ (no real direction is preserved). Some matrices have fewer independent eigenvectors than $n$ ("defective" matrices, such as $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$), but the symmetric matrices of physics never do.

Why eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent

Suppose $A\mathbf v_1=\lambda_1\mathbf v_1$ and $A\mathbf v_2=\lambda_2\mathbf v_2$ with $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$ and $c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2=\mathbf0$. Applying $A$ gives $c_1\lambda_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\lambda_2\mathbf v_2=\mathbf0$; subtracting $\lambda_2$ times the first equation leaves $c_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\mathbf v_1=\mathbf0$, so $c_1=0$ and then $c_2=0$. Hence $n$ distinct eigenvalues give $n$ independent eigenvectors and $A$ is diagonalisable.

Lecture 2. Diagonalisation#

If $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_n$, put them as columns of $P$ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$. Then $AP=P\Lambda$, so $$A=P\Lambda P^{-1}.$$ In the basis of eigenvectors, $A$ is just the diagonal matrix $\Lambda$: a set of independent stretches. This makes functions of $A$ trivial: $A^k=P\Lambda^kP^{-1}$ and, more generally, $f(A)=P\,f(\Lambda)\,P^{-1}$, with $f(\Lambda)=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(f(\lambda_i)\bigr)$. For instance $A^{-1}$ has eigenvalues $1/\lambda_i$ (so $A$ is invertible iff no $\lambda_i=0$), $A^k$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_i^k$, and the matrix exponential is $e^{A}=Pe^{\Lambda}P^{-1}$.

Applications: repeated application of a matrix (Markov chains and kinetic Monte Carlo transition matrices; $A^k$ for large $k$ is dominated by the largest eigenvalue, which is how the power iteration finds a ground state) and the solution of linear differential equations (section 5).

Worked example 2.1: powers by diagonalisation

Compute $A^3$ for $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$ by diagonalising.

The eigenpairs are $\lambda=5$ with $(1,1)$ and $\lambda=2$ with $(-1,2)$ (check $A(-1,2)^T=(-2,4)^T=2(-1,2)^T$). With $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $A=P\,\mathrm{diag}(5,2)\,P^{-1}$ and $P^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Then $A^3=P\,\allowbreak \mathrm{diag}(125,8)\,\allowbreak P^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}125&-8\\125&16\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}86&39\\78&47\end{pmatrix}$. (Direct multiplication, $A\cdot A\cdot A$, agrees.) For large $k$ the term $5^k$ dominates, so $A^k\approx5^k\times$ the projector onto $(1,1)$: this is how a Markov chain or a power iteration converges to its dominant eigenvector.

Lecture 3. Symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem#

Spectral theorem

If $A$ is real and symmetric ($A^T=A$), then (i) all its eigenvalues are real; (ii) eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and a full orthonormal set of eigenvectors can always be chosen; hence $$A=Q\Lambda Q^{T}=\sum_i\lambda_i\,\mathbf q_i\mathbf q_i^{T},$$ with $Q$ orthogonal ($Q^TQ=I$). The second form writes $A$ as a sum of eigenvalues times projectors onto the eigenvectors.

Why orthogonal? If $A\mathbf u=\lambda\mathbf u$ and $A\mathbf w=\mu\mathbf w$ with $\lambda\ne\mu$, then $\lambda\,\allowbreak \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u=\mathbf w\cdot A\mathbf u=A\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u=\mu\,\allowbreak \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u$ (using symmetry), so $(\lambda-\mu)\,\allowbreak \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u=0$ and $\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u=0$. The same argument with complex conjugates shows the eigenvalues are real. Repeated eigenvalues (degeneracy) leave a free choice of orthonormal basis within the degenerate subspace, which is exactly the freedom of orienting degenerate vibrational modes or $d$-orbitals.

Symmetric (or, for complex matrices, Hermitian) matrices are not a special case but the typical case in physics, because they come from second derivatives of energies and from observables:

  • Hamiltonian $H$: real energies; orthogonal states (section 7).
  • Hessian of the energy and the dynamical matrix: real vibrational frequencies$^2$ and orthogonal normal modes.
  • Stress and strain tensors: orthogonal principal axes (MATH 302).
  • Covariance matrix of a dataset: orthogonal principal components (section 8).

In practice, library routines (eigh in NumPy and LAPACK) exploit symmetry and are faster, more accurate and guarantee real eigenvalues. Use the symmetric solver whenever the matrix is symmetric.

Worked example 3.2: a three-mass chain

Three equal masses are joined by springs to each other and to fixed walls at both ends: $K=k\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Find the normal modes.

The matrix is symmetric, so the eigenvectors are orthogonal. Trying the shapes $\sin(n\pi j/4)$ gives $\lambda=2-2\cos(n\pi/4)$: $n=1$: $\lambda=2-\sqrt2$ with $\mathbf v=(1,\sqrt2,1)$; $n=2$: $\lambda=2$ with $(1,0,-1)$; $n=3$: $\lambda=2+\sqrt2$ with $(1,-\sqrt2,1)$. Frequencies $\omega=\sqrt{\lambda k/m}=0.765,\allowbreak \ 1.414,\allowbreak \ 1.848$ in units of $\sqrt{k/m}$. Mode 1 is all masses in phase (the long-wavelength mode), mode 2 has the centre mass fixed, mode 3 alternates. The eigenvalues sum to the trace, $(2-\sqrt2)+2+(2+\sqrt2)=6$. ✓

Lecture 4. Quadratic forms and definiteness#

For a symmetric matrix $A$ the quadratic form is the scalar $q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}x_ix_j$. In the eigenbasis, with $\mathbf y=Q^T\mathbf x$, $$q=\sum_i\lambda_i\,y_i^2 .$$ So the sign of $q$ is controlled entirely by the eigenvalues:

TypeConditionMeaning for an energy $E=E_0+\tfrac12\mathbf x^TH\mathbf x$
Positive definiteall $\lambda_i>0$minimum: stable equilibrium
Positive semi-definiteall $\lambda_i\ge0$, some $=0$flat directions: zero modes (translations, rotations, soft modes)
Negative definiteall $\lambda_i<0$maximum
Indefinitemixed signssaddle point; one negative $\lambda$ = transition state

A practical test for positive definiteness without finding the eigenvalues: Sylvester’s criterion, that all leading principal minors are positive (for $2\times2$: $A_{11}>0$ and $\det A>0$). For a function $f$, the quadratic form of its Hessian at a stationary point is exactly the second-order Taylor term (MATH 102), so the eigenvalues of the Hessian are the curvatures along the principal axes of the potential energy surface. The level sets $q(\mathbf x)=1$ are ellipsoids (all $\lambda>0$) with semi-axes $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ along $\mathbf q_i$: stiff directions have short axes.

In materials science

A crystal is mechanically stable only if its elastic-constant matrix (in Voigt form) is positive definite: for a cubic crystal this means $C_{11}>|C_{12}|$, $C_{11}+2C_{12}>0$ and $C_{44}>0$ (the Born stability criteria). An atomic structure at a stationary point is a true minimum only if its Hessian has no negative eigenvalues apart from the $6$ zero modes of rigid translation and rotation (of a free cluster); a negative eigenvalue marks a saddle, i.e. an imaginary phonon frequency, signalling a structural instability that the structure can lower its energy by distorting along that eigenvector.

Worked example 4.2: the shape of an energy well

An energy is $E=\tfrac12\mathbf x^TH\mathbf x$ with $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (energy units). Describe the surface $E=\tfrac12$.

$H$ has eigenvalues $3$ and $1$ with eigenvectors $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$, so $E=\tfrac12\bigl(3y_1^2+y_2^2\bigr)$ in the rotated coordinates. $E=\tfrac12$ is an ellipse with semi-axes $1/\sqrt3=0.577$ along $(1,1)$ (the stiff direction) and $1$ along $(1,-1)$ (the soft direction). Both eigenvalues are positive, so $H$ is positive definite and the point is a stable minimum; the stiffness ratio $3:1$ is the condition number that will matter for optimisers in MATH 304.

Lecture 5. Coupled oscillators and normal modes#

Consider $n$ masses with positions $x_i$ (measured from equilibrium) connected by springs, with potential energy $U=\tfrac12\mathbf x^TK\mathbf x$ and kinetic energy $T=\tfrac12\sum m_i\dot x_i^2$. With equal masses $m$, Newton’s equations are $m\ddot{\mathbf x}=-K\mathbf x$, where $K$ (the Hessian of $U$, or force-constant matrix) is symmetric. Try $\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf v\,\allowbreak \cos(\omega t)$: then $-m\omega^2\mathbf v=-K\mathbf v$, i.e. $$K\mathbf v=m\omega^2\mathbf v .$$ This is an eigenproblem: each eigenvector of $K$ is a normal mode in which all atoms oscillate in phase at a single frequency, with $\omega_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i/m}$ where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues. Because $K$ is symmetric, the modes are orthogonal and independent: any motion is a superposition of them. (For unequal masses, rescale to $D_{ij}=K_{ij}/\sqrt{m_im_j}$, the dynamical matrix.) A positive-definite $K$ gives real frequencies; a negative eigenvalue gives $\omega^2<0$, i.e. exponential growth, an instability.

Worked example 5.1: two coupled masses

Two equal masses $m$ are joined to walls and to each other by three identical springs of stiffness $k$. Find the normal modes and frequencies.

With displacements $x_1,x_2$: $m\ddot x_1=-kx_1+k(x_2-x_1)=-2kx_1+kx_2$ and $m\ddot x_2=kx_1-2kx_2$. So $K=k\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvalues of $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$ are $1$ and $3$ with eigenvectors $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ (as in Example 1.1 with the sign of the off-diagonal flipped). Hence $$\omega_1=\sqrt{k/m}\ \ (\text{in-phase: both move together, the middle spring is not stretched}),\qquad\omega_2=\sqrt{3k/m}\ \ (\text{out-of-phase}).$$ This is the two-atom model of a lattice with a low-frequency “acoustic” mode and a higher-frequency “optical” mode; an $N$-atom chain gives $N$ modes forming the phonon dispersion (Problem 9).

Linear dynamical systems#

The same technique solves any linear system $\dot{\mathbf y}=A\mathbf y$. Expand $\mathbf y$ in the eigenvectors, $\mathbf y=\sum_ic_i(t)\mathbf v_i$, and the equations decouple: $\dot c_i=\lambda_ic_i$, so $$\mathbf y(t)=\sum_ic_i(0)\,e^{\lambda_it}\mathbf v_i=e^{At}\mathbf y(0),\qquad e^{At}=Pe^{\Lambda t}P^{-1}.$$ Solutions grow if some eigenvalue has $\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0$, decay if all have $\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ (stable), and oscillate when $\lambda$ is complex. Linearising a nonlinear system about a fixed point gives exactly this problem with $A$ the Jacobian (see MATH 203), so the eigenvalues of the Jacobian decide the stability of kinetic models and the stiffness of reaction networks (the ratio of the largest to the smallest $|\lambda|$).

Worked example 5.2: an oscillator as a first-order system

Solve $\ddot x=-4x$, $x(0)=1$, $\dot x(0)=0$ by writing $\mathbf y=(x,\dot x)$.

$\dot{\mathbf y}=A\mathbf y$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-4&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\operatorname{tr}A=0$, $\det A=4$, so $\lambda=\pm2i$. Eigenvectors $(1,\pm2i)$. Writing the real solution $x=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(c\,e^{2it}\bigr)$ with $x(0)=1$, $\dot x(0)=0$ gives $x=\cos2t$, $\dot x=-2\sin2t$: purely imaginary eigenvalues mean undamped oscillation at angular frequency $2$. Eigenvalues with a negative real part would add a decaying envelope.

Lecture 6. The singular value decomposition#

Non-symmetric and rectangular matrices (a data table of $m$ samples by $n$ features, say) have no eigen-decomposition in general, but every real matrix has a singular value decomposition.

Singular value decomposition (SVD)

Every $m\times n$ real matrix can be written $$A=U\Sigma V^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i\,\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^{T},$$ where $U$ ($m\times m$) and $V$ ($n\times n$) are orthogonal, $\Sigma$ is diagonal with the singular values $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r>0$ ($r=\operatorname{rank}A$), the columns of $V$ are the right singular vectors (eigenvectors of $A^TA$) and the columns of $U$ the left singular vectors (eigenvectors of $AA^T$); the $\sigma_i^2$ are the nonzero eigenvalues of both.

Geometrically: $A$ first rotates by $V^T$, then stretches along the axes by $\sigma_i$, then rotates by $U$; the image of the unit sphere is an ellipsoid with semi-axes $\sigma_i\mathbf u_i$. Consequences:

  • Condition number $\kappa(A)=\sigma_1/\sigma_r$, the quantity in MATH 103, measures how far the ellipsoid is from a sphere.
  • Best low-rank approximation (Eckart–Young theorem): the matrix of rank $k$ closest to $A$ (in the Frobenius or 2-norm) is $A_k=\sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^T$; the error is $\sigma_{k+1}$ (2-norm). Small singular values are noise or redundancy. This is image compression, descriptor reduction and regularisation.
  • Pseudoinverse $A^+=V\Sigma^{-1}U^T$ (inverting only the nonzero $\sigma_i$) gives the least-squares solution of $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ (MATH 303).

Principal component analysis#

Take $m$ samples of $n$ features, with each column of the data matrix $X$ ($m\times n$) shifted to zero mean. The covariance matrix is $C=\tfrac1{m-1}X^TX$ (symmetric, positive semi-definite). Its eigenvectors are the principal components, the orthogonal directions of decreasing variance, and the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is the variance along the $i$-th: the fraction of variance retained by the first $k$ components is $\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i/\sum_i\lambda_i$. With $X=U\Sigma V^T$, the principal components are the columns of $V$ and $\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2/(m-1)$, so PCA is computed directly from the SVD. In materials work, PCA reduces high-dimensional descriptors, finds collective variables in molecular dynamics (the dominant, large-amplitude motions of a protein or a defect) and visualises structure–property maps (Machine learning for materials).

Worked example 6.2: SVD of a shear

Find the singular values of $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and its condition number.

$A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ has trace $3$ and determinant $1$: $\lambda=\dfrac{3\pm\sqrt5}2=2.618,\allowbreak \,\allowbreak 0.382$. The singular values are the square roots, $1.618$ and $0.618$ (the golden ratio and its reciprocal), with product $1=|\det A|$. Condition number $1.618/0.618=2.618$. The unit circle is mapped to an ellipse with these semi-axes: a $100\%$ shear stretches by $62\%$ along one direction and squeezes by $38\%$ along the other, the finite-strain picture of MATH 302, Problem 6.12.

Lecture 7. Electronic structure as an eigenproblem#

The time-independent Schrödinger equation $\hat H\psi=E\psi$ is an eigenvalue equation for the Hamiltonian operator. Expanding the unknown wavefunction in a finite basis of known functions $\phi_\mu$ (plane waves, Gaussians or atomic orbitals), $\psi=\sum_\mu c_\mu\phi_\mu$, turns it into a matrix problem $$H\mathbf c=E\,S\,\mathbf c,\qquad H_{\mu\nu}=\langle\phi_\mu|\hat H|\phi_\nu\rangle,\quad S_{\mu\nu}=\langle\phi_\mu|\phi_\nu\rangle,$$ a generalised eigenproblem with overlap matrix $S$ (the identity for an orthonormal basis, e.g. plane waves). The eigenvalues $E_i$ are the energy levels, the eigenvectors the orbitals, and the spectral theorem guarantees real energies and orthogonal states. A generalised problem is reduced to an ordinary one using $S=Q_s\Lambda_sQ_s^T$ to form $S^{-1/2}=Q_s\Lambda_s^{-1/2}Q_s^T$, giving $\tilde H=S^{-1/2}HS^{-1/2}$ (this fails if $S$ is ill-conditioned because the basis is nearly dependent: the conditioning problem of MATH 103). Diagonalising $H$ at every iteration of the self-consistent loop dominates the cost of DFT, scaling as the cube of the matrix size, which is why large calculations use iterative methods that find only the lowest few eigenpairs.

In materials science: tight-binding chain

A chain of atoms with one orbital each, on-site energy $\varepsilon$ and hopping $-t$ between neighbours, has $H$ with $\varepsilon$ on the diagonal and $-t$ on the first off-diagonals. Its eigenvectors are plane waves $c_j=e^{ikja}$ with energies $E(k)=\varepsilon-2t\cos ka$: the energy band, with bandwidth $4t$. For a ring of $N$ atoms (periodic) the allowed $k=2\pi n/(Na)$ give the $N$ eigenvalues, a discrete preview of Bloch’s theorem and of the Fourier methods of MATH 205 (Problem 10).

Summary of the unit#

An eigenvector is an axis along which a matrix acts as a simple stretch; for the symmetric matrices of physics these axes are orthogonal and complete, so that in the right coordinates every coupled problem becomes independent one-dimensional ones. Eigenvalues are the stretch factors: frequencies$^2$ for vibrations, energies for electrons, curvature for stability, variance for data. The SVD extends the same picture to arbitrary matrices. Next we put the matrices to work on the physical quantities that are naturally tensors: stress, strain and the elastic stiffness of crystals.

Key takeaways
  • An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches, by the eigenvalue; eigenvalues are the roots of $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$, and the trace and determinant equal their sum and product.
  • A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors, so $A=Q\Lambda Q^{T}$: in the right axes it is a set of independent stretches. Hamiltonians, Hessians, stress tensors and covariance matrices are all symmetric.
  • A matrix power or function is easy in the eigenbasis: $A^{n}=Q\Lambda^{n}Q^{T}$ and $e^{At}=Qe^{\Lambda t}Q^{T}$, so a linear system of ODEs decouples into independent modes, and the signs of the eigenvalues decide stability.
  • A quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x}$ is positive for all $\mathbf{x}$ (positive definite) exactly when all eigenvalues are positive; this classifies minima, saddles and stable crystals, and a negative Hessian eigenvalue is an imaginary vibrational frequency.
  • The singular value decomposition $A=U\Sigma V^{T}$ exists for every matrix; truncating it gives the best low-rank approximation and underlies principal component analysis, the condition number and the pseudoinverse.

Problem 5.1Basic

Find the eigenvalues and normalised eigenvectors of $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$. Verify that the sum of eigenvalues equals the trace and their product the determinant. Is $A$ symmetric? Are the eigenvectors orthogonal?

Hint

Characteristic equation: $\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0$.

Full solution

$\operatorname{tr}A=7$, $\det A=12-2=10$, so $\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0\Rightarrow\lambda=2,\allowbreak 5$. Sum $=7$ ✓, product $=10$ ✓.

$\lambda=5$: $(A-5I)\mathbf v=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}\mathbf v=\mathbf0\Rightarrow v_2=v_1$, $\mathbf v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$. $\lambda=2$: $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf v=\mathbf0\Rightarrow v_2=-2v_1$, $\mathbf v=\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(1,-2)$.

$A$ is not symmetric, and the eigenvectors are not orthogonal: $(1,1)\cdot(1,-2)=-1\ne0$. Both eigenvalues are real and distinct, so $A$ is diagonalisable, but the eigenbasis is skewed. (Orthogonality of eigenvectors is a property of symmetric matrices.)

Problem 5.2Basic

The stress tensor at a point in a steel part, in MPa, is $\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}120&40&0\\40&60&0\\0&0&30\end{pmatrix}$. Find the principal stresses and the principal directions in the $xy$-plane, and the maximum shear stress $\tau_\mathrm{max}=\tfrac12(\sigma_1-\sigma_3)$ where $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\sigma_3$.

Hint

The $z$-direction decouples: $\sigma_{zz}=30$ is already an eigenvalue. Solve the $2\times2$ block.

Full solution

The block $\begin{pmatrix}120&40\\40&60\end{pmatrix}$ has trace $180$ and determinant $7200-1600=5600$, so $\lambda^2-180\lambda+5600=0$, $\lambda=90\pm\sqrt{8100-5600}=90\pm50$: $\lambda=140$ and $40$. With the decoupled $30$: the principal stresses are $\sigma_1=140$, $\sigma_2=40$, $\sigma_3=30$ MPa.

Direction for $140$: $(120-140)v_1+40v_2=0\Rightarrow v_2=\tfrac12v_1$, i.e. $\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(2,1)$, at $\arctan(1/2)=26.6^\circ$ to the $x$-axis. For $40$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(-1,2)$, perpendicular, as the spectral theorem requires. The third direction is $z$.

$\tau_\mathrm{max}=\tfrac12(140-30)=55$ MPa. Yielding criteria (Tresca: $\tau_\mathrm{max}=\sigma_Y/2$) use exactly this quantity, which is why materials engineers diagonalise the stress tensor.

Problem 5.3Core

Diagonalise $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ as $Q\Lambda Q^T$. Use it to compute (a) $A^{10}$, (b) $A^{-1}$, (c) $\sqrt A$ (the symmetric positive square root), and (d) $e^{A}$ exactly.

Hint

From Example 1.1: $\lambda=1,3$, $Q=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$ with columns $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$. Use $f(A)=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}f(3)+f(1)&f(3)-f(1)\\f(3)-f(1)&f(3)+f(1)\end{pmatrix}$.

Full solution

With projectors $P_3=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $P_1=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $f(A)=f(3)P_3+f(1)P_1=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}f(3)+f(1)&f(3)-f(1)\\f(3)-f(1)&f(3)+f(1)\end{pmatrix}$.

(a) $f=\lambda^{10}$: $3^{10}=59049$: $A^{10}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}59050&59048\\59048&59050\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}29525&29524\\29524&29525\end{pmatrix}$. (Direct multiplication nine times would be tedious; the largest eigenvalue dominates, $A^{10}\approx3^{10}P_3$.)

(b) $f=1/\lambda$: $\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac13+1&\tfrac13-1\\\tfrac13-1&\tfrac13+1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2/3&-1/3\\-1/3&2/3\end{pmatrix}$. Check: $A A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}4/3-1/3&-2/3+2/3\\\ldots\end{pmatrix}=I$. ✓

(c) $f=\sqrt\lambda$: $\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3+1&\sqrt3-1\\\sqrt3-1&\sqrt3+1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1.366&0.366\\0.366&1.366\end{pmatrix}$. Squaring gives $\begin{pmatrix}1.866+0.134&2(1.366)(0.366)\\\ldots\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. ✓

(d) $f=e^\lambda$: $e^A=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}e^3+e&e^3-e\\e^3-e&e^3+e\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}11.40&8.68\\8.68&11.40\end{pmatrix}$. Note $e^A\neq$ the matrix of entrywise exponentials: functions of matrices act on eigenvalues, not entries.

Problem 5.4Core

Classify the Hessian of $f(x,y,z)=x^2+2y^2+z^2+2xy+2yz$ at the origin, using (a) Sylvester’s criterion and (b) eigenvalues. What kind of stationary point is the origin? If instead the cross-term were $4xy$, what would change?

Hint

$f=\tfrac12\mathbf x^TH\mathbf x$ with $H_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_jf$: here $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&2&0\\2&4&2\\0&2&2\end{pmatrix}$.

Full solution

$H=\begin{pmatrix}2&2&0\\2&4&2\\0&2&2\end{pmatrix}$. Leading minors: $2>0$; $\begin{vmatrix}2&2\\2&4\end{vmatrix}=4>0$; $\det H=2(8-4)-2(4-0)+0=8-8=0$. The third minor is zero, so $H$ is only positive semi-definite, not positive definite.

Eigenvalues: the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^3-(\operatorname{tr}H)\lambda^2+(\text{sum of principal }2\times2\text{ minors})\lambda-\det H$. Here $\operatorname{tr}H=8$, the minors are $(2\cdot4-4)+(2\cdot2-0)+(4\cdot2-4)=4+4+4=12$, and $\det H=0$, so $\lambda(\lambda^2-8\lambda+12)=0$, giving $\lambda=0,\,2,\,6$.

The zero eigenvalue's eigenvector satisfies $2v_1+2v_2=0$, $2v_2+2v_3=0$: $\mathbf v\propto(1,-1,1)$. Along that line, $f=x^2+2x^2+x^2+2x(-x)+2(-x)(x)=4x^2-4x^2=0$: $f$ is flat along it, so the origin is a non-isolated minimum (a valley floor, $f\ge0$ and $f=0$ along a line). In an atomistic setting this is a zero mode: a sliding or rotation direction along which the energy does not change.

With a cross-term $4xy$, $H_{12}=4$ and the minor $\begin{vmatrix}2&4\\4&4\end{vmatrix}=-8<0$: indefinite, so the origin becomes a saddle point.

Problem 5.5Core

A cubic crystal has $C_{11}=108$, $C_{12}=62$ and $C_{44}=28$ GPa (aluminium). (a) Verify the Born stability conditions. (b) The $3\times3$ block of the Voigt matrix for normal strains is $\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}&C_{12}\\C_{12}&C_{11}&C_{12}\\C_{12}&C_{12}&C_{11}\end{pmatrix}$. Find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors; interpret them as bulk and shear (tetragonal) moduli. (c) What happens to stability if $C_{12}$ increased to $120$ GPa?

Hint

The matrix is $(C_{11}-C_{12})I+C_{12}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$ with $\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)$. Try $\mathbf v=(1,1,1)$ and any vector orthogonal to it.

Full solution

(a) $C_{11}>|C_{12}|$: $108>62$ ✓; $C_{11}+2C_{12}=232>0$ ✓; $C_{44}=28>0$ ✓. Stable.

(b) For $\mathbf v=(1,1,1)$: each row sums to $C_{11}+2C_{12}$, so the eigenvalue is $C_{11}+2C_{12}=232$ GPa: the hydrostatic (uniform dilation) direction, equal to $3B$ where $B=(C_{11}+2C_{12})/3=77.3$ GPa is the bulk modulus. For any $\mathbf v\perp(1,1,1)$ (a two-dimensional space, since $\mathbf1\mathbf1^T\mathbf v=0$), the eigenvalue is $C_{11}-C_{12}=46$ GPa, doubly degenerate: tetragonal shear strains that change shape at constant volume, with shear modulus $C'=(C_{11}-C_{12})/2=23$ GPa. So the eigenvalues $232,46,46$ are all positive and the matrix is positive definite. The degeneracy of $46$ is the consequence of cubic symmetry.

(c) With $C_{12}=120>C_{11}=108$: $C_{11}-C_{12}=-12<0$, a negative eigenvalue (twice): the crystal is unstable against tetragonal shear and would spontaneously distort. Indeed this is how martensitic transformations arise in alloys where $C'=(C_{11}-C_{12})/2$ softens towards zero on cooling (for example in Fe–Pd and Ni–Al alloys): the zero of an eigenvalue marks the transformation.

Problem 5.6Core

Two identical masses ($m=1.0\times10^{-25}$ kg) connect to fixed walls and to each other by three springs of stiffness $k=10$ N/m (Example 5.1). (a) Find the two normal-mode frequencies in THz ($\nu=\omega/2\pi$). (b) The system starts at rest with $x_1(0)=1$ pm, $x_2(0)=0$. Find $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ and describe the energy exchange (beats).

Hint

Write the initial state as a combination of the two mode vectors: $(1,0)=\tfrac12(1,1)+\tfrac12(1,-1)$.

Full solution

(a) $\omega_1=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{10/10^{-25}}=\sqrt{10^{26}}=10^{13}$ rad/s, i.e. $\nu_1=\omega_1/2\pi=1.59$ THz. $\omega_2=\sqrt3\,\allowbreak \omega_1=1.732\times10^{13}$ rad/s, $\nu_2=2.76$ THz.

(b) Initial conditions: $(x_1,x_2)=\tfrac12(1,1)+\tfrac12(1,-1)$ in units of $1$ pm, zero velocity, so each mode is excited with amplitude $\tfrac12$ pm and no phase: $x_1(t)=\tfrac12\bigl[\cos\omega_1t+\cos\omega_2t\bigr]$, $x_2(t)=\tfrac12\bigl[\cos\omega_1t-\cos\omega_2t\bigr]$ pm. Using sum-to-product, $x_1=\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\omega_1+\omega_2}2t\bigr)\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\omega_2-\omega_1}2t\bigr)$ and $x_2=\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\omega_1+\omega_2}2t\bigr)\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\omega_2-\omega_1}2t\bigr)$: fast oscillation at the mean frequency, modulated by a slow envelope at $\tfrac12(\omega_2-\omega_1)=3.66\times10^{12}$ rad/s. Energy sloshes back and forth between the two masses with period $2\pi/(\omega_2-\omega_1)=0.86$ ps: the first signs of heat conduction by phonons in a larger chain.

Problem 5.7Core

Solve $\dot x=-2x+y$, $\dot y=x-2y$ with $x(0)=1$, $y(0)=0$ by diagonalising. Then do the same for the reversible reaction network A $\rightleftharpoons$ B with rates $k_f=2$, $k_b=1$ (units s$^{-1}$): $\dot a=-k_fa+k_bb$, $\dot b=k_fa-k_bb$ with $a(0)=1$, $b(0)=0$. What is the long-time state and the relaxation time?

Hint

First system: $A=-2I+\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$; use the eigenvectors $(1,\pm1)$. Second: $A=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $0$ and $-3$.

Full solution

First system: $A=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvectors $(1,1)$ with $\lambda=-1$ and $(1,-1)$ with $\lambda=-3$. Initial state $(1,0)=\tfrac12(1,1)+\tfrac12(1,-1)$. So $x(t)=\tfrac12\bigl(e^{-t}+e^{-3t}\bigr)$, $y(t)=\tfrac12\bigl(e^{-t}-e^{-3t}\bigr)$. Both eigenvalues are negative: stable, everything decays to $0$.

Reaction network: $A=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $\operatorname{tr}A=-3$, $\det A=0$, so $\lambda=0,-3$. For $\lambda=0$: $-2a+b=0\Rightarrow(1,2)$. For $\lambda=-3$: $(-2+3)a+b=0\Rightarrow(1,-1)$. Initial $(1,0)=\tfrac13(1,2)+\tfrac23(1,-1)$. So $a(t)=\tfrac13+\tfrac23e^{-3t}$, $b(t)=\tfrac23-\tfrac23e^{-3t}$. At long times $a\to\tfrac13$, $b\to\tfrac23$, the equilibrium with $b/a=k_f/k_b=2$ (detailed balance), and the relaxation rate is $3=k_f+k_b$, so $\tau=1/3$ s. The zero eigenvalue is the conservation of total amount ($a+b=1$) and its eigenvector is the equilibrium state; the nonzero eigenvalue is the relaxation rate. This structure, a zero eigenvalue for equilibrium plus negative ones for relaxation, holds for any master equation.

Problem 5.8Core

(a) Find the SVD of $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{pmatrix}$ by computing the eigenvalues of $A^TA$. (b) What are the condition number and the semi-axes of the ellipse $A$ maps the unit circle to? (c) Give the best rank-1 approximation of $A$ and its error.

Hint

$A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $45$ and $5$.

Full solution

(a) $A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\0&5\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $25\pm20=45,5$ with eigenvectors $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$. Singular values $\sigma_1=\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt5=6.708$, $\sigma_2=\sqrt5=2.236$. Right singular vectors $\mathbf v_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $\mathbf v_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$. Left ones: $\mathbf u_i=A\mathbf v_i/\sigma_i$: $A\mathbf v_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(3,9)$, so $\mathbf u_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(3,9)/(3\sqrt5)=\tfrac1{\sqrt{10}}(1,3)$; $A\mathbf v_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(3,-1)$, so $\mathbf u_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(3,-1)/\sqrt5=\tfrac1{\sqrt{10}}(3,-1)$. Check: $\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2=\tfrac1{10}(3-3)=0$. ✓ Also $\sigma_1\sigma_2=\sqrt{225}=15=|\det A|$. ✓

(b) $\kappa=\sigma_1/\sigma_2=3$. The image of the unit circle is an ellipse with semi-axes $6.708$ and $2.236$ along $\mathbf u_1$ and $\mathbf u_2$ (area $\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2=15\pi$, i.e. $|\det A|$ times the circle’s area).

(c) $A_1=\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^T=3\sqrt5\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt{10}\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\end{pmatrix}(1\ \ 1)=\tfrac{3\sqrt5}{\sqrt{20}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\3&3\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac32\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\3&3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1.5&1.5\\4.5&4.5\end{pmatrix}$. The 2-norm error is $\sigma_2=2.236$ (Eckart–Young): no rank-1 matrix is closer to $A$.

Problem 5.9Challenge

A ring of $N$ identical atoms (mass $m$) joined by springs of stiffness $k$ has the force-constant matrix $K$ with $2k$ on the diagonal and $-k$ on the cyclic neighbours. (a) Show that $\mathbf v^{(q)}_j=e^{iqja}$ with $q=2\pi n/(Na)$ is an eigenvector, and find the eigenvalues. (b) Derive the phonon dispersion $\omega(q)=2\sqrt{k/m}\,\allowbreak \bigl|\sin(qa/2)\bigr|$ and its long-wavelength limit (sound velocity). (c) For $k=10$ N/m, $m=10^{-25}$ kg, $a=2.5$ Å find $\omega_\mathrm{max}$ and the sound velocity.

Hint

$(K\mathbf v)_j=2kv_j-kv_{j+1}-kv_{j-1}$. Substitute $v_j=e^{iqja}$ and factor out $e^{iqja}$.

Full solution

(a) $(K\mathbf v)_j=k\bigl[2-e^{iqa}-e^{-iqa}\bigr]e^{iqja}=k\bigl[2-2\cos qa\bigr]v_j=4k\sin^2(qa/2)\,\allowbreak v_j$. So every plane wave is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda(q)=4k\sin^2(qa/2)$. Periodicity $v_{j+N}=v_j$ requires $qNa=2\pi n$, giving $N$ distinct $q$ (values differing by $2\pi/a$ are equivalent): $N$ eigenvectors, the full set. (This is a circulant matrix, diagonalised by the discrete Fourier transform: MATH 205.)

(b) $\omega^2=\lambda/m=\dfrac{4k}m\sin^2(qa/2)$, so $\omega(q)=2\sqrt{k/m}\,|\sin(qa/2)|$. For small $q$, $\sin(qa/2)\approx qa/2$ and $\omega\approx a\sqrt{k/m}\,q=c\,q$: linear dispersion, with sound velocity $c=a\sqrt{k/m}$. The $n=0$ eigenvalue is $0$: the rigid translation mode.

(c) $\omega_\mathrm{max}=2\sqrt{k/m}=2\times10^{13}$ rad/s ($3.2$ THz) at $qa=\pi$ (the zone boundary, neighbours moving in opposite directions). $c=a\sqrt{k/m}=2.5\times10^{-10}\times10^{13}=2.5\times10^3$ m/s, typical of a solid. The bandwidth and slope of the dispersion are set by one number, the stiffness of the bond, tying the Hessian eigenvalues to the experimentally measured vibration spectrum.

Problem 5.10Challenge

Take a ring of $N=6$ atoms, each with one orbital, on-site energy $\varepsilon=0$ and hopping $-t$ between neighbours (the benzene $\pi$-system in Hückel theory). (a) Using the plane-wave eigenvectors, find the six energy levels and their degeneracies. (b) Fill the levels with $6$ electrons (two per orbital) and compute the total electronic energy in units of $t$. (c) Compare with three isolated double bonds (three ethene-like dimers, each with levels $\pm t$, filled with the two electrons at $-t$) to find the delocalisation (resonance) energy.

Hint

From the tight-binding formula $E(k)=\varepsilon-2t\cos(ka)$ with $ka=2\pi n/6$, $n=0,\ldots,5$.

Full solution

(a) $E_n=-2t\cos(2\pi n/6)$. $n=0$: $-2t$. $n=1,5$: $-2t\cos60^\circ=-t$. $n=2,4$: $-2t\cos120^\circ=+t$. $n=3$: $-2t\cos180^\circ=+2t$. Levels: $-2t$ (non-degenerate), $-t$ (twofold), $+t$ (twofold), $+2t$ (non-degenerate): sum $=0=\operatorname{tr}H$. ✓

(b) Six electrons fill the three lowest orbitals ($-2t,-t,-t$) with two each: $E=2(-2t)+2(-t)+2(-t)=-8t$.

(c) Three isolated double bonds: each dimer has $H=\begin{pmatrix}0&-t\\-t&0\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\mp t$ ($-t$ filled, two electrons), so $E=3\times2(-t)=-6t$. The ring is lower by $-8t-(-6t)=-2t$ in these units, a delocalisation (resonance) energy of $2|t|$; fitting $|t|$ to the measured thermochemical stabilisation of benzene gives a value of order $1$ eV, so the model has the right sign and structure but its single parameter must be fitted. The qualitative message, that spreading electrons over more atoms lowers the energy via a wider band, is the origin of metallic bonding and of aromatic stability.

Problem 5.11Core

Four samples of two correlated properties (after subtracting the means) are the rows of $X=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\1&1.5\\-1&-1.5\\-2&-2\end{pmatrix}$ (for example a lattice parameter and a bond length measured on four related structures). (a) Compute the covariance matrix $C=X^TX/(m-1)$. (b) Find its eigenvalues and the first principal component. Problem 5.12Challenge

A system obeys the kinetic Monte Carlo master equation $\dot{\mathbf p}=W\mathbf p$ for the occupation probabilities of three states $1\rightleftharpoons2\rightleftharpoons3$ (a particle hopping on a three-site chain), with rate $w$ between neighbouring sites: $W=w\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\1&-2&1\\0&1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $W/w$. (b) Starting from $\mathbf p(0)=(1,0,0)$, find $\mathbf p(t)$. (c) What is the equilibrium, and the slowest relaxation time?

Hint

Try $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,0,-1)$; the third eigenvector is orthogonal to both.

Full solution

(a) $M=W/w$ is symmetric. $M(1,1,1)^T=0$, so $\lambda_0=0$. $M(1,0,-1)^T=(-1,\ 1-1,\ 1)=(-1,0,1)=-(1,0,-1)$, so $\lambda_1=-1$. Third: orthogonal to both, $(1,-2,1)$: $M(1,-2,1)^T=(-1-2,\ 1+4+1,\ -2-1)=(-3,6,-3)=-3(1,-2,1)$, so $\lambda_2=-3$. Eigenvalues $0,-1,-3$ (trace $-4$ ✓).

(b) Normalised: $\mathbf e_0=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)$, $\mathbf e_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,0,-1)$, $\mathbf e_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt6}(1,-2,1)$. $\mathbf p(0)=(1,0,0)$ has coefficients $\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf p(0)=\tfrac1{\sqrt3},\allowbreak \tfrac1{\sqrt2},\allowbreak \tfrac1{\sqrt6}$. So $\mathbf p(t)=\tfrac13(1,1,1)+\tfrac12(1,0,-1)e^{-wt}+\tfrac16(1,-2,1)e^{-3wt}$. Check at $t=0$: first component $\tfrac13+\tfrac12+\tfrac16=1$ ✓, second $\tfrac13-\tfrac13=0$ ✓, third $\tfrac13-\tfrac12+\tfrac16=0$ ✓.

(c) Equilibrium: $\mathbf p_\infty=\tfrac13(1,1,1)$ (uniform, because all rates are equal: detailed balance). The slowest decaying mode has $\lambda=-1$, so $\tau=1/w$: the time to spread across the chain is of order one hop time times the number of sites squared (compare the diffusive $L^2/D$). The total probability $\sum p_i=1$ is conserved by the zero eigenvalue.

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Eigenvalues

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Solution

$\lambda^2-2\lambda-3=0$, so $\lambda=3$ with $(1,1)$ and $\lambda=-1$ with $(1,-1)$ (orthogonal, as the matrix is symmetric; the sum $2$ is the trace and the product $-3$ the determinant).

Quiz 2Functions of a matrix

$A$ has eigenvalues $2$ and $3$. Give the eigenvalues of $A^2$, $A^{-1}$ and $A+5I$.

Solution

$4,9$; $\tfrac12,\tfrac13$; $7,8$. Functions of a matrix act on its eigenvalues with the same eigenvectors.

Quiz 3Definiteness

Classify $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Solution

First: leading minors $2>0$ and $\det=3>0$: positive definite (eigenvalues $1,3$). Second: $\det=-3<0$: indefinite (eigenvalues $3,-1$), a saddle.

Quiz 4Normal modes

Three equal masses between walls joined by identical springs: give the three frequencies in units of $\sqrt{k/m}$.

Solution

$\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}=0.765$, $\sqrt2=1.414$, $\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}=1.848$ (Example 3.2).

Quiz 5SVD

Find the singular values of $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Solution

$\sqrt{(3\pm\sqrt5)/2}=1.618,\ 0.618$; condition number $2.618$.

Quiz 6PCA

A covariance matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Find the principal components and the variance fractions.

Solution

Trace $5$, determinant $0$: eigenvalues $5$ and $0$. The first component is $(2,1)/\sqrt5$ and carries $100\%$ of the variance: the two variables are perfectly correlated and the data lie on a line.
