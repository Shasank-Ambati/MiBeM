Materials science essentials

Simulation models materials, so it helps to be fluent in what they are and how they are described. This page is a compact refresher of the core ideas (bonding, structure, defects, phase transformations, mechanical behaviour, processing and the experiments used to test materials), always pointing to where a simulation method connects.

Starting point: This page is a refresher at first-year university level.

1. The framework#

Materials science is organised around one chain: processing (how a material is made and treated) determines its structure (arrangement of atoms, defects, grains and phases at every scale); structure determines properties (strength, conductivity, corrosion resistance and so on); and properties determine performance in service. A steel sheet is a good illustration: composition and the thermomechanical schedule (rolling temperature, cooling rate, coiling temperature) set the mix of ferrite, bainite and martensite; that microstructure sets the yield strength and formability; and those set how the part forms and how it absorbs energy in a crash.

Link in the chainTypical questionSimulation that addresses it
Processing → structureWhich phases and grain size result from this schedule?CALPHAD, diffusion models, phase-field, process finite elements
Structure → propertiesHow strong is this microstructure? How does a defect move?Crystal plasticity, molecular dynamics, DFT
Properties → performanceWill the component survive the load, heat or environment?Continuum finite elements
All links, in reverseWhich composition and process would give this property?Machine learning and optimisation

2. Bonding#

Everything starts with how atoms hold together. The bond type largely decides the properties and, importantly for simulation, which interatomic model or electronic method is appropriate.

BondNatureExamples and cohesive energyTypical consequencesSuitable models
MetallicValence electrons delocalised over all atoms, non-directionalCu 3.5, Fe 4.3, W 8.9 eV/atomDuctile, conducting, usually close-packed or body-centred cubic structuresEmbedded-atom potentials; DFT
CovalentElectrons shared in directional bondsSi 4.6, diamond 7.4 eV/atomHard, brittle, open structures, semiconductingTersoff and Stillinger–Weber potentials; DFT
IonicElectron transfer; electrostatic attraction between ionsNaCl, MgO, Al2O3 (lattice energy of NaCl about 8 eV per ion pair)Hard, brittle, insulating, high melting pointsCoulomb plus short-range potentials; DFT+U
van der WaalsWeak attraction from fluctuating dipolesNoble gases, graphite interlayers, polymer chain packing; 0.01–0.1 eVLow melting points, soft, layered slidingLennard-Jones; dispersion-corrected DFT
Hydrogen bondAttraction between H and an electronegative atomWater, proteins, polyamides; 0.1–0.4 eVStructure of water and many polymersSpecialised force fields

All bonds share one shape of energy curve: repulsion at short distance, attraction at long, and a minimum at the equilibrium spacing r0 with depth equal to the bond energy. The curvature at the minimum is the bond stiffness k, so macroscopic stiffness follows from it: Young’s modulus is roughly E ≈ k/r0, giving about 80 GPa for k = 20 N/m and r0 = 0.25 nm, the right order for metals. The asymmetry of the curve (it is steeper on the repulsive side) is the origin of thermal expansion, and deeper wells mean higher melting points and stiffer solids.

3. Structure at many scales#

Properties depend on structure at every scale from the atomic to the component, so simulations are matched to the scale that controls the property.

ScaleFeaturesTypical size
AtomicCrystal lattice or amorphous arrangement; point defects; dislocation cores0.1–2 nm
NanoPrecipitates, clusters, solute atmospheres, thin films1–100 nm
MicroGrains, phases, inclusions, pores, dislocation networks0.1–100 µm (steel sheet grains: 5–20 µm)
MacroComponent, weld, casting, residual-stress fieldmm–m

Most engineering materials are polycrystals: aggregates of crystalline grains with different orientations separated by grain boundaries. The distribution of grain orientations is the texture, which makes properties direction-dependent (anisotropy): rolled sheet forms differently along and across the rolling direction. Other classes differ in structure. Glasses (and metallic glasses) lack long-range order but keep a regular short-range arrangement; polymers are long chain molecules, with crystalline and amorphous regions and a glass transition temperature; ceramics are mostly ionic or covalent compounds, usually made by sintering powders into polycrystals; composites combine phases (fibres in a matrix) to get properties neither has alone.

A central empirical result links scales: the yield strength of a polycrystal rises as the grain size d falls, as described by the Hall–Petch relation:

σy = σ0 + k d−1/2

where σ0 is the friction stress for dislocation motion and k measures how hard it is to pass slip across a boundary. For steels k is of order 0.5–0.7 MPa m1/2.

4. Defects and strengthening#

A perfect crystal is a poor model of a real material: the useful properties of metals come largely from their defects.

  • Point defects (vacancies, interstitials, substitutional solutes) exist in equilibrium at a concentration that rises exponentially with temperature (see crystal structures); vacancies are what lets substitutional atoms diffuse.
  • Dislocations are line defects, characterised by the Burgers vector b (the magnitude and direction of the lattice distortion), with edge, screw and mixed character. Plastic deformation is the glide of dislocations on close-packed planes under shear stress, at stresses far below the theoretical shear strength of a perfect crystal. The dislocation density ρ (line length per volume) is about 1010–1012 m−2 in annealed metals and 1014–1016 m−2 in heavily deformed ones.
  • Planar defects: grain boundaries, stacking faults, twin boundaries, phase boundaries and free surfaces.
  • Volume defects: pores, inclusions and second-phase particles.

Strengthening a metal means hindering dislocation motion. The main mechanisms are summarised below.

MechanismHow it worksScaling
Solid-solution strengtheningSolute atoms distort the lattice and interact with dislocationsIncreases with solute concentration (roughly as a power of 1/2 to 2/3)
Grain-boundary strengtheningBoundaries block slipHall–Petch: ∝ d−1/2
Work (strain) hardeningDislocations multiply and obstruct each otherTaylor: Δσ = αMGb√ρ, with α ≈ 0.2–0.5 and Taylor factor M ≈ 3
Precipitation (dispersion) strengtheningFine particles are cut by or bowed around by dislocationsOrowan bowing: Δτ ≈ Gb/L, with L the particle spacing
Transformation strengtheningHard, fine product phases such as martensite or bainiteDepends on carbon content and transformation temperature

Here G is the shear modulus. These mechanisms combine, which is the basis of alloy design, and each ties to a modelling scale: solute–dislocation binding by DFT, dislocation mobility by molecular dynamics, precipitate evolution by phase-field and grain-level plasticity by crystal plasticity.

5. Phases and transformations#

A phase is a region of uniform composition and structure. The equilibrium phases for a given composition and temperature are what a phase diagram shows (the calculation is in thermodynamics and CALPHAD), but real processing is rarely at equilibrium, so which microstructure forms depends on kinetics. The main transformation types are:

  • Nucleation and growth (diffusional). A new phase forms as small nuclei that need to overcome an energy barrier, then grows by atoms moving across the interface; the rate is set by diffusion and by the driving force. Examples: precipitation, pearlite, ferrite growth from austenite.
  • Diffusionless (displacive). The lattice changes by a coordinated shear faster than atoms can diffuse, so composition is unchanged. The classic example is martensite in steel, formed on rapid cooling, which is hard because carbon remains trapped.
  • Spinodal decomposition. A solution inside the spinodal separates continuously, with no barrier (see phase-field modelling).
  • Order–disorder and magnetic transitions. Changes in the arrangement of species or spins with no change of lattice.

Because outcomes depend on the cooling path, engineers use time–temperature–transformation (TTT) and continuous-cooling transformation (CCT) diagrams, which show how long a transformation takes to start and finish at each temperature or cooling rate; a critical cooling rate must be exceeded to produce martensite. The overall kinetics are often described by the Johnson–Mehl–Avrami–Kolmogorov equation (see diffusion and kinetics). Heat treatment exploits all of this: annealing softens by recovery, recrystallisation and grain growth; normalising refines grains; quenching traps non-equilibrium phases; tempering relaxes quenched structures by precipitating carbides; ageing forms fine precipitates, as in the Al–Cu system, where the sequence of Guinier–Preston zones and metastable precipitates gives peak strength before the equilibrium phase coarsens.

6. Mechanical behaviour#

Stress and strain#

Engineering stress is σ = F/A0 (force over the original area) and engineering strain ε = ΔL/L0. When strains are large, true quantities account for the changing size: σtrue = σ(1 + ε) and εtrue = ln(1 + ε), valid until necking. A tensile test produces the curve from which most properties are read.

  • Elastic region. Reversible; σ = Eε (Hooke’s law). The Poisson ratio ν = −εtransverse/εaxial (about 0.3 for metals) links the moduli: G = E/[2(1 + ν)] and K = E/[3(1 − 2ν)].
  • Yield and plasticity. Permanent deformation starts at the yield strength. Afterwards the flow stress rises by strain hardening, often described by σ = Kεn (Hollomon), up to the ultimate tensile strength, where necking begins (Considère criterion: at a true strain equal to n for a power law). Ductility is the strain to fracture.
  • Hardness, resistance to local indentation, is roughly three times the yield strength for most metals.

Fracture, fatigue and creep#

  • Fracture. Brittle fracture occurs when a crack grows at stresses below yield; the Griffith/Irwin result is σf = KIc/(Y√(πa)) for a crack of length a, with fracture toughness KIc a material property and Y a geometry factor. BCC metals show a ductile–brittle transition with temperature.
  • Fatigue. Failure under repeated loading at stresses well below yield; described by S–N curves and the Paris law for crack growth, da/dN = C(ΔK)m.
  • Creep. Slow deformation under constant load at high temperature, controlled by diffusion, with steady-state rate ε̇ = Aσnexp(−Q/RT).

7. Other properties#

  • Electrical. Set by the band structure (see physics for simulation): metals conduct (copper resistivity about 1.7 × 10−8 Ω·m), semiconductors have a gap of order 1 eV that doping controls, insulators have gaps above about 3 eV.
  • Thermal. Heat is carried by electrons and phonons: conductivity is about 400 W/(m·K) for copper, 235 for aluminium, 50 for typical steels and above 1000 for diamond. Thermal expansion coefficients are about 12 × 10−6 K−1 for steel, 23 × 10−6 for aluminium and about 1.2 × 10−6 for Invar, an iron–nickel alloy whose magnetism cancels the expansion.
  • Magnetic. Ferromagnets (Fe, Co, Ni) have aligned moments below their Curie temperatures; soft magnets are easy to magnetise and used in transformers, hard magnets resist demagnetisation.
  • Corrosion and oxidation. Electrochemical or chemical reactions with the environment, governed by thermodynamic stability (Pourbaix and Ellingham diagrams) and by the kinetics of transport through any surface film.

Key takeaways
  • Processing sets structure, structure sets properties, and properties set performance; simulation can enter at every link.
  • Bonding type (metallic, covalent, ionic, van der Waals) decides which interatomic model or electronic method is appropriate.
  • Defects control most useful properties: dislocations govern strength, vacancies govern diffusion, and boundaries govern grain-size effects.
  • Phase diagrams give the equilibrium; transformation kinetics (nucleation, growth, diffusion) decide which microstructure actually forms.
  • Know what each experiment measures (diffraction, microscopy, tensile tests, calorimetry), because that is what a simulation must be compared with.

10. Check your understanding#

Why are metals usually ductile and covalent solids such as silicon brittle at room temperature?

Metallic bonding is non-directional, so atoms can slip past each other and dislocations glide easily. Covalent bonds are directional, so moving a dislocation requires breaking strong bonds and the material cracks first.

A steel obeys Hall–Petch with k = 0.6 MPa m1/2. How much stronger does refining the grain size from 20 µm to 5 µm make it?

Δσ = k(5 × 10−6)−1/2 − k(20 × 10−6)−1/2 = 0.6 × (447 − 224) ≈ 134 MPa, roughly 130 MPa stronger.

Which simulation method would you use first to estimate the driving force for precipitating a carbide at 500 °C, and why?

CALPHAD: it gives the Gibbs energy change for forming the phase from the current matrix composition at that temperature, which is the thermodynamic input to nucleation and growth models, at a tiny cost compared with atomistic methods.
