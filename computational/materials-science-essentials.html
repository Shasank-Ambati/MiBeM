Materials science essentials

Why is copper ductile and glass brittle, steel strong after quenching but weak after annealing, silicon a semiconductor and diamond an insulator? The answer in every case lies in the structure of the material at one or more length scales, and the way it was processed. This unit introduces the structure–property relations that everything else in the curriculum builds on: the simulation courses compute the properties of structures, and the structures you will build in the labs are the ones described here.

Lecture 1. Materials, bonding and the bond energy curve#

Engineering materials fall into four classes: metals (metallic bonding; strong, ductile, conduct heat and electricity), ceramics (ionic and covalent bonding; hard, brittle, high melting point, usually insulating), polymers (long molecules with covalent bonds along the chain and weak secondary bonds between chains; light, flexible) and composites (combinations of the above, such as carbon fibre in epoxy). Semiconductors, biomaterials and glasses cut across this classification. The central idea of the field is the processing–structure–properties–performance chain: how a material is made determines its structure at all scales, the structure determines its properties, and the properties determine whether it performs in service. Changing a property means changing the structure, usually by changing the processing.

The atoms of a solid are held together by bonds, which come in four principal types:

All bonds have the same qualitative energy–distance curve. Two atoms at distance $r$ attract each other at long range and repel at short range (the electron clouds overlap, the Pauli principle), so the energy $E(r)$ has a minimum at an equilibrium separation $r_0$ with depth $E_0$ (the bond energy). A simple model that you will use in simulations is the Lennard-Jones potential $E(r)=4\varepsilon[(\sigma/r)^{12}-(\sigma/r)^6]$, with $r_0=2^{1/6}\sigma$ and $E(r_0)=-\varepsilon$.

What the shape of the curve tells you

Stiffness. Near the minimum the curve is a parabola, $E\approx E_0+\tfrac12k(r-r_0)^2$, with the bond stiffness $k=\mathrm d^2E/\mathrm dr^2|_{r_0}$ (N/m). Stretching a crystal by a strain $\varepsilon$ separates every pair of planes by $\varepsilon r_0$, and the force per bond is $k\varepsilon r_0$ over an area $\approx r_0^2$ per bond, so the stress is $\sigma\approx k\varepsilon/r_0$ and the Young modulus is $E\approx k/r_0$: a deep, narrow well (strong bonds) means a high modulus. Melting. The melting temperature scales with the bond energy $E_0$, since melting happens when the thermal energy $k_\mathrm BT$ becomes a fixed fraction of it. Thermal expansion. The curve is asymmetric (steeper on the compression side), so as the atoms vibrate with a larger amplitude at higher $T$ their mean separation increases: the expansion is a consequence of the anharmonicity of the well, and stronger bonds, with deeper and more symmetric wells, expand less. A harmonic crystal would not expand at all.

Worked example 1.1: the stiffness of solid argon

Solid argon is bound by van der Waals forces, well described by a Lennard-Jones potential with $\varepsilon=0.0104$ eV and $\sigma=3.405$ Å. Find the equilibrium separation, the bond stiffness and an estimate of the Young modulus. For the potential $V(r)=4\varepsilon[(\sigma/r)^{12}-(\sigma/r)^6]$ the second derivative at the minimum is $57.15\,\varepsilon/\sigma^2$.

$r_0=2^{1/6}\sigma=1.1225\times3.405=3.822$ Å ($0.382$ nm; the measured nearest-neighbour distance in solid argon is $3.76$ Å, which differs because the many-neighbour sums shift the minimum). The stiffness is $k=57.15\,\varepsilon/\sigma^2=57.15\times1.666\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J/(3.405\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm m)^2=0.821$ N/m. Hence $E\approx k/r_0=0.821/3.822\times10^{-10}=2.1$ GPa. The measured isothermal bulk modulus of solid argon near $0$ K is about $3$ GPa: the simple estimate is correct in order of magnitude and in the conclusion that a van der Waals solid is very soft, a hundred times softer than a metal (copper: $130$ GPa) and $500$ times softer than diamond ($1050$ GPa). The melting point of argon, $84$ K, reflects the same weak bonds (depth $0.0104$ eV against $3.5$ eV for the cohesive energy of copper).

The bonding type also fixes what a crystal does when deformed. Metallic bonding is non-directional: planes of atoms can slide over one another without breaking the bonding (the electron sea adapts), which makes metals ductile. Ionic crystals resist sliding that would bring ions of like charge together, and covalent crystals have directional bonds that break rather than bend: both are brittle at room temperature. Polymers show the greatest variety because their long chains can uncoil: the deformation of a rubber is mostly a change of entropy of the chains, not of bond lengths (MSE 302).

Lecture 2. Crystal structures and their geometry#

In a crystal the atoms are arranged in a pattern that repeats in three dimensions. The pattern is described by a lattice (the set of translation vectors) and a basis (the atoms associated with each lattice point). The smallest repeating volume is the primitive cell; in practice one uses the larger conventional (unit) cell that shows the full symmetry, defined by the lattice parameters $a$, $b$, $c$ and the angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$. For cubic crystals only $a$ is needed. Three structures account for the great majority of metals:

fcc and hcp are the two ways of stacking close-packed layers of spheres: the layers are stacked $ABABAB\ldots$ (hcp) or $ABCABC\ldots$ (fcc); both have the maximum packing fraction $0.74$ for equal spheres and coordination $12$. The open diamond structure of silicon has only four neighbours, set by the directional $sp^3$ covalent bonds. The atoms per cell are counted by sharing: a corner atom belongs to $8$ cells, a face atom to $2$, an edge atom to $4$, an interior atom to $1$; the fcc cell has $8\times\tfrac18+6\times\tfrac12=4$ atoms.

Density from the structure

$\rho=\dfrac{nA}{V_cN_\mathrm A}$, where $n$ is the number of atoms in the cell, $A$ the atomic mass (g/mol), $V_c$ the cell volume and $N_\mathrm A=6.022\times10^{23}$ mol$^{-1}$. Checking a density against the measured one is the quickest test that a structure and a lattice parameter are consistent (LAB 201 does this for eight crystals).

Worked example 2.1: densities of copper, iron and tungsten

Compute the densities of copper (fcc, $a=0.3615$ nm, $A=63.55$), $\alpha$-iron (bcc, $a=0.2866$ nm, $A=55.85$) and tungsten (bcc, $a=0.3165$ nm, $A=183.84$) and compare with the measured $8.96$, $7.87$ and $19.25$ g/cm$^3$.

Copper: $\rho=4(63.55)/[(0.3615\times10^{-7})^3(6.022\times10^{23})]=8.93$ g/cm$^3$. Iron: $2(55.85)/[(0.2866\times10^{-7})^3N_\mathrm A]=7.88$ g/cm$^3$. Tungsten: $2(183.84)/[(0.3165\times10^{-7})^3N_\mathrm A]=19.26$ g/cm$^3$. All three are within $0.2\%$ of the measured values (the small differences come from vacancies, impurities and the temperature of the lattice parameter). Note why W is so much denser than Fe despite the same structure: the cell is a little larger but the atomic mass is $3.3$ times larger.

Directions and planes: Miller indices

To specify directions and planes in a crystal one uses the lattice axes. A direction is written $[uvw]$, the smallest integers proportional to the components of a vector along the axes: $[100]$ is the cube edge, $[110]$ the face diagonal and $[111]$ the body diagonal. A plane is described by Miller indices $(hkl)$: take the intercepts of the plane with the axes in units of the lattice parameters ($x_1,x_2,x_3$, with $\infty$ for parallel axes), take reciprocals and clear fractions to the smallest integers: a plane with intercepts $1,\infty,\infty$ is $(100)$; with $1,1,\infty$ it is $(110)$; with $1,1,1$ it is $(111)$; with $\tfrac12,1,\infty$ it is $(210)$. A bar denotes a negative index, $(\bar110)$. Families of equivalent planes by symmetry are denoted $\{hkl\}$ and directions $\langle uvw\rangle$. In cubic crystals the direction $[hkl]$ is perpendicular to the plane $(hkl)$, and the spacing of planes is $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$ (valid for cubic lattices; with the structure-factor rules of the fcc and bcc lattices that remove some reflections, this is the basis for the X-ray diffraction of LAB 201).

The packing of atoms differs between planes and directions, and this controls how crystals deform. In fcc the close-packed planes are $\{111\}$ and the close-packed directions are $\langle110\rangle$; slip occurs on these planes and in these directions (twelve slip systems, which makes fcc metals very ductile). In bcc there is no close-packed plane (the $\{110\}$ planes are the most densely packed), so the slip systems are more numerous but harder to activate, and bcc metals have a ductile–brittle transition at low temperature (MSE 302). Hcp has one close-packed plane (the basal plane) with three directions, so it is much less ductile at room temperature unless it is deformed by twinning (Mg, Ti, Zn).

Worked example 2.2: planar and linear densities in copper

For fcc copper ($a=0.3615$ nm) compute the planar density (atoms per nm$^2$) of the $(100)$, $(110)$ and $(111)$ planes and the linear density along $[110]$ and $[100]$.

The $(100)$ face of the cell contains $4\times\tfrac14+1=2$ atoms in an area $a^2=0.1307$ nm$^2$: $15.3$ atoms/nm$^2$. The $(110)$ plane is a rectangle $a\times\sqrt2a$ (area $0.1848$ nm$^2$) holding $4\times\tfrac14+2\times\tfrac12=2$ atoms: $10.8$ atoms/nm$^2$. The $(111)$ plane is an equilateral triangle of side $\sqrt2a$ with area $\tfrac{\sqrt3}2a^2=0.1132$ nm$^2$ containing $3\times\tfrac16+3\times\tfrac12=2$ atoms: $17.7$ atoms/nm$^2$, the densest of the three (the close-packed plane). Along $[110]$ the atoms touch and the repeat distance is $a/\sqrt2=0.2556$ nm with one atom per repeat: $3.91$ atoms/nm; along $[100]$ the repeat is $a=0.3615$ nm: $2.77$ atoms/nm, $29\%$ lower. The $\langle110\rangle$ directions are the directions of closest packing in fcc, and they are the slip directions.

Lecture 3. Imperfections: vacancies, dislocations and grain boundaries#

A perfect crystal would be far stronger and far less useful than a real one: most properties that matter in engineering (strength, ductility, conductivity of doped semiconductors, diffusion) are controlled by defects. They are classified by dimension.

  • Point defects (0D): vacancies (a missing atom), self-interstitials (an extra atom between lattice sites), and impurities that are substitutional (replacing a host atom: Zn in Cu, giving brass) or interstitial (small atoms in the holes of the lattice: C in Fe, giving steel). Vacancies exist in thermal equilibrium: from Lecture 4 of PHYS 301, the fraction is $n/N=\exp(-E_f/k_\mathrm BT)$ (times a factor of order 1–10 for the formation entropy). For copper, $E_f=1.28$ eV, the fraction is $3\times10^{-22}$ at $300$ K and $1.1\times10^{-5}$ at $1300$ K, just below melting.
  • Line defects (1D): dislocations. An edge dislocation is the edge of an extra half-plane of atoms inserted in the crystal; a screw dislocation is a line around which the planes form a helical ramp. Their strength is the Burgers vector $\mathbf b$, the lattice vector that closes a loop drawn around the dislocation (in a perfect crystal the same loop closes); $|\mathbf b|$ is the shortest lattice repeat, $a/\sqrt2=0.256$ nm for fcc copper along $\langle110\rangle$. For an edge dislocation $\mathbf b$ is perpendicular to the line, for a screw it is parallel. The dislocation density $\rho$ is the line length per volume (m/m$^3$ $=$ m$^{-2}$): $10^{10}$–$10^{12}$ m$^{-2}$ in annealed metals, $10^{14}$–$10^{16}$ m$^{-2}$ in heavily cold-worked ones, $\lesssim10^{3}$ m$^{-2}$ in silicon wafers.
  • Planar defects (2D): grain boundaries (the interface between two crystals of different orientation), stacking faults, twin boundaries and free surfaces. A polycrystal is an aggregate of grains; the mean grain size $d$ is measured by line-intercept methods and reported also as the ASTM grain-size number $G$, defined by $n=2^{G-1}$ grains per square inch on a micrograph at $100\times$ (a larger $G$ means finer grains: $G=5$ gives $n=16$, mean grain area $0.0040$ mm$^2$ and a diameter of about $64$ µm).
  • Volume defects (3D): pores, cracks, precipitates and inclusions.

Dislocations and plastic deformation

Plastic flow is carried by dislocation glide, not by the sliding of whole planes. To slip a plane rigidly over its neighbour needs a shear stress of order $G/10$ (the theoretical strength, several GPa for metals: the $6$ GPa found for copper in the tensile test of LAB 503 is of this kind), but a dislocation moves by shifting one row of atoms at a time, which needs a stress $10^3$–$10^4$ times smaller. The glide takes place on the close-packed plane in the close-packed direction (the slip system); for fcc, $\{111\}\langle110\rangle$: $4$ planes $\times$ $3$ directions $=12$ systems. The dislocation density rises with deformation (dislocations multiply at Frank–Read sources) and the dislocations obstruct each other: this is work hardening, described by the Taylor relation $$\Delta\sigma_y=\alpha Gb\sqrt\rho,\qquad\alpha\approx0.2\text{–}0.5,$$ with $G$ the shear modulus ($48$ GPa for copper). For copper ($b=0.256$ nm, $\alpha=0.3$): $\rho=10^{12}$ m$^{-2}$ gives $3.7$ MPa, $10^{14}$ gives $37$ MPa and $10^{15}$ gives $116$ MPa; the yield strength of annealed copper is about $30$ MPa and of heavily cold-worked copper above $300$ MPa, the remainder coming from the other mechanisms of Lecture 5.

Grain boundaries and the Hall–Petch relation

A grain boundary disrupts the slip planes: a dislocation cannot continue into a neighbouring grain of different orientation and piles up at the boundary. The finer the grains, the more boundaries per length and the higher the stress needed to push dislocations through: the yield stress rises as $$\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_y\,d^{-1/2}\qquad(\text{Hall–Petch}),$$ where $\sigma_0$ is the friction stress of the single crystal and $k_y$ a constant of the material. Grain refinement is the only strengthening mechanism that also raises toughness. The relation is limited at very small grain sizes ($d\lesssim10$ nm), where the boundaries themselves slide and the strength decreases (the “inverse Hall–Petch” regime of nanocrystalline metals).

Worked example 3.1: Hall–Petch from two measurements

A steel has a yield strength of $180$ MPa for a mean grain size of $100$ µm and $250$ MPa for $25$ µm. Find $\sigma_0$ and $k_y$ and predict the yield strength at $5$ µm.

Express the grain size in metres: $d^{-1/2}=(10^{-4})^{-1/2}=100$ m$^{-1/2}$ and $(25\times10^{-6})^{-1/2}=200$ m$^{-1/2}$. The slope is $k_y=(250-180)/(200-100)=0.70$ MPa m$^{1/2}$, and the intercept is $\sigma_0=180-0.70\times100=110$ MPa. At $d=5$ µm, $d^{-1/2}=447$ m$^{-1/2}$ and $\sigma_y=110+0.70\times447=423$ MPa: a twenty-fold reduction of the grain size from $100$ to $5$ µm raises the strength by a factor $2.3$. (The values of $k_y$ in real metals range from $\approx0.1$ MPa m$^{1/2}$ for copper to $\approx0.6$–$0.8$ for steels.)

Worked example 3.2: how much of the strength is work hardening?

Annealed copper has $\rho=10^{11}$ m$^{-2}$. After rolling, $\rho=3\times10^{14}$ m$^{-2}$. Estimate the increase of yield strength from the Taylor relation ($\alpha=0.3$, $G=48$ GPa, $b=0.256$ nm).

$\Delta\sigma=\alpha Gb\sqrt\rho$ with $\alpha Gb=0.3\times48\times10^9\times0.256\times10^{-9}=3.69$ N/m. Before: $3.69\sqrt{10^{11}}=1.2$ MPa. After: $3.69\sqrt{3\times10^{14}}=63.9$ MPa. The increase is about $63$ MPa: the strength of the copper has doubled or tripled (annealed copper has $\sigma_y\approx30$ MPa), at the price of ductility, as the dislocations that provide the strength are also the obstacles that prevent further deformation. Heating (annealing) lets the dislocations rearrange and annihilate (recovery, recrystallisation), restoring the soft state.

Lecture 4. Diffusion and thermally activated processes#

Atoms in a solid move: by exchanging with a neighbouring vacancy (vacancy mechanism, the route of substitutional atoms and of self-diffusion) or by hopping between interstitial sites (small atoms: C, N, H in iron). The rate of such jumps depends exponentially on temperature, because an atom must overcome an energy barrier, the activation energy $Q$: the fraction of the time an atom has enough energy is $\exp(-Q/RT)$ (with $Q$ in J/mol), the Arrhenius law. The diffusion coefficient has the same form, $$D=D_0\,e^{-Q/RT}.$$ Taking logarithms, $\ln D=\ln D_0-(Q/R)(1/T)$: a straight line in an Arrhenius plot ($\ln D$ against $1/T$) with slope $-Q/R$, from which $Q$ is found. For substitutional diffusion $Q=E_f+E_m$, the sum of the vacancy formation and migration energies (Exercise 6 of PHYS 301); for interstitials only the migration energy appears, which is why carbon in iron diffuses so much faster than iron in iron: at $927{}^\circ$C the diffusivity of C in fcc Fe is $8.3\times10^{-12}$ m$^2$/s, while the self-diffusion of Fe is below $10^{-16}$ m$^2$/s.

Fick’s laws

First law (steady state): the flux of atoms (atoms per area per time) is proportional to the concentration gradient and directed down it, $J=-D\,\dfrac{\mathrm dC}{\mathrm dx}$. Second law (non-steady state), from conservation of matter, $\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial t}=D\dfrac{\partial^2C}{\partial x^2}$ for constant $D$. For a semi-infinite solid with a constant surface concentration $C_s$ and uniform initial concentration $C_0$ the solution is $$\frac{C_s-C(x,t)}{C_s-C_0}=\mathrm{erf}\Bigl(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\Bigr),$$ where $\mathrm{erf}$ is the error function (tabulated: $\mathrm{erf}(0.5)=0.520$, $\mathrm{erf}(1)=0.843$, $\mathrm{erf}(2)=0.995$). The scale of the penetration is the diffusion distance $\sqrt{Dt}$: a given concentration is reached at a depth $x\propto\sqrt{t}$, so doubling the depth takes four times the time.

Worked example 4.1: how far do copper atoms move?

The self-diffusion of copper has $D_0=2.0\times10^{-5}$ m$^2$/s and $Q=197$ kJ/mol. Find the diffusion distance $\sqrt{Dt}$ after one hour at $600$, $1000$ and $1300$ K, and after one year at $600$ K.

$D=D_0e^{-Q/RT}$: at $600$ K, $Q/RT=39.5$ and $D=1.4\times10^{-22}$ m$^2$/s; at $1000$ K, $Q/RT=23.7$, $D=1.0\times10^{-15}$ m$^2$/s; at $1300$ K, $D=2.4\times10^{-13}$ m$^2$/s. The distances after one hour ($t=3600$ s): $0.7$ nm (about three lattice spacings) at $600$ K, $1.9$ µm at $1000$ K and $30$ µm at $1300$ K. After a whole year at $600$ K ($t=3.15\times10^7$ s) it is $67$ nm. A grain-boundary or a free surface offers much faster paths (the activation energy is about half of the bulk), which is why sintering and the coarsening of fine microstructures are effective at temperatures where bulk diffusion is negligible. The thousand-fold change of $D$ from $1000$ K to $1300$ K shows why heat treatments are done hot.

Worked example 4.2: carburising a gear

A low-carbon steel with $0.2$ wt% C is carburised at $927{}^\circ$C with a surface carbon content of $1.0$ wt% ($D=8.3\times10^{-12}$ m$^2$/s). How long does it take to reach $0.5$ wt% C at a depth of $0.6$ mm? What if the temperature is $1000{}^\circ$C ($D=1.95\times10^{-11}$ m$^2$/s)?

$(C_s-C_x)/(C_s-C_0)=(1.0-0.5)/(1.0-0.2)=0.625=\mathrm{erf}(z)$, which gives $z=0.627$ from the table of the error function. With $z=x/2\sqrt{Dt}$, $t=(x/2z)^2/D=(0.6\times10^{-3}/1.254)^2/8.3\times10^{-12}=2.29\times10^{-7}/8.3\times10^{-12}=2.76\times10^4$ s $=7.6$ h. At $1000{}^\circ$C: $t=2.29\times10^{-7}/1.95\times10^{-11}=1.17\times10^4$ s $=3.3$ h, about $2.3$ times faster for $73$ K more. (A finite-element solution of the same problem agrees with the error-function solution to $0.001$ wt% in LAB 506.)

Other thermally activated processes

The same exponential describes the rate of creep, the growth of precipitates and grains, the oxidation of metals, the conductivity of intrinsic semiconductors ($\propto e^{-E_g/2k_\mathrm BT}$) and the rate of chemical reactions. If you see a property that changes by orders of magnitude over a modest temperature range, expect an activation energy. A useful rule: for $Q=1$ eV a temperature increase from $300$ K to $330$ K speeds a process up by $e^{(1/0.02585-1/0.02844)}=e^{3.52}=34$ times.

Lecture 5. Mechanical properties and strengthening#

In a tensile test a specimen of initial cross-section $A_0$ and gauge length $L_0$ is pulled at constant rate while the force $F$ and elongation $\Delta L$ are recorded. The engineering stress $\sigma=F/A_0$ and engineering strain $\varepsilon=\Delta L/L_0$ give a curve with characteristic regions (MSE 302 treats them in depth):

  • Elastic region: $\sigma=E\varepsilon$ (Hooke’s law) with the Young modulus $E$; the deformation is reversible, and is set by the bond stiffness of Lecture 1. Steel $207$ GPa, copper $130$, aluminium $70$, glass $70$, polymers $0.01$–$4$, diamond $1050$. The lateral contraction is described by Poisson’s ratio $\nu=-\varepsilon_\mathrm{lateral}/\varepsilon_\mathrm{axial}\approx0.3$ for metals.
  • Yield strength $\sigma_y$: the stress at which plastic deformation begins (conventionally the stress at $0.2\%$ permanent strain, the offset yield strength, found by drawing a line of slope $E$ from $\varepsilon=0.002$).
  • Tensile strength (UTS): the maximum engineering stress. Beyond it, the deformation localises in a neck and the engineering stress falls although the material continues to harden (the true stress $\sigma_T=F/A_\mathrm{instantaneous}$ keeps rising).
  • Ductility: the percentage elongation after fracture $\%EL=100(L_f-L_0)/L_0$ and the percentage reduction in area $\%RA=100(A_0-A_f)/A_0$.
  • Toughness: the energy absorbed per unit volume up to fracture, the area under the stress–strain curve; the resilience (elastic energy per volume at yield) is $U_r=\sigma_y^2/2E$.
Worked example 5.1: a tensile test

A cylindrical steel specimen ($d_0=12.8$ mm, $L_0=50.8$ mm) yields at $35$ kN, reaches a maximum load of $62$ kN and fractures at a gauge length of $63.5$ mm and a neck diameter of $10.2$ mm. Find the yield strength, tensile strength, $\%EL$ and $\%RA$.

$A_0=\pi(12.8)^2/4=128.7$ mm$^2$. Yield strength $\sigma_y=35\,000/128.7=272$ MPa; tensile strength $62\,000/128.7=482$ MPa. $\%EL=(63.5-50.8)/50.8\times100=25\%$. $A_f=\pi(10.2)^2/4=81.7$ mm$^2$ and $\%RA=(128.7-81.7)/128.7\times100=36.5\%$. The ratio $\sigma_y/\mathrm{UTS}=0.56$ shows considerable capacity for strain hardening. For a design with a safety factor of $2$ against yield the allowable stress is $136$ MPa.

Worked example 5.2: true stress and strain

At a point on the engineering curve the engineering stress is $400$ MPa and the engineering strain is $0.15$ (uniform deformation, before necking). Find the true stress and the true strain.

For uniform deformation at constant volume, $A=A_0L_0/L=A_0/(1+\varepsilon)$, so $\sigma_T=\sigma(1+\varepsilon)=400\times1.15=460$ MPa and $\varepsilon_T=\ln(1+\varepsilon)=\ln1.15=0.140$. True strains add (a strain of $\ln2$ followed by $\ln2$ is $\ln4$) whereas engineering strains do not, which is why true strain is used for large deformations. The relations hold only until necking.

All four increase the strength at the cost of ductility, except grain refinement, which also increases the toughness. The strengthening of a real alloy is the sum of several of these terms, which is the basis for the design of alloys and for models of yield strength as a sum of contributions.

Lecture 6. Phase diagrams#

A phase is a homogeneous part of a system with a uniform structure and composition (liquid, solid solution $\alpha$, compound Fe$_3$C). A phase diagram is a map, in temperature and composition (at fixed pressure), of the phases that are stable at equilibrium. From it one reads which phases are present, their compositions and, by the lever rule, their amounts. Equilibrium means infinitely slow cooling; real alloys are often out of equilibrium (Lecture 7), but the equilibrium diagram is the starting point, and the CALPHAD method (MSE 301, LAB 505) computes it from thermodynamic data.

Rules for reading a binary diagram

(1) Gibbs phase rule at constant pressure: $F=C-P+1$, with $C$ components, $P$ phases and $F$ degrees of freedom. In a one-phase region of a binary ($C=2$, $P=1$), $F=2$: temperature and composition can both be varied. In a two-phase region $F=1$: at a given temperature the compositions of both phases are fixed. At a eutectic or eutectoid point $P=3$, $F=0$: the temperature and compositions are fixed. (2) In a two-phase region, draw the horizontal tie line at the temperature; its ends give the compositions of the two phases. (3) Lever rule: for an alloy of overall composition $C_0$ whose tie line ends at $C_\alpha$ and $C_\beta$, the weight fractions are $W_\alpha=\dfrac{C_\beta-C_0}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}$ and $W_\beta=\dfrac{C_0-C_\alpha}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}$: each phase fraction is the length of the lever arm on the opposite side divided by the whole tie line. The rule follows from conservation of mass, $C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta$ with $W_\alpha+W_\beta=1$.

Three kinds of binary diagram cover most situations. Isomorphous systems (Cu–Ni) have complete solubility in both liquid and solid; cooling an alloy gives a solid richer in the higher-melting component. Eutectic systems (Pb–Sn, Al–Si, Ag–Cu) have limited solid solubilities and a eutectic reaction $\mathrm{L}\rightleftharpoons\alpha+\beta$ at a fixed temperature and composition, the lowest melting point of the system; this is why solder is a Pb–Sn or now Sn–Ag–Cu eutectic alloy, and why cast irons and aluminium–silicon casting alloys are near-eutectic (they melt at low temperature and fill moulds well). Eutectoid reactions are the solid-state equivalent, $\gamma\rightleftharpoons\alpha+\mathrm{Fe_3C}$ in steel. The teaching diagram of LAB 505 is of the eutectic kind.

The lead–tin eutectic

In the Pb–Sn diagram (composition in wt% Sn) the eutectic is at $183{}^\circ$C and $61.9$ wt% Sn. At that temperature the Pb-rich solid solution $\alpha$ holds up to $19.2$ wt% Sn and the Sn-rich solid solution $\beta$ up to $97.5$ wt% Sn; the solubility of Sn in $\alpha$ decreases on cooling.

To find the microstructure, consider the alloy just above $183{}^\circ$C: it is a mixture of primary $\alpha$ (composition $19.2$) and liquid of the eutectic composition ($61.9$). By the lever rule $W_{\alpha\prime}=(61.9-40)/(61.9-19.2)=0.513$ and $W_\mathrm L=0.487$. On cooling through $183{}^\circ$C the liquid transforms entirely into the eutectic mixture of fine $\alpha$ and $\beta$ lamellae. The microstructure therefore contains $51.3\%$ large primary $\alpha$ grains and $48.7\%$ eutectic (which contains both $\alpha$ and $\beta$): the fraction of the eutectic microconstituent is not the same as the fraction of $\beta$ phase ($26.6\%$). The iron–carbon diagram

Iron is the base of steels (below $2.1$ wt% C) and cast irons (above). Pure iron is bcc ferrite ($\alpha$) up to $912{}^\circ$C, fcc austenite ($\gamma$) up to $1394{}^\circ$C, bcc ($\delta$) up to the melting point of $1538{}^\circ$C. The interstitial carbon is much more soluble in fcc austenite (maximum $2.14$ wt% at $1147{}^\circ$C, because the octahedral holes of fcc are larger) than in bcc ferrite (maximum $0.022$ wt% at $727{}^\circ$C). The compound cementite Fe$_3$C ($6.70$ wt% C) is hard and brittle. The most important features of the diagram are:

  • the eutectoid at $727{}^\circ$C and $0.76$ wt% C: $\gamma\rightleftharpoons\alpha+\mathrm{Fe_3C}$, which gives the lamellar microconstituent pearlite (alternating plates of ferrite and cementite);
  • the eutectic at $1147{}^\circ$C and $4.30$ wt% C: $\mathrm L\rightleftharpoons\gamma+\mathrm{Fe_3C}$ (ledeburite), characteristic of white cast iron.

A steel with less than $0.76$ wt% C is hypoeutectoid: on cooling, proeutectoid ferrite forms from the austenite at the grain boundaries, then the remaining austenite (now $0.76$ wt% C) becomes pearlite at $727{}^\circ$C. A steel with more than $0.76$ wt% C is hypereutectoid, with proeutectoid cementite and pearlite.

Lecture 7. Microstructure, heat treatment and material selection#

An equilibrium diagram says nothing about how fast a transformation occurs. Real microstructures are controlled by kinetics: diffusion (Lecture 4) is thermally activated, so rapid cooling can freeze structures that equilibrium would not give. The nucleation and growth of a new phase needs undercooling below the equilibrium temperature to get a driving force, and the overall rate is a product of a nucleation rate (which rises as the undercooling rises) and a growth rate limited by diffusion (which falls as the temperature falls): the rate has a maximum at an intermediate temperature, giving the C-shaped curves of the time–temperature–transformation (TTT) diagram.

Heat treatment of steel

  • Annealing / normalising: heat to the austenite range, cool slowly (furnace) or in air; equilibrium coarse (annealed) or fine (normalised) pearlite, soft and ductile.
  • Quenching: austenite cooled so rapidly (in water or oil) that diffusion cannot form ferrite and cementite; below the martensite start temperature the fcc lattice shears, without diffusion, to martensite, a body-centred tetragonal structure with all the carbon trapped in the lattice (supersaturated). Martensite is extremely hard (up to about $65$ on the Rockwell C scale for high-carbon steels) because of the lattice distortion, the high dislocation density and the fine lath structure, and it is brittle.
  • Tempering: reheating martensite at $200$–$650{}^\circ$C lets carbon diffuse out as fine carbides, reducing the strength and restoring toughness: quench plus temper gives the combinations of strength and toughness of tool and structural steels, and the temperature of tempering is the control variable. Since tempering is controlled by diffusion, time and temperature trade off: a short time at a high temperature is equivalent to a long time at a low one (the Hollomon–Jaffe parameter $T(C+\log t)$).
  • Hardenability: the ability of a steel to form martensite deep inside a thick section, set by the alloying elements (Cr, Mo, Mn, Ni) that delay the diffusional transformations to longer times (displacing the TTT curve to the right) so that a slower cooling rate of the interior still avoids pearlite.

Precipitation hardening

An alloy with a solubility that falls with temperature (the solvus of Al–Cu, with $\alpha$ dissolving up to $5.65$ wt% Cu at $548{}^\circ$C and very little at room temperature) is heat treated in three steps: solution treatment (heat into the single-phase $\alpha$ region and hold), quench (to a supersaturated solid solution), and ageing (at room temperature or $100$–$200{}^\circ$C: fine coherent precipitates of Cu-rich phases form, which obstruct the dislocations). The strength is highest at a peak-aged state of fine precipitates (spacing of nanometres) and falls on over-ageing, when the precipitates coarsen and are bypassed (Orowan looping). This is how the aerospace alloys of the 2000 and 7000 series reach yield strengths of $400$–$500$ MPa from pure aluminium’s $\sim30$ MPa. The phase-field and precipitate-growth simulations of CMS 505 and LAB 506 are tools for this kind of microstructure design.

Selecting materials

Choosing a material means matching its properties to the function while minimising a quantity (mass, cost). The systematic method of Ashby plots one property against another on logarithmic axes and uses material indices. For a light beam that must meet a stiffness requirement in bending, the mass of a beam of fixed shape and length is proportional to $\rho/E^{1/2}$; for a light panel it is proportional to $\rho/E^{1/3}$; for a tie that must carry a tensile load without yielding, $\rho/\sigma_y$. The best materials have the largest $E^{1/2}/\rho$, $E^{1/3}/\rho$ and $\sigma_y/\rho$. A surprising fact comes out of the numbers:

Worked example 7.1: the aluminium beam

By what factor can the mass of a beam of fixed length and cross-section shape be reduced if aluminium ($E=70$ GPa, $\rho=2.70$) replaces steel ($207$ GPa, $7.85$) for the same bending stiffness?

For beams of the same shape the second moment of area scales with the size $t$ as $I\propto t^4$ and the bending stiffness as $EI$, so $t\propto E^{-1/4}$ and the mass $m\propto\rho t^2\propto\rho/E^{1/2}$. Ratio: $m_\mathrm{Al}/m_\mathrm{steel}=(2.70/\sqrt{70})/(7.85/\sqrt{207})=0.3227/0.5456=0.59$: a $41\%$ mass reduction. The aluminium section is larger, $t_\mathrm{Al}/t_\mathrm{steel}=(207/70)^{1/4}=1.31$, which is why aluminium aircraft skins are thicker than the steel they replace; for a tension member, where only $\rho/E$ matters, the aluminium member is no lighter than steel ($\rho/E$ is $0.0386$ against $0.0379$ g/cm$^3$ per GPa).

Key takeaways
  • Materials science links four things: processing, structure, properties and performance; structure exists at every scale from the bond (0.1 nm) through crystals and defects (nm to um) to the microstructure of grains and phases (um to mm), and each scale has its own tools.
  • The bond energy curve explains stiffness, melting and thermal expansion: for argon the Lennard-Jones potential gives r0 = 0.382 nm and a bond stiffness of 0.82 N/m, so the Young modulus is about k/r0 = 2.1 GPa (measured bulk modulus of solid argon: about 3 GPa); strong covalent bonds (diamond) are 500 times stiffer.
  • Crystal structures are described by a lattice and a basis: fcc and hcp pack spheres at 74 per cent, bcc at 68 per cent, diamond at 34 per cent, and the density follows from the cell: copper (fcc, a = 0.3615 nm) is 8.93 g/cm3, iron (bcc, a = 0.2866 nm) 7.88 g/cm3.
  • Defects control most properties: the equilibrium vacancy fraction exp(-E_f/kT) is 1e-5 for copper at 1300 K; dislocations (Burgers vector 0.256 nm in copper) carry plastic flow and raise the strength as sigma = alpha G b sqrt(rho), 37 MPa at 1e14 per m2; grain boundaries add strength by the Hall-Petch relation sigma_y = sigma_0 + k d^(-1/2).
  • Phase diagrams give equilibrium phases and amounts by the lever rule: a Pb-40 wt% Sn alloy just below 183 C is 73.4 per cent alpha and 26.6 per cent beta, of which 48.7 per cent is eutectic; a 0.4 wt% C steel below 727 C is 48.8 per cent proeutectoid ferrite and 51.2 per cent pearlite.

(b) Annealing removes the work-hardening contribution (dislocations rearrange and annihilate: recovery and recrystallisation) and, if long and hot enough, coarsens the precipitates (over-ageing) and grows the grains, so reducing the Hall–Petch term. The solid solution contribution remains until the solute is precipitated. (c) The contributions are not independent: obstacles act on the same dislocations, and the combination of different obstacles is better described by a root-mean-square sum for obstacles of comparable strength or a linear sum for weak and strong ones; the model also ignores interactions (the precipitate size depends on the dislocation density through heterogeneous nucleation). The linear sum is a convenient first estimate and a good way to see where the strength comes from.

(a) $15.6$, $14.9$ MPa; total $190.5$ MPa (b) work hardening and (when over-aged) precipitates (c) not independent

Problem 10Core

A Pb–30 wt% Sn alloy is cooled slowly to just below $183{}^\circ$C (the eutectic at $61.9$ wt% Sn; $\alpha$ holds up to $19.2$ and $\beta$ up to $97.5$ wt% Sn). (a) Give the total fractions of $\alpha$ and $\beta$. (b) Give the fractions of primary $\alpha$ and of eutectic. (c) At $150{}^\circ$C the solvus compositions are about $11$ and $98$ wt% Sn: what are the phase fractions there?

Hint

Use the tie line $19.2$–$97.5$ for the phases at $183{}^\circ$C, and the tie line $19.2$–$61.9$ above the eutectic for the microconstituents.

Full solution

(a) $W_\alpha=(97.5-30)/(97.5-19.2)=0.862$, $W_\beta=0.138$. (b) Just above $183{}^\circ$C: $W_{\alpha\prime}=(61.9-30)/(61.9-19.2)=0.747$ primary $\alpha$ and $W_\mathrm L=0.253$ liquid of eutectic composition, which becomes eutectic: so $74.7\%$ primary $\alpha$, $25.3\%$ eutectic. (c) At $150{}^\circ$C: $W_\alpha=(98-30)/(98-11)=0.782$ and $W_\beta=0.218$: on cooling, the solubility of Sn in $\alpha$ decreases and the fraction of $\alpha$ falls from $86.2\%$ to $78.2\%$ while $\beta$ precipitates from $\alpha$ (in a real casting the $\beta$ nucleates inside the $\alpha$ grains as fine particles).

(a) $86.2\%$ $\alpha$, $13.8\%$ $\beta$ (b) $74.7\%$ primary $\alpha$, $25.3\%$ eutectic (c) $78.2\%$, $21.8\%$

Problem 11Challenge

A hypereutectoid steel contains $1.0$ wt% C and is cooled slowly. (a) Find the fractions of proeutectoid cementite and of pearlite just below $727{}^\circ$C. (b) Find the total fraction of cementite. (c) Compare with the $0.4$ wt% steel of Worked example 6.2 and say which is harder and which tougher.

Hint

For hypereutectoid steels, the tie line is from the eutectoid composition $0.76$ to cementite $6.70$.

Full solution

(a) Proeutectoid cementite: $W_{\mathrm{Fe_3C}\prime}=(1.0-0.76)/(6.70-0.76)=0.040$ ($4.0\%$), pearlite $96.0\%$. (b) The total cementite is, with the ferrite–cementite tie line $0.022$–$6.70$, $W_\mathrm{Fe_3C}=(1.0-0.022)/(6.70-0.022)=0.146$ ($14.6\%$). (c) The $0.4$ wt% steel has $48.8\%$ ferrite and $51.2\%$ pearlite ($5.7\%$ cementite in total): it is softer and much tougher (large ductile ferrite fraction). The $1.0$ wt% steel is almost all pearlite plus a brittle network of cementite on the austenite grain boundaries (which is why it is heat treated, first spheroidised or quenched, before use as a tool steel): harder but less tough. Hardness rises with carbon content, toughness falls.

(a) $4.0\%$ cementite, $96.0\%$ pearlite (b) $14.6\%$ (c) $1.0$ wt% is harder, $0.4$ wt% tougher

Problem 12Challenge

A bicycle frame tube of fixed length and cross-section shape must have a given bending stiffness. (a) Compute the mass relative to steel for aluminium ($70$ GPa, $2.70$), titanium ($114$, $4.43$) and magnesium ($45$, $1.74$) using $m\propto\rho/E^{1/2}$. (b) Repeat for a panel ($m\propto\rho/E^{1/3}$). (c) Why are most bicycle frames not made of magnesium, and what changes if the same tube must also not yield under load?

Hint

Take the ratio of $\rho/E^{1/2}$ to the value for steel, $7.85/\sqrt{207}=0.5456$.

Full solution

(a) Beam: Al $0.591$, Ti $0.760$, Mg $0.475$ times the mass of the steel tube. (b) Panel: $\rho/E^{1/3}$ for steel is $7.85/5.916=1.327$; Al $2.70/4.121=0.655$ (ratio $0.494$), Ti $4.43/4.848=0.914$ ($0.688$), Mg $1.74/3.557=0.489$ ($0.369$). (c) Magnesium is the lightest for equal stiffness but it corrodes easily, is flammable when machined or welded, has a lower fatigue strength, and the thin sections it would need ($t\propto E^{-1/4}$, a $1.5$ times larger tube than steel) are impractical for the frame geometry; aluminium offers $41\%$ mass saving at low cost, and titanium is chosen when strength, fatigue and corrosion matter. If the tube must also not yield under a bending moment, the index is $\sigma_y^{2/3}/\rho$ and strong alloys (heat-treated Al 7075 with $\sigma_y=500$ MPa, Ti–6Al–4V with $880$ MPa) are favoured over soft ones: material selection always has several constraints, and the best choice is the one that satisfies all of them with the lowest mass or cost.

(a) $0.59$, $0.76$, $0.48$ (b) $0.49$, $0.69$, $0.37$ (c) corrosion, fatigue, flammability; strength changes the index

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Basic

Explain, using the bond energy curve, why materials with strong bonds have high melting points, high stiffness and low thermal expansion.

Solution

A deep well means that a large energy is needed to separate the atoms ($T_m\propto E_0$). A narrow, steep well means a large curvature $k=\mathrm d^2E/\mathrm dr^2$ and so a large modulus $E\approx k/r_0$. Thermal expansion arises from the asymmetry of the well: at higher temperature the atoms vibrate with larger amplitude and, because the repulsive wall is steeper than the attractive tail, the mean separation increases. A deeper, narrower well is more symmetric over the thermal range, so the shift of the mean position is smaller.

Quiz 2Basic

Compute the number of atoms per unit cell and the packing fraction of the bcc structure.

Solution

$8\times\tfrac18+1=2$ atoms. Atoms touch along the body diagonal: $4R=\sqrt3a$, so $a=4R/\sqrt3$. Packing fraction $=2\cdot\tfrac43\pi R^3/a^3=\tfrac{8\pi R^3}{3}\cdot\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{64R^3}=\pi\sqrt3/8=0.680$.

Quiz 3Core

What is the Burgers vector, and why does a dislocation allow plastic deformation at stresses a thousand times below the theoretical shear strength?

Solution

The Burgers vector is the lattice vector that measures the closure failure of a circuit around the dislocation, giving the magnitude and direction of the slip it produces. A dislocation moves by shifting one row of atoms at a time, so only a few bonds are broken at once and the stress needed is that of the Peierls barrier ($\sim10^{-4}$–$10^{-2}G$ for metals), while shearing a whole plane rigidly would break all bonds across the plane simultaneously (about $G/10$).

Quiz 4Core

The grain size of a steel is reduced from $100$ to $10$ µm. By what factor does the Hall–Petch term $k_yd^{-1/2}$ increase, and does the toughness increase or decrease?

Solution

$d^{-1/2}$ rises by $\sqrt{100/10}=3.16$. For the steel of Worked example 3.1, the term goes from $70$ to $221$ MPa. Grain refinement is exceptional among strengthening mechanisms because toughness also increases (more boundaries deflect cracks and the ductile–brittle transition temperature falls).

Quiz 5Core

Define the lever rule and use it for an alloy of composition $C_0=20$ wt% B in a two-phase region whose tie line ends at $C_\alpha=10$ and $C_\beta=50$ wt% B.

Solution

The weight fraction of a phase is the length of the lever arm on the opposite side of the alloy composition, divided by the tie-line length: $W_\alpha=(50-20)/(50-10)=0.75$, $W_\beta=(20-10)/(50-10)=0.25$. It follows from conservation of mass: $C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta$.

Quiz 6Challenge

Why does quenching a $0.8$ wt% C steel give a hard, brittle structure whereas slow cooling gives a soft, ductile one, and what is the role of tempering?

Solution

Slow cooling allows carbon to diffuse so that austenite transforms into pearlite (ferrite plus cementite lamellae), which is close to equilibrium and relatively soft. Fast quenching suppresses diffusion; the fcc lattice shears to martensite, in which all the carbon remains in solution in a distorted body-centred tetragonal lattice with a high dislocation density: very hard and brittle, with large internal stresses. Tempering at $200$–$650{}^\circ$C allows the carbon to diffuse out as fine carbides and the dislocations to rearrange, trading some hardness for much greater toughness; the temperature and time of tempering set the compromise.
