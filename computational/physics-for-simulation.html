Physics for simulation

Behind every method in this series is a handful of physical principles: Newton’s laws for atoms, electrostatics for charges, the Schrödinger equation for electrons and thermodynamics for what is stable. This page states them in the form the simulation methods use them, and gives the units and constants you need to move between them.

2. Classical mechanics#

Forces, energy and the potential energy surface#

Newton’s second law, F = ma, together with the fact that interatomic forces derive from a potential energy, F = −∇U, defines the whole of classical atomistic modelling. The function U(r1, …, rN) of all atomic positions is the potential energy surface. Its features have direct physical meaning: a point where the gradient vanishes is an equilibrium; a minimum (all second derivatives positive) is stable; a saddle point with one negative curvature is a transition state between two minima; the depth of a minimum is a binding or cohesive energy and the height of a saddle above it is an activation barrier.

Total energy is conserved when only conservative forces act, E = K + U = constant, which is the check used to validate integrators in molecular dynamics. Linear and angular momentum are also conserved for an isolated system; these conservation laws follow from the symmetry of space under translation and rotation.

Vibrations and phonons#

Near a minimum, any potential looks like a parabola, so atoms behave like masses on springs. A single oscillator of stiffness k and mass m vibrates with angular frequency ω = √(k/m). For a crystal of N atoms, the coupled equations have 3N independent normal modes, each oscillating at one frequency, found by diagonalising the matrix of second derivatives (the dynamical matrix, see eigenvalue problems). Quantised normal modes are called phonons. For a one-dimensional chain of identical atoms with spacing a and nearest-neighbour stiffness K, the frequency depends on the wavenumber as

ω(k) = 2 √(K/m) |sin(ka/2)|

which is linear for small k (sound waves, with speed √(K/m)a) and flattens at the edge of the Brillouin zone. Typical atomic vibration frequencies in solids are a few to tens of terahertz, periods of about 0.1 ps, which is why molecular dynamics needs femtosecond steps. Phonons carry heat and set the vibrational free energy.

Hamiltonian mechanics#

The Hamiltonian H(q, p) = Σpi²/2mi + U(q) gives the equations of motion in first-order form:

dqi/dt = ∂H/∂pi,    dpi/dt = −∂H/∂qi

The state of the system is a point in phase space of positions and momenta, and a key result (Liouville’s theorem) is that the flow preserves phase-space volume. Numerical integrators that respect this structure (symplectic integrators such as velocity-Verlet) keep the energy from drifting over millions of steps, which is why they are standard in molecular dynamics. The same Hamiltonian appears again in statistical mechanics, where the Boltzmann factor is exp(−H/kBT).

3. Electrostatics and magnetism#

Coulomb interactions#

Two point charges q1 and q2 a distance r apart have potential energy U = q1q2/(4πε0r). In atomistic units, e²/(4πε0) = 14.40 eV·Å, so two unit charges 2 Å apart interact with about 7.2 eV, far more than typical bond energies, which is why ionic solids are strongly bound. The electrostatic potential φ of a charge density ρ obeys Poisson’s equation, ∇²φ = −ρ/ε0 (see partial differential equations).

Why long range matters#

Coulomb interactions decay only as 1/r, while the number of charges at distance r grows as r². A direct sum over a periodic crystal therefore converges only conditionally, and truncating at a cutoff gives errors that do not vanish. The remedy is the Ewald summation (1921) and its mesh-based descendants (PPPM, particle-mesh Ewald), which split the interaction into a short-range part summed in real space and a smooth long-range part summed in reciprocal space. For a rock-salt crystal, the result is the Madelung constant, α ≈ 1.7476 for NaCl, which sets the electrostatic energy per ion pair. Practical consequences for simulation cells: they must be charge neutral, a charged defect in a periodic cell needs a compensating background and a correction, and dielectric screening reduces interactions in the real material.

Magnetism#

Electrons carry a spin magnetic moment of about one Bohr magneton, μB = eℏ/2me ≈ 5.79 × 10−5 eV/T. Whether the moments of neighbouring atoms line up is governed by the quantum exchange interaction, often modelled by the Heisenberg form E = −JΣSi·Sj. A positive J favours parallel alignment (ferromagnetism, as in iron, cobalt and nickel), and a negative J favours antiparallel (antiferromagnetism). Ferromagnetic order disappears above the Curie temperature (1043 K for iron), and its energy contribution is large enough to change which crystal structure is stable, a point that matters for iron-based alloys.

4. Quantum mechanics essentials#

Wavefunctions and the Schrödinger equation#

An electron is described by a wavefunction ψ(r), with |ψ|² the probability density of finding it near r, so that ∫|ψ|² dV = 1. Allowed energies and states come from the time-independent Schrödinger equation, an eigenvalue problem:

Ĥ ψ = E ψ,    Ĥ = − (ℏ² / 2m) ∇² + V(r)

The first term is the kinetic energy and V the potential. Because the equation has acceptable solutions only for particular energies, energies are quantised. The standard solvable cases build intuition:

  • Particle in a box of width L: En = n²h²/(8mL²), ψn = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L), n = 1, 2, … Confinement raises the energy: for an electron, E1 = 37.6 eV·Å²/L².
  • Harmonic oscillator: En = (n + ½)ℏω, with a non-zero zero-point energy ½ℏω; the origin of quantum effects on light atoms such as hydrogen and of the failure of classical heat capacity at low temperature.
  • Hydrogen atom: En = −13.606 eV/n², with orbitals labelled s, p, d, f by angular momentum. These shapes are the basis of the periodic table and of chemical bonding in molecules and solids.

Four principles used constantly#

  1. Spin and the Pauli exclusion principle. Electrons have spin ½, and no two can occupy the same quantum state. Filling levels from the bottom with two electrons each (spin up and down) gives the electronic structure of atoms, the Fermi level of a metal and the occupied bands of a semiconductor.
  2. Exchange and correlation. Because electrons are identical fermions, their joint wavefunction changes sign when two are swapped (exchange), and their motion is correlated by Coulomb repulsion. Everything in many-electron theory beyond independent electrons is these two effects, which is the content hidden in the exchange–correlation functional of DFT.
  3. The variational principle. For any trial wavefunction, the expectation value of the energy is at least the true ground-state energy, E[ψtrial] ≥ E0. So the best approximation is the one with the lowest energy, which turns quantum mechanics into an optimisation problem; basis-set convergence in a calculation means the energy goes down toward a limit.
  4. Born–Oppenheimer approximation. Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so the electrons settle instantly into the ground state for any arrangement of nuclei, which defines the potential energy surface of Section 2.

Electrons in crystals#

In a periodic potential, Bloch’s theorem says each eigenstate has the form ψnk(r) = eik·runk(r), with u having the periodicity of the lattice and k a wavevector in the first Brillouin zone. The energies En(k) form bands separated by gaps. Whether the highest filled band is full or partly filled determines whether the material is an insulator or semiconductor (a gap at the Fermi level) or a metal (a partly filled band).

The simplest model is free electrons, E = ℏ²k²/2m, filled up to the Fermi energy EF = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)2/3 for electron density n. For copper (n ≈ 8.5 × 1028 m−3) this gives about 7 eV, much larger than kBT at any realistic temperature, which is why only electrons near the Fermi level respond to heat and why metals have a small electronic heat capacity. The density of states g(E), the number of states per unit energy, is the quantity that links band structure to properties and to the occupation integrals of DFT.

Tunnelling, the penetration of a particle into a classically forbidden region, with probability falling as e−2κa across a barrier of width a, explains scanning tunnelling microscopy and is the quantum correction to hydrogen hopping in metals.

5. Thermodynamics#

The first law, dU = δQ − δW, states conservation of energy; the second law says the entropy S of an isolated system never decreases, which is why equilibrium means a free-energy minimum rather than an energy minimum. The state functions that matter are the internal energy U, enthalpy H = U + pV, Helmholtz energy F = U − TS and Gibbs energy G = H − TS. In differential form:

dG = −S dT + V dp + Σi μi dni

so entropy and volume are slopes of G, and the chemical potential μi is the Gibbs energy cost of adding a particle. Response functions follow by differentiation: heat capacity CV = (∂U/∂T)V, thermal expansion α, bulk modulus B and the relation Cp − CV = TVα²B. The third law, S → 0 as T → 0, and the Dulong–Petit limit, C → 3R ≈ 24.9 J mol−1 K−1 for a classical solid, frame what simulations should reproduce at the extremes.

The microscopic origin of these quantities (the Boltzmann factor, partition functions, entropy as the logarithm of the number of microstates, S = kB ln Ω) is developed in statistical mechanics for simulation, and the practical computation of equilibrium for alloys in thermodynamics and CALPHAD.

6. Waves and diffraction#

A wave has wavelength λ, wavenumber k = 2π/λ and frequency ν; waves superpose, and the sum of waves scattered by different atoms is large only where they arrive in phase. For a crystal with plane spacing d, this gives Bragg’s law, nλ = 2d sinθ, and in reciprocal-space language the Laue condition that the change in wavevector equals a reciprocal lattice vector, Δk = G (see crystal structures). X-rays from a copper tube have λ = 1.54 Å (photon energy = 12.398 keV·Å/λ), comparable to atomic spacings, which is why they resolve crystal structure; neutrons and electrons work the same way with different sensitivities.

For simulation this matters in two ways: the powder diffraction pattern or phonon dispersion of a computed structure can be calculated directly and compared with experiment, and the same wave mathematics (plane waves, Fourier transforms, Brillouin zones) underlies the electronic-structure methods.

Simulation codes use different unit systems, and most errors in units are factors of 1.6 × 10−19 or 96 hiding in a conversion. The most useful equivalences are below.

Common program conventions: DFT codes often use eV and Å (VASP) or Rydberg and Bohr (Quantum ESPRESSO); LAMMPS “metal” units use eV, Å, ps, g/mol and bar. Always write the units of an input and a result next to the number.

Key takeaways
  • A potential energy surface turns forces, equilibrium, stability and vibrations into calculus: forces are slopes, stiffness is curvature, and normal modes are eigenvectors.
  • Electrostatics is long-ranged, which is why charged systems need special summation methods and why neutral cells matter.
  • Quantum mechanics enters through the Schroedinger equation, the Pauli principle, the variational principle and Bloch's theorem for periodic solids.
  • Thermodynamics supplies the quantities that simulations are compared with: free energies, chemical potentials and heat capacities.
  • Know your units: 1 eV is about 96.5 kJ/mol, kT is about 0.0259 eV at 300 K, and 1 Hartree is about 27.2 eV.

8. Check your understanding#

An interatomic bond has stiffness 20 N/m between atoms of mass 63.5 u. What is the vibration frequency?

ω = √(k/m) = √(20/(63.5 × 1.661 × 10−27)) ≈ 1.4 × 1013 rad/s, so ν = ω/2π ≈ 2.2 THz and the period is about 0.45 ps. A time step of 1 fs is a few hundred times shorter than this, which is why molecular dynamics needs such small steps.

Why must a simulation cell of ions be charge neutral, and what does a truncated Coulomb sum do wrong?

The Coulomb energy of a net-charged periodic system diverges (or depends on arbitrary conventions), so a cell is made neutral or a neutralising background is added. Truncating the 1/r sum at a cutoff ignores a contribution that does not vanish with distance, because the number of charges grows as r², so Ewald or particle-mesh methods are used instead.

Estimate the Fermi energy of copper from its electron density of 8.5 × 1028 m−3.

kF = (3π²n)1/3 ≈ 1.36 × 1010 m−1, and EF = ℏ²kF²/2m ≈ 1.13 × 10−18 J ≈ 7 eV, some 270 times kBT at room temperature.
