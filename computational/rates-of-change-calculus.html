Rates of change: calculus from scratch

Calculus is the mathematics of change. Nearly every law in this series says how fast something changes: how quickly heat flows, how fast atoms move, how a force follows from an energy. This page builds the three main ideas (slope, area and equations for rates) from pictures, without proofs.

1. Change and rates#

Suppose a car travels 150 metres in 10 seconds. Its average speed is the distance divided by the time: 150/10 = 15 m/s. On a graph of distance against time, this is the slope of the straight line joining the start and the end. But a real car speeds up and slows down, and the speedometer shows something different: its speed right now. How can we measure speed at a single instant, when no time passes?

The answer is to look at shorter and shorter intervals. The average speed over the last second is a good guess, over the last tenth of a second a better one, over the last thousandth better still. As the interval shrinks toward zero, the average settles toward a definite number, the instantaneous rate of change. That is the central idea of calculus, and it is the step that the first demo below lets you take.

2. The derivative#

The slope of the straight line between two points on a curve is called a secant. Move the second point closer to the first and the secant swings toward the tangent, the line that just touches the curve at one point. The slope of the tangent is the derivative of the function at that point. In symbols, for a function f(x):

f′(x) = limh→0 [ f(x + h) − f(x) ] / h

The expression is the slope of the secant (rise over run) with gap h; the “lim” says “what it approaches as h shrinks to zero”. The derivative is also written df/dx (read “dee f by dee x”), and if the variable is time, dx/dt. Its unit is the unit of f per unit of x: the derivative of position (metres) with respect to time (seconds) is velocity in m/s.

Two rules let you combine them. A constant multiplies through: d(3x2)/dx = 3 × 2x = 6x. Sums are done term by term: d(5 + 2t − t2)/dt = 0 + 2 − 2t = 2 − 2t.

Worked example. A ball’s height is h(t) = 20t − 5t2 metres. Its velocity is the derivative, v(t) = 20 − 10t m/s. At t = 1 s the velocity is 10 m/s (still rising); at t = 2 s it is 0 (the top of the flight); at t = 3 s it is −10 m/s (falling). The derivative of velocity, −10 m/s2, is the acceleration due to gravity (the minus sign says “downward”), found by differentiating twice.

3. Finding the bottom of a valley#

A ball rolls to the lowest point of a valley and stays there. At the very bottom the ground is flat for an instant: the slope is zero. So to find a minimum (or a maximum) of any smooth curve, set the derivative to zero and solve. This is how equilibrium positions are found throughout science, because systems tend to settle where their energy is lowest.

Worked example. The energy of two atoms depends on their separation x (in some units) as U(x) = (x − 2)2 + 1. Then U′(x) = 2(x − 2). Setting it to zero gives x = 2, and the energy there is U(2) = 1. So the preferred separation is 2.

Zero slope alone does not tell you whether you are at a valley or a hilltop. For that look at the second derivative, the derivative of the derivative, which measures how fast the slope changes (the curvature). If U″ > 0 the curve bends upward like a bowl: a minimum, a stable equilibrium. If U″ < 0 it bends downward: a maximum, unstable. In the example U″ = 2 > 0, so it is a stable minimum.

The curvature also has a physical meaning: it is the stiffness. A steep-sided narrow valley (large U″) means any displacement is strongly resisted, like a stiff spring. For a spring the energy is ½k(x − x0)2, whose second derivative is exactly the spring constant k. Near the bottom, nearly every energy curve looks like such a parabola, which is why springs describe vibrating atoms so well.

4. Adding up: area and integrals#

Calculus has a second main idea, the reverse of finding a rate: adding up many small pieces. If a car moves at a constant 20 m/s for 5 s, it covers 20 × 5 = 100 m, the area of a rectangle on a speed–time graph (height 20, width 5). If the speed changes, cut the time into many thin strips, treat the speed as constant in each strip, and add the areas. Thinner strips give a better answer, and in the limit of infinitely thin strips the total is called the integral, written with an elongated S for “sum”:

distance = ∫ab v(t) dt

The integral equals the area under the curve between a and b. Move the slider to add more rectangles and watch the sum approach the exact area.

Integrals can often be found by running the derivative table backwards: ∫ xn dx = xn+1/(n + 1). This reveals the deep link: differentiation and integration undo each other.

Worked examples. A speed that rises steadily as v = 2t from 0 to 4 s covers a distance equal to the area of a triangle, ½ × 4 × 8 = 16 m. The same result from the rule: ∫04 2t dt = [t2]04 = 16 − 0 = 16. And ∫03 x2 dx = [x3/3]03 = 27/3 = 9, the exact value the area demo approaches.

Integrals appear whenever a total is built from varying pieces: the energy absorbed is the integral of power over time, the amount of material in a region is the integral of concentration over volume, and the average of a quantity is its integral divided by the length of the interval.

5. Equations about rates#

The most important use of derivatives in science is writing laws as equations about how fast something changes. These are called differential equations. The simplest and most common says that the rate of change is proportional to the amount present:

dN/dt = −k N

Read it as: “the amount N decreases at a rate proportional to itself”. The more there is, the faster it goes; as it dwindles, the loss slows. Its solution is an exponential decay, N(t) = N0 e−kt, where N0 is the starting amount. You can check by differentiating: the derivative of N0 e−kt is −k N0 e−kt = −k N. ✓

The half-life, the time for N to fall to half, is t½ = ln 2/k ≈ 0.693/k. If k = 0.05 per minute, the half-life is 13.9 minutes. With a plus sign instead of a minus the same equation describes growth with a doubling time.

The cooling of a cup of tea follows the same pattern with one change: the rate depends on the difference from room temperature, dT/dt = −k(T − Troom). Its solution is T = Troom + (T0 − Troom) e−kt: the tea relaxes exponentially toward room temperature. A computer can also get the answer without any formula by taking small steps: new T = old T + (rate) × (time step). That is exactly how simulations work, and you will build one in Your first simulation.

6. More than one variable#

Often a quantity depends on several things: the height of a hilly landscape depends on x and y; the energy of a system depends on the positions of many atoms. A partial derivative is the slope in one direction while the other variables are held fixed, written ∂h/∂x. For h(x, y) = 4 − x2 − y2 (a smooth hill), ∂h/∂x = −2x and ∂h/∂y = −2y.

Putting the slopes together gives the gradient, an arrow written ∇h = (∂h/∂x, ∂h/∂y) that points in the direction of steepest uphill, with length equal to the steepness. For the hill, ∇h = (−2x, −2y), pointing back toward the summit at the origin.

This leads to the most important formula in atomistic modelling. A ball on a slope is pushed downhill, opposite to the gradient. In the same way, the force on an object is minus the gradient of its potential energy:

F = −∇U

For U(x, y) = x2 + 3y2 the force is (−2x, −6y), which at (1, 2) is (−2, −12): it points toward the minimum and is stronger in the steeper direction. Computing forces on atoms from an energy is exactly what molecular dynamics does at every step.

Key takeaways
  • A derivative is the slope of a curve at a point: the instantaneous rate of change.
  • Where the slope of an energy curve is zero there is an equilibrium; a positive curvature there means it is stable.
  • An integral adds up many small pieces and equals the area under a curve.
  • Many laws say 'the rate of change depends on the amount', and their solutions are exponential decay or growth.
  • Force is minus the slope of the energy: the arrow that points downhill.

7. Check your understanding#

A runner covers 150 m in 10 s. What is the average speed?

Distance divided by time: 150/10 = 15 m/s. This is the slope of the line joining the start and finish of the distance–time graph.

Differentiate 4x3.

Bring down the power and subtract one from it, keeping the constant: 4 × 3x2 = 12x2.

Where is the minimum of U(x) = 3(x − 1)2 + 2, and how stiff is it?

U′ = 6(x − 1) = 0 gives x = 1, where U = 2. The second derivative is U″ = 6, positive, so it is a minimum with stiffness 6.

What is the area under the constant function f(x) = 3 from x = 0 to x = 5?

It is a rectangle of height 3 and width 5, so the area (the integral) is 15.

A quantity decays as dN/dt = −0.1N (time in hours). What is its half-life?

ln 2/k = 0.693/0.1 = 6.93 hours.

A potential is U(x, y) = x2 + y2. What is the force at (1, −2)?

F = −∇U = (−2x, −2y) = (−2, 4) at that point. It points toward the origin, the minimum.
