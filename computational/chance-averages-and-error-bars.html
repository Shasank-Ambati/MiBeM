Chance, averages and error bars

Nothing in science is measured exactly, and nothing in a simulation is calculated from a single run. Atoms move randomly, instruments have noise, and simulations use random numbers. This page teaches how to handle chance sensibly: how to average, how to state how sure you are, and how much data you need.

1. Why chance matters here#

There are three reasons you will meet chance throughout the series. First, measurements are noisy: measure the same length twice and the numbers differ slightly. Second, atoms move randomly: at any temperature each atom gets knocked about by its neighbours in an unpredictable way, so properties like temperature and pressure are averages over enormous numbers of atoms. Third, many simulation methods deliberately use random numbers (the name Monte Carlo comes from the casino), and any result computed that way carries a statistical error that you must estimate. Knowing how to do this separates a result from a guess.

2. Probability#

The probability of an event is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) saying how likely it is. When all outcomes are equally likely, the probability is the number of outcomes you want divided by the total number of possible outcomes. A fair coin: the probability of heads is 1/2 = 0.5. A fair die: the probability of a six is 1/6 ≈ 0.17.

  • Independent events (one does not affect the other) multiply. Two heads in two flips: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Three heads in a row: 1/8.
  • The chance that an event does not happen is 1 minus the chance that it does. At least one head in three flips: 1 − (chance of no heads) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.
  • Mutually exclusive outcomes (they cannot both happen) add. The chance of a one or a six on a die: 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3.

The expected value is the average you would get over many repeats. For a fair die it is (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 3.5, a value you can never roll, but the long-run average of many rolls. Probability does not say what one trial will do, only what happens in the long run, and that is exactly how it is used.

3. Averages and spread#

Given a set of numbers, two summaries do most of the work.

The mean (average) is the sum divided by how many there are. The standard deviation, written σ (sigma) or s, tells you how spread out the numbers are around the mean. Roughly, it is the typical distance of a value from the mean. To calculate it: subtract the mean from each value, square the differences (so negatives do not cancel), add them, divide by one less than the number of values, and take the square root.

Worked example. The values 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 have mean (4+5+6+7+8)/5 = 6. The differences are −2, −1, 0, 1, 2; squared: 4, 1, 0, 1, 4; their sum is 10. Dividing by 5 − 1 = 4 gives 2.5, and the square root is σ = 1.58. (We divide by n − 1 instead of n because the mean itself was estimated from the same data, which makes the spread look slightly smaller than it is.)

A histogram shows the whole picture: divide the range into bins and count how many values fall into each. A tall narrow histogram means small spread; a low wide one means large spread. The median is the middle value when the data are sorted, which is less affected by a single wild value than the mean is.

4. More samples, smaller error#

Suppose each measurement has a random error with spread σ. If you average N independent measurements, the average is a more reliable estimate, because random highs and lows partly cancel. How much better? The uncertainty of the average, called the standard error, is

standard error = σ / √N

So with σ = 10 and N = 25 measurements, the standard error is 10/5 = 2; with N = 100 it is 1. This square-root law has an important consequence: to cut the error in half you need four times as many measurements, and to cut it by ten you need a hundred times as many. Gains get expensive, so decide how accurate you need to be before starting.

5. The bell curve#

When you plot the histogram of many random errors, or of averages of many samples, the shape is almost always the same: a symmetric hump called the normal distribution, or bell curve. There is a reason, known as the central limit theorem: when a result is the sum of many small, independent random effects, the total tends to a bell shape whatever the individual effects look like. That is why the bell curve is everywhere.

The bell curve has well-known proportions, in terms of the mean and the standard deviation σ:

  • about 68% of values fall within ±1σ of the mean,
  • about 95% within ±1.96σ (often rounded to 2σ),
  • about 99.7% within ±3σ.

For an average, use the standard error in place of σ. If you measure a length 100 times and find a mean of 12.34 mm with a standard error of 0.05 mm, then you can say it is 12.34 ± 0.05 mm (68% confidence) or 12.34 ± 0.10 mm (about 95% confidence). Always say which one you mean.

6. Random walks#

Here is a chance process that will return in the diffusion topics. A walker stands on a line and at each step moves one step left or right, decided by a coin flip. After N steps, where is the walker? On average, exactly where it started, because left and right are equally likely. But it is not likely to be at the start: the typical distance away grows as the square root of the number of steps, √N. After 10 000 steps the walker is typically about 100 steps from home, not 10 000.

This is the same square-root law as for the standard error, and it is no coincidence: the position is the sum of many independent random steps. Many atoms taking random hops produce diffusion, the spreading of one substance into another, and that is why diffusion distance is proportional to the square root of time. You will see a crowd of random walkers produce diffusion in Your first simulation.

7. Reporting honestly#

  • Always give an uncertainty. A number without an error bar cannot be compared with anything. State what it represents (standard deviation, standard error, or a 95% range).
  • Random versus systematic error. Averaging reduces random error but cannot remove a systematic one: a thermometer that reads 3 degrees too high gives a wrong answer however many times you average. In simulations the systematic error might be a poor model; more runs shrink the noise but not the model error, so the answer converges to the wrong number with a very small error bar.
  • Beware of small samples. Three measurements tell you very little about the spread. Be wary of conclusions from a handful of data points.
  • Outliers. A single wildly different value can drag the mean. Investigate it; do not silently throw it away or silently keep it.
  • Correlation is not causation. Two quantities rising together does not mean one causes the other; both may depend on a third.
  • Do not cherry-pick. Report all the runs, not only the one that looked best.
Key takeaways
  • Probability measures how likely an outcome is, from 0 (never) to 1 (always); independent chances multiply.
  • The mean says where data centre; the standard deviation says how spread out they are.
  • The error of an average falls as one over the square root of the number of samples: four times as many for half the error.
  • Sums of many small random effects form a bell curve, which is why it appears everywhere.
  • Always report an uncertainty: a number without an error bar is a guess.

8. Check your understanding#

What is the chance of flipping heads three times in a row with a fair coin?

The flips are independent, so multiply: 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8 = 0.125.

Find the mean and standard deviation of 4, 5, 6, 7, 8.

Mean 6. Squared deviations sum to 10; 10/(5 − 1) = 2.5; σ = √2.5 = 1.58.

Individual measurements have σ = 10. What is the standard error of the average of 25?

σ/√N = 10/√25 = 10/5 = 2.

You want to cut a standard error from 2 down to 0.5. How many times more data do you need?

The error must fall by a factor of 4, and since it falls as 1/√N, you need 42 = 16 times as many measurements.

A random walker takes 10 000 steps of one unit, left or right at random. About how far from the start is it likely to be?

Typically about √10 000 = 100 units.

An instrument reads 3 units too high. You average 1000 readings. What is wrong with the result?

The random noise is reduced, but the average is still 3 units too high. That is a systematic error, which averaging cannot remove; the instrument must be calibrated.
