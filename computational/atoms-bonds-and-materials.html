Atoms, bonds and materials from scratch

Why is a diamond hard and graphite soft when both are pure carbon? Why does a metal bend instead of shatter? The answers lie in how atoms are bonded and arranged. This page builds that picture from the beginning, with two interactive tools to explore it.

1. Everything is atoms#

Every material, whether steel, wood, water or air, is built from atoms. There are about a hundred different kinds, each a different element: iron, carbon, oxygen, silicon and so on. An atom is incredibly small, roughly 0.1 to 0.3 nanometres across, which is a few tenths of a billionth of a metre. A single pinch of iron weighing one gram contains about 1022 of them, that is 10 000 000 000 000 000 000 000.

To count such numbers chemists use the mole: one mole is 6.022 × 1023 items (the Avogadro number). The mass of one mole of an element in grams, its molar mass, is a number you can read off the periodic table: iron 55.85 g/mol, copper 63.55, aluminium 26.98, carbon 12.01. So the number of atoms in 1 g of iron is (1/55.85) × 6.022 × 1023 ≈ 1.08 × 1022.

What a material is like depends on two things: which atoms it contains, and how they are arranged and held together. Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon; the only difference is the arrangement, and the properties are completely different.

2. Inside an atom#

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus at the centre, made of protons (positive electric charge) and neutrons (no charge). Around it is a cloud of electrons (negative charge, much lighter than protons). The nucleus is only about 10−15 m across, 100 000 times smaller than the atom: almost all of an atom’s size is the electron cloud, and almost all of its mass is the nucleus.

  • The number of protons, the atomic number, decides which element it is: 6 for carbon, 8 for oxygen, 26 for iron. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.
  • Electrons are arranged in shells at different distances. The electrons in the outermost shell, the valence electrons, are the ones that take part in bonding, and the number of them decides the atom’s chemistry.
  • The periodic table lists elements by atomic number and puts atoms with similar valence electrons in the same column. That is why sodium and potassium behave alike, and why the metals fill the left and middle of the table.

The rules governing electrons come from quantum mechanics. You do not need them yet: it is enough to know that electrons are what hold atoms together.

3. Why atoms stick together#

Two atoms attract each other because the positive nucleus of one pulls on the negative electrons of the other. But if they get too close their electron clouds and nuclei repel strongly. The balance of attraction and repulsion produces a preferred distance, the bond length, where the energy is lowest, just as a ball settles at the bottom of a valley. The bond energy (or well depth) is the energy needed to pull the atoms apart; typical values are 0.5 to 8 eV per atom.

Two numbers describe a bond. The strength is the depth of the valley: stronger bonds mean materials that melt at higher temperatures and are harder to break. The stiffness is how steep the sides of the valley are: stiff bonds resist being stretched or squeezed, so a material with stiff bonds is hard to bend elastically. Make the bond stronger with the second slider and notice that the valley deepens and gets steeper; in general strong bonds are stiff bonds.

Materials are commonly grouped into four classes by their bonding and behaviour, plus combinations:

  • Metals (steel, aluminium, copper): strong, tough, conduct electricity and heat.
  • Ceramics and glasses (alumina, concrete, window glass): hard, brittle, resist heat and corrosion, insulate.
  • Polymers (polyethylene, nylon, rubber): long chain molecules; light, flexible, easy to shape, insulating.
  • Semiconductors (silicon, gallium arsenide): conduct only a little, in a way that can be controlled, which is the basis of electronics.
  • Composites (carbon fibre in resin): combine two or more to get properties that none has alone.

5. How atoms arrange in solids#

In a crystal the atoms sit in a regular pattern that repeats in all directions, like oranges stacked in a crate. The smallest repeating block is the unit cell. Many common metals take one of three simple patterns: face-centred cubic (aluminium, copper, and iron at high temperature), body-centred cubic (iron at room temperature, tungsten) and hexagonal close-packed (magnesium, titanium). Each has a different density of packing and different ways of deforming, which is why iron and copper behave differently.

A glass has no repeating pattern: the atoms are jumbled like a frozen liquid. Many polymers are partly crystalline and partly jumbled. And most real metals are polycrystals: they are made of millions of tiny crystals called grains, each with the atoms tilted differently, meeting along surfaces called grain boundaries. The pattern of grains and other features, visible in a microscope at micrometre scale, is the microstructure, and it is one of the most important things engineers control.

6. Imperfections#

A perfect crystal would be a poor guide to a real material. Real crystals always contain defects, and defects control many useful properties.

  • A vacancy is an empty site where an atom is missing. At any temperature a few sites are always empty, and vacancies are what allow atoms to move around inside a solid by hopping into the gaps.
  • An interstitial atom is an atom squeezed into the space between the normal sites; small atoms like carbon sit this way in iron.
  • A solute atom (or substitutional atom) replaces a normal atom with a different element. Mixing elements in this way gives an alloy.
  • A dislocation is a line defect: imagine an extra half-sheet of atoms pushed into the crystal, like an extra card in a deck. When a metal bends permanently, dislocations glide through the crystal one row at a time, a bit like moving a ruck across a carpet instead of dragging the whole carpet. This is why metals deform at far smaller forces than you would calculate by pulling whole planes of atoms apart.
  • A grain boundary, as described above, is a surface defect between grains.

How many vacancies are there? The fraction of empty sites in equilibrium is e−E/(kT), where E is the energy cost of making one (the exponential from the graphs page, with k and T to be explained soon). For a typical cost of 1 eV at 1000 K that fraction is about 10−5: one site in a hundred thousand. It is tiny but vital, and it rises very steeply with temperature.

7. From atoms to properties#

Properties that you can measure in the lab come from the atomic picture:

PropertyWhat controls it at the atomic level
Stiffness (how much it stretches under a load)Bond stiffness: steep valleys in the energy curve
Melting pointBond strength compared with the thermal jiggling of atoms
Electrical conductivityWhether electrons are free to move (metals) or bound (ceramics)
Strength and ductilityHow easily dislocations move and how many obstacles there are
DensityAtomic mass and how tightly the atoms pack
Corrosion resistanceChemical reactions at the surface and the films that form there

Take steel, the world’s most important engineering material. Pure iron is soft because dislocations glide easily. Adding a little carbon, which sits between the iron atoms, puts obstacles in the way of dislocations, so the material becomes much stronger. Heat treatment then controls the size of the grains and the tiny particles that form, and the strength can change by a factor of several. Understanding and predicting all this from atoms upward is what computational materials science is about.

8. Why simulate#

We simulate because we cannot see inside a material at the atomic scale in real time, because experiments are slow and costly, and because the number of possible compositions and treatments is far too large to test one by one. A simulation can show a dislocation moving, or tell us whether an alloy will be stable, before anyone makes it. It does not replace experiments; it helps decide which ones to do and explains what they find.

Key takeaways
  • Everything is made of atoms; properties come from which atoms there are and how they are arranged and bonded.
  • Bonds balance attraction and repulsion, giving a preferred spacing and a stiffness; deeper bonds mean higher melting points.
  • Crystals are repeating patterns; real crystals contain defects such as vacancies, solute atoms and dislocations.
  • Defects, not perfect order, explain why metals bend, conduct and can be strengthened by alloying.
  • We simulate because we cannot see atoms directly and cannot afford to test every composition.

9. Check your understanding#

How many atoms are in 1 g of aluminium (molar mass 26.98 g/mol)?

(1/26.98) × 6.022 × 1023 ≈ 2.2 × 1022 atoms.

Why does pulling two bonded atoms slightly apart make them pull back?

Beyond the bond length the attraction between the nucleus of one atom and the electrons of the other is stronger than the repulsion, so the net force points back toward the bond length. The force is the slope of the energy curve and always points toward the bottom of the valley.

Which type of bond involves atoms sharing pairs of electrons in definite directions?

Covalent bonding. This directionality is why covalent solids such as diamond and silicon are hard and brittle.

If a material’s bonds are made stronger, what generally happens to its stiffness and melting point?

Both tend to increase: a deeper, steeper energy valley resists stretching (more stiffness) and takes more thermal jiggling to overcome (higher melting point).

Why are alloys often stronger than the pure metal?

The foreign atoms distort the crystal and act as obstacles that dislocations have to push past, so more force is needed to deform the metal permanently.

What fraction of atomic sites is vacant at 1000 K if a vacancy costs 1 eV? (Use kT = 0.0862 eV at 1000 K.)

The fraction is e−1/0.0862 = e−11.6 ≈ 9 × 10−6, about one site in a hundred thousand.
