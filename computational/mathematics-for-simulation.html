Mathematics for simulation

Simulation looks like a wall of equations, but a small set of mathematical ideas does most of the work. This page collects them with one aim: so that when a later topic writes a gradient, an eigenvalue problem or a standard error, you know what it means and why it appears.

1. How much maths is needed#

First-year university mathematics (calculus, linear algebra and introductory statistics) is enough to follow every topic in this series. What matters is fluency with a few ideas rather than a long list of techniques. If you are starting from school-level mathematics, read Numbers, units and graphs and Rates of change first: they build the same ideas from scratch. The table maps each idea to where it returns.

2. Calculus and vector calculus#

Derivatives and Taylor series#

The derivative f′(x) is the slope of a function, and a function near a point can be approximated by its Taylor series:

f(x + h) = f(x) + h f′(x) + ½ h² f″(x) + ⅙ h³ f′′′(x) + …

Truncating this series is the source of two of the most used approximations in simulation. Near an energy minimum the first derivative vanishes, leaving the harmonic approximation U(x) ≈ U0 + ½k(x − x0)² with stiffness k = U″(x0); and replacing derivatives by differences of nearby values gives the finite-difference methods of the numerical methods page, whose error is set by the first neglected Taylor term.

For functions of several variables, partial derivatives ∂f/∂x hold the other variables fixed, and the chain rule links them: df = (∂f/∂x) dx + (∂f/∂y) dy. Thermodynamics is full of partial derivatives (for example μi = (∂G/∂ni) at fixed T and p), and the subscripts say what is held fixed.

Gradient, divergence and Laplacian#

Three operators built from ∇ = (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z) cover most of the continuum physics used here.

OperatorActs onMeaningWhere it is used
Gradient ∇fscalar fieldVector pointing uphill with the steepest slopeForce F = −∇U; diffusion flux J = −D∇c; chemical-potential gradients
Divergence ∇·Jvector fieldNet outflow per unit volumeConservation laws: ∂c/∂t + ∇·J = 0
Laplacian ∇²f = ∇·∇fscalar fieldHow much a value differs from the average of its neighbours (curvature)Diffusion, heat flow, Poisson and Schrödinger equations, gradient-energy terms
Curl ∇×vvector fieldLocal rotationElectromagnetism, dislocation theory (less common here)

Combining the first two gives Fick’s second law in one line: substituting J = −D∇c into the continuity equation yields ∂c/∂t = ∇·(D∇c). The divergence theorem, ∫V ∇·F dV = ∮S F·n dS, says that what flows out through a surface equals the sum of sources inside. It is the reason conservation laws can be written for a small cell, and the reason the finite-volume and finite-element methods work.

Integrals worth remembering#

  • The Gaussian integral ∫−∞∞ e−x² dx = √π underlies the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution and the diffusion kernel.
  • Averages over a distribution are integrals, ⟨A⟩ = ∫ A(x) p(x) dx, the form that every ensemble average in statistical mechanics takes.
  • The Dirac delta δ(x) represents an idealised point (a point source, a point charge, a sharp interface) with ∫ δ(x) dx = 1.

3. Linear algebra and tensors#

Vectors, matrices and coordinate changes#

A matrix is a linear map, and matrix multiplication is composition of maps (so, in general, AB ≠ BA). The determinant of a matrix whose columns are three lattice vectors is the cell volume, V = a1·(a2×a3) = det[a1 a2 a3]. Converting fractional coordinates f to Cartesian positions is a matrix product, r = Af, and the reciprocal lattice vectors are the columns of 2π(A−1)T (see crystal structures and periodic cells). A rotation of axes is an orthogonal matrix R with RTR = I and det R = +1, which preserves lengths and angles.

Eigenvalue problems#

An eigenvector of a matrix A is a direction that A only stretches, by a factor called the eigenvalue:

A v = λ v

For a real symmetric matrix the eigenvalues are real and the eigenvectors can be chosen orthogonal, so A = QΛQT with Q orthogonal and Λ diagonal: in the right axes the matrix is just a set of independent stretches. This one structure explains many results:

  • Principal stresses. The stress tensor is a symmetric 3×3 matrix; its eigenvalues are the principal stresses and its eigenvectors the principal directions. The von Mises stress and the maximum shear stress follow from them.
  • Vibrations. For a crystal near equilibrium, Newton’s equations reduce to an eigenvalue problem for the dynamical matrix, with eigenvalues ω² (the squared vibration frequencies) and eigenvectors giving the atomic motions of each normal mode.
  • Electrons. The Schrödinger equation, once expanded in a basis, becomes Hc = Ec (or Hc = ESc with an overlap matrix S); the eigenvalues are the energy levels. Diagonalising this matrix is the main cost of DFT.
  • Stability. At a stationary point of an energy landscape, the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix of second derivatives decide stability: all positive means a minimum (stable); one negative means a saddle point (a transition state); a negative eigenvalue of the dynamical matrix is an imaginary frequency.
  • Data. Principal component analysis diagonalises a covariance matrix and keeps the directions of largest variance.

Tensors and index notation#

A tensor is the generalisation of scalars (rank 0) and vectors (rank 1). Stress σij and strain εij are rank-2 tensors; the elastic stiffness Cijkl linking them is rank 4. With the Einstein convention, a repeated index is summed over:

σij = Cijkl εkl   (sum over k and l)

A rank-4 tensor has 81 components, but symmetry of stress and strain and the existence of a strain energy reduce the number of independent constants to 21 in the most general (triclinic) crystal, 5 for hexagonal and 3 for cubic. The compact Voigt notation writes the symmetric stress and strain as six-component vectors and C as a 6×6 matrix.

4. Differential equations#

A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives. Solving one means finding the function; nearly every model in this series is one.

Ordinary differential equations#

An ODE has one independent variable, usually time. Two cases cover most needs:

  • First-order decay, dc/dt = −kc, has the solution c = c0e−kt: a process whose rate is proportional to what remains, such as a first-order reaction, relaxation to equilibrium or radioactive decay. The time constant is 1/k.
  • The harmonic oscillator, m d²x/dt² = −kx, has oscillatory solutions x = A cos(ωt + φ) with ω = √(k/m). It describes every small vibration about equilibrium.

Newton’s equations for N atoms are 3N coupled second-order ODEs, which is what molecular dynamics integrates in time.

Partial differential equations#

A PDE has several independent variables (space and time). Three prototypes are enough to recognise the rest:

TypePrototypeBehaviourMaterials example
Parabolic∂c/∂t = D∇²cSmooths and spreads; irreversible in timeDiffusion, heat conduction, the Allen–Cahn equation
Hyperbolic∂²u/∂t² = v²∇²uWaves travel at finite speed, with no smoothingElastic waves, impact and explosion problems
Elliptic∇²φ = −ρ/ε0Steady state: the answer in one place depends on the whole boundaryElectrostatics, steady heat flow, static equilibrium of a solid

The Schrödinger equation is parabolic-like in time (with an imaginary coefficient, so it oscillates instead of decaying), and its time-independent form is an eigenvalue problem.

Conditions that make the problem well posed#

A PDE alone has infinitely many solutions. A unique one needs initial conditions (the state at t = 0) and boundary conditions on the edge of the domain: Dirichlet (the value is prescribed, such as a fixed temperature or a surface at fixed concentration), Neumann (the flux is prescribed, with zero flux meaning an insulated or symmetry boundary), Robin (flux proportional to the difference from the surroundings, as in convective cooling) and periodic (the domain wraps around, as in a simulation cell).

Characteristic times and modes#

Dividing the equation by its natural scales gives a quick estimate: diffusion across a length L takes a time of order τ ≈ L²/D. More precisely, the diffusion equation has solutions of the form eik·re−Dk²t: a fluctuation of wavenumber k decays at the rate Dk², so short-wavelength features vanish fastest. The same mode analysis shows how a spinodal pattern selects its length scale (see phase-field modelling). Equations with several very different time scales are called stiff, and they strongly constrain numerical methods.

5. Fourier analysis#

Any reasonable periodic function can be written as a sum of sines and cosines, or equivalently of complex exponentials. For a function with period L:

f(x) = Σn cn ei kn x,   kn = 2πn / L,   cn = (1/L) ∫0L f(x) e−i kn x dx

The coefficient cn says how much of wavenumber kn is present. Three consequences matter later.

  • Derivatives become multiplication. Differentiating eikx gives ikeikx, so ∂/∂x → ik and ∇² → −k². Spectral solvers (the semi-implicit scheme in the phase-field page) exploit this to handle the Laplacian exactly.
  • A periodic simulation cell has a discrete set of wavenumbers, k = 2πn/L, and none smaller than 2π/L. That is the origin of the finite-size limit on correlations, and the same reasoning gives plane-wave basis sets and the Brillouin zone in solids.
  • The fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes all coefficients of N samples in about N log N operations instead of N², which is why plane-wave DFT and spectral methods are practical.

Sharp features need many terms: the Fourier series of a square wave overshoots by roughly 9% of the jump height near the discontinuity however many terms are kept (the Gibbs phenomenon), which is why discontinuities cause ringing in spectral and plane-wave methods. The Fourier transform is the limit for non-periodic functions, f̂(k) = ∫ f(x)e−ikx dx. Diffraction patterns are the Fourier transform of the electron-density or scattering distribution in the sample, which is how the structure factor links simulated and measured structures.

6. Probability and statistics#

Random variables and distributions#

A random variable X has a probability density p(x), a mean μ = ⟨X⟩ and a variance σ² = ⟨(X − μ)²⟩. Four distributions recur:

  • Uniform: all values in a range equally likely; the basis of random-number generators.
  • Gaussian (normal), p(x) = exp[−(x − μ)²/2σ²]/(σ√(2π)): measurement noise, velocities of atoms in equilibrium, and the limit of sums (below).
  • Exponential: waiting times between independent random events with a constant rate R, p(t) = Re−Rt, the basis of kinetic Monte Carlo.
  • Poisson: the number of independent rare events in a fixed interval, such as counted defects or detected photons, with variance equal to the mean.

Averages and error bars#

The central limit theorem says that the average of N independent samples is approximately Gaussian with mean μ and standard deviation σ/√N. That last quantity is the standard error, and about 95% of the time the true mean lies within ±1.96 standard errors of the sample average. Two practical rules follow: to halve the error bar you need four times as many independent samples, and correlated samples (successive steps of a simulation) count as fewer than N independent ones (see statistical mechanics).

Least squares and propagation of error#

Fitting a model f(x; θ) to data (xi, yi) by least squares means choosing the parameters that minimise Σ(yi − f(xi; θ))². For a straight line this has a closed form (the normal equations). A very common use in materials work is the Arrhenius plot: since D = D0exp(−Q/RT), a straight-line fit of ln D against 1/T gives slope −Q/R and intercept ln D0. Always plot the residuals, and quote parameter uncertainties from the fit.

If a result f depends on independent measurements xi with uncertainties σi, the uncertainty in f propagates as

σf² ≈ Σi ( ∂f/∂xi )² σi²

so a quantity that depends exponentially on a barrier amplifies the barrier error: a 0.1 eV error in Q changes exp(−Q/kBT) by a factor of about 3 at 1000 K.

Bayes’ theorem and Markov chains#

Bayes’ theorem, P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B), says how to update a belief about A after observing B. It is the logic behind Bayesian fitting and the surrogate models used in active learning. A Markov chain is a random sequence in which the next state depends only on the current one; under suitable conditions it settles into a stationary distribution, which is the idea behind Metropolis Monte Carlo.

7. Scaling and estimation#

Before running any calculation, estimate the answer. Dimensional analysis says that physical laws can only depend on dimensionless combinations of the variables. Two appear constantly: the Fourier number Fo = Dt/L² (whether diffusion has had time to act across a length L: values near or above 1 mean yes) and the Biot number Bi = hL/k (whether a body has uniform temperature, or the surface heat transfer limits cooling: Bi below about 0.1 means near-uniform temperature). Writing an equation in dimensionless form reduces the number of independent parameters and shows which effects matter.

A small set of orders of magnitude makes estimates routine.

QuantityTypical value
Interatomic spacing in solids0.25–0.30 nm
Atomic number density of a metalabout 6–9 × 1028 m−3 (so about 1011 atoms in 1 µm3)
Atomic vibration frequencyabout 1013 Hz (period about 100 fs)
Thermal energy kBT at 300 K25.9 meV (about 2.5 kJ/mol)
Longitudinal sound speed in steelabout 5.9 km/s
Avogadro number6.022 × 1023 mol−1

Use them to check feasibility. A cube holding one million atoms of a metal has a side of about 23 nm; the time for diffusion to cross 1 µm with D = 10−14 m²/s is L²/D = 100 s, which already shows why that process needs a coarser model than atoms.

Key takeaways
  • Most simulation maths reduces to a few ideas: gradients and divergences, eigenvalue problems, differential equations, Fourier modes and averages over random variables.
  • Forces are gradients of energy (F = -grad U), fluxes are gradients of potentials (J = -D grad c), and conservation laws are divergence equations.
  • Many physical quantities are eigenvalues of matrices: principal stresses, vibration frequencies and electronic energies.
  • A differential equation needs initial and boundary conditions, and its character (diffusive, wave-like, oscillatory) decides the numerical method.
  • Plane waves and the Fourier transform connect real space and reciprocal space, and the standard error of an average falls as one over the square root of the number of independent samples.

8. Check your understanding#

A potential is U(x, y) = x² + 3y². What is the force at (1, 2)?

F = −∇U = (−2x, −6y), which at (1, 2) is (−2, −12). The force points toward the minimum at the origin and is larger along y, where the potential is steeper.

A diffusion problem has L = 100 µm and D = 10−12 m²/s. About how long does the profile take to relax?

τ ≈ L²/D = (10−4)²/10−12 = 104 s, a little under three hours.

You average 10,000 correlated samples with a correlation time of 50 steps. What is the standard error compared with σ?

The number of independent samples is about N/(2τ) = 10 000/100 = 100, so the standard error is about σ/√100 = σ/10, not σ/100.
