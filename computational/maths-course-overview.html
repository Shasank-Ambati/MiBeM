Maths course: overview and study plan

Every method in this series, from density functional theory to phase-field modelling, is built from a small set of mathematical ideas. This course teaches those ideas at the level of a university science or engineering degree, always with the materials context in view: not a list of formulas, but the concepts explained, derived and then used, followed by problems you solve yourself with every step of the solution shown.

2. Choosing a path#

The modules are written to be read in order, M1 to M12, and doing so is the most complete route. If you are short of time, take the path for your goal; each module states what it assumes, so you can fill gaps as they appear.

3. How to get the most from the problems#

  1. Read actively. After each worked example, cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, read it again; do not move on.
  2. Attempt every problem on paper first, for at least ten minutes, before opening the hint. The struggle is where the learning happens.
  3. Use the hint, then try again. Open the full solution only once you have a genuine attempt to compare. When you read it, check each line rather than skimming.
  4. Check numbers with a computer. Most problems can be verified in a few lines of Python with NumPy (matrices, eigenvalues, integrals, fits); doing so is also the best way to learn the numerical side.
  5. Keep an error log. Note each mistake (a sign, a missing Jacobian, a unit) and why; the same few errors recur.
  6. Return after a week to the problems you ticked and redo two or three without notes.

Basic problems test a technique, core problems apply it to a materials setting, and challenge problems combine several ideas or derive a result you will later rely on. You do not need to solve every challenge problem on a first pass.

4. A placement check#

One question per module. Try each on paper; open the answer to check. Whenever you cannot answer one quickly, start that module (and read earlier ones if its prerequisites feel shaky).

M1. Differentiate $f(x)=\ln\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)$.

Chain rule: $f'(x)=\dfrac{2e^{2x}}{1+e^{2x}}$. (Tends to $2$ for large $x$ and to $0$ for very negative $x$.)

M2. For $f(x,y)=x^2y+y^3$, find $\nabla f$ at $(1,2)$.

$\nabla f=(2xy,\ x^2+3y^2)=(4,\ 13)$.

M3. Find the divergence and curl of $\mathbf F=(-y,\,x,\,0)$.

$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,0,2)$: a pure rotation with no sources.

M4. Compute $\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&3\end{pmatrix}$ and say what it means geometrically.

$2\cdot3-1\cdot4=2$: the matrix scales areas by $2$ and preserves orientation.

M5. Find the eigenvalues of $\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$.

$\lambda^2-6\lambda+8=0$, so $\lambda=4$ (eigenvector $(1,1)$) and $\lambda=2$ (eigenvector $(1,-1)$).

M6. A bar is in uniaxial tension, $\sigma_x=100$ MPa and all other stress components zero. What is the maximum shear stress?

$\tau_\mathrm{max}=\tfrac12(\sigma_1-\sigma_3)=\tfrac12(100-0)=50$ MPa, on planes at $45^\circ$ to the axis.

M7. Solve $\dot y=-3y$ with $y(0)=2$, and find the half-life.

$y=2e^{-3t}$; the half-life is $\ln2/3=0.23$.

M8. Write $\cos\theta$ in terms of complex exponentials.

$\cos\theta=\tfrac12\bigl(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}\bigr)$, and $|e^{i\theta}|=1$.

M9. What type of PDE is $u_t=Du_{xx}$, and how does the diffusion distance depend on time?

Parabolic. The distance scales as $\sqrt{Dt}$, so doubling it takes four times as long.

M10. A fair die is thrown. Give the mean and variance of the score.

Mean $3.5$; variance $\langle X^2\rangle-\langle X\rangle^2=91/6-49/4=35/12=2.92$.

M11. Five independent measurements have sample standard deviation $0.20$. What is the standard error of their mean?

$0.20/\sqrt5=0.089$. (For a 95% interval with only $4$ degrees of freedom use the $t$ factor $2.78$, not $1.96$.)

M12. Find the Euler–Lagrange equation of $\int(\tfrac12y'^2-gy)\,\mathrm dx$.

$\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}y'-(-g)=0$, i.e. $y''=-g$: a parabola, the shape of a loaded string.

5. Notation and conventions#

SymbolMeaning
$x$, $\mathbf r$, $\mathbf F$, $\mathbf v$scalars in italics; vectors in bold; the unit vector is $\hat{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r/r$
$A$, $\mathsf H$, $\sigma_{ij}$matrices and tensors; $A^T$ transpose; $A^{-1}$ inverse; $I$ identity
$\langle X\rangle$, $\mathrm{Var}\,X$, $\sigma$average (expectation), variance, standard deviation; $\sigma$ is also a stress, with the meaning clear from context
$\partial_x f$, $f_x$, $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$partial derivatives; $\dot y=\mathrm dy/\mathrm dt$; $y'=\mathrm dy/\mathrm dx$
$\nabla$, $\nabla\cdot$, $\nabla\times$, $\nabla^2$gradient, divergence, curl, Laplacian
$k_\mathrm{B}$, $\beta=1/k_\mathrm{B}T$Boltzmann constant ($8.617\times10^{-5}$ eV/K) and inverse temperature
$\mathbf G$, $\mathbf b_i$, $\mathbf a_i$reciprocal lattice vectors, reciprocal and direct lattice basis vectors, with $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$
$F(k)=\int f(x)e^{-ikx}\mathrm dx$Fourier transform convention (inverse has $1/2\pi$)

Sign conventions: tension is positive in stress; forces are $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$; angles in radians unless marked with a degree sign; $\ln$ is the natural logarithm. Numbers in solutions are rounded for display, so expect small differences in the last digit if you recompute.

Key takeaways
  • The course has twelve modules, each with explained concepts, derivations, worked examples, interactive figures and twelve graded problems with hints and full solutions.
  • Read in order if you can; otherwise follow a goal-based path (electronic structure, atomistic simulation, continuum and phase-field, or data and machine learning).
  • Learning happens in the problems: attempt each on paper first, then use the hint, and only then read the solution.
  • Your ticks are stored only in your browser; the table below counts them per module.

7. Where to go next#

With the course behind you, the rest of the series reads as applications. For a quick refresher on all of the mathematics in one place, see B1. Mathematics for simulation. The methods themselves start with the introduction, and the topic map shows how everything connects.

  • Electrons and atoms: density functional theory, molecular dynamics, Monte Carlo methods.
  • Phases and microstructure: CALPHAD, diffusion and kinetics, phase-field modelling.
  • Components and data: finite element method, machine learning for materials.
