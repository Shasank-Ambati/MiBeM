Tensors: stress, strain and anisotropy

Materials respond to loads, heat flow and fields in ways that depend on direction. Stress, strain, elastic stiffness, thermal conductivity, thermal expansion and the diffusion matrix are all tensors: objects whose components change in a precise way when you rotate your axes, and which relate one directional quantity to another. This module teaches the language of tensors (index notation and transformations), applies it to stress and strain, builds Hooke’s law for crystals and isotropic solids, and shows how crystal symmetry cuts the number of independent material constants.

Lecture 1. What a tensor is#

A scalar (temperature, density) has one value independent of axes. A vector (force, flux, displacement) has three components that change when you rotate the axes. A second-rank tensor has nine components $T_{ij}$ and connects two vectors linearly: if $\mathbf b=T\mathbf a$ then $b_i=T_{ij}a_j$. The central examples are:

  • the stress $\sigma_{ij}$, which maps a surface normal to the force per area on that surface;
  • the strain $\varepsilon_{ij}$, which maps a displacement direction to its relative stretch;
  • the thermal conductivity $\kappa_{ij}$, with heat flux $q_i=-\kappa_{ij}\,\partial_jT$, so that heat does not necessarily flow along the temperature gradient in an anisotropic crystal; likewise the diffusion tensor $D_{ij}$ with $J_i=-D_{ij}\partial_jc$;
  • the thermal expansion tensor $\alpha_{ij}$, with strain $\varepsilon_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}\,\allowbreak \Delta T$.

Higher-rank tensors link tensors to tensors: the elastic stiffness $C_{ijkl}$ (rank 4) links stress to strain, and the piezoelectric tensor $d_{ijk}$ (rank 3) links an electric field to strain. What makes an array a tensor is its behaviour under a change of axes (section 3).

Why the trace is an invariant

Under a rotation $T'=QTQ^T$ with $Q^TQ=I$. Then $\operatorname{tr}T'=\operatorname{tr}(QTQ^T)=\operatorname{tr}(Q^TQT)=\operatorname{tr}T$, using the cyclic property of the trace. In index form, $T'_{ii}=Q_{ik}Q_{il}T_{kl}=\delta_{kl}T_{kl}=T_{kk}$. The same cyclic argument shows $\operatorname{tr}(T'^2)=\operatorname{tr}(T^2)$ and $\det T'=\det T$, which proves that $I_1,I_2,I_3$ are invariants.

Lecture 2. Index notation#

The Einstein summation convention: an index repeated twice in a product is summed over $1,2,3$ and is called dummy; an index that appears once is free and labels a component. So $a_ib_i=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$; $T_{ij}a_j=\sum_jT_{ij}a_j$ is the $i$-th component of $T\mathbf a$; $T_{ii}=\operatorname{tr}T$; $A_{ik}B_{kj}$ is the $(ij)$ entry of $AB$. Two special objects make most manipulations short:

The double contraction $A:B=A_{ij}B_{ij}$ gives a scalar (it is the sum of all products of corresponding entries), used for energy densities.

Lecture 3. Transformation under rotation#

Let a new set of axes $\mathbf e'_i$ be related to the old $\mathbf e_j$ by the direction cosines $Q_{ij}=\mathbf e'_i\cdot\mathbf e_j$, an orthogonal matrix ($QQ^T=I$). For a rotation of the axes by an angle $\theta$ anticlockwise about $z$, $$Q=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta&0\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$ The components of the same physical object in the new axes are $$a'_i=Q_{ij}a_j,\qquad T'_{ij}=Q_{ik}Q_{jl}T_{kl}\ \ (\text{i.e. }T'=QTQ^T),\qquad C'_{ijkl}=Q_{ip}Q_{jq}Q_{kr}Q_{ls}C_{pqrs}.$$ This rule defines a tensor: each index transforms like a vector index. Quantities that do not change are invariants (scalars): for a second-rank tensor, $I_1=T_{ii}=\operatorname{tr}T$, $I_2=\tfrac12\bigl[(\operatorname{tr}T)^2-\operatorname{tr}(T^2)\bigr]$ (the sum of principal $2\times2$ minors) and $I_3=\det T$, the same functions of the eigenvalues in every frame. A symmetric second-rank tensor can always be rotated to a frame in which it is diagonal, with the eigenvalues as entries (M5): its principal axes.

Worked example 3.1: rotating a second-rank tensor

The tensor $T=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ is given in axes $x,y$. Find its components in axes rotated $30^\circ$ anticlockwise, and check the invariants.

With $c=\cos30^\circ=0.8660$, $s=\sin30^\circ=0.5$: $T'_{11}=c^2T_{11}+2csT_{12}+s^2T_{22}=2.25+0.866+0.5=3.616$; $T'_{22}=s^2T_{11}-2csT_{12}+c^2T_{22}=0.75-0.866+1.5=1.384$; $T'_{12}=-cs\,\allowbreak T_{11}+(c^2-s^2)T_{12}+cs\,\allowbreak T_{22}=-1.299+0.5+0.866=0.067$. So $T'=\begin{pmatrix}3.616&0.067\\0.067&1.384\end{pmatrix}$. Trace: $3.616+1.384=5=3+2$ ✓. Determinant: $5.0049-0.0045=5.000=6-1$ ✓. The eigenvalues are $(5\pm\sqrt5)/2=3.618,1.382$, and the principal axis lies at $\tfrac12\arctan\dfrac{2T_{12}}{T_{11}-T_{22}}=31.7^\circ$, very close to the $30^\circ$ chosen, which is why $T'$ is nearly diagonal.

Worked example 3.2: rotating a stress and finding the principal values

A plate carries $\sigma_x=80$, $\sigma_y=20$, $\tau_{xy}=40$ MPa in plane stress. Find the principal stresses, their direction, the maximum in-plane shear and the stress on a plane rotated $30^\circ$.

Centre of Mohr's circle $=\tfrac12(80+20)=50$ MPa; radius $R=\sqrt{30^2+40^2}=50$ MPa. So $\sigma_{1,2}=50\pm50=100$ and $0$ MPa, and the maximum in-plane shear is $R=50$ MPa. The principal direction satisfies $\tan2\theta_p=2\cdot40/(80-20)=4/3$, so $\theta_p=26.57^\circ$ from the $x$ axis (the direction of the $100$ MPa stress). At $\theta=30^\circ$: $\sigma_{x'}=50+30\cos60^\circ+40\sin60^\circ=50+15+34.64=99.64$ MPa and $\tau_{x'y'}=-30\sin60^\circ+40\cos60^\circ=-25.98+20=-5.98$ MPa, close to the principal state because $30^\circ$ is near $26.57^\circ$. Check with the circle: $(99.64-50)^2+5.98^2=2464+36=2500=R^2$ ✓.

Why the plane stresses lie on a circle

Write $c=\tfrac12(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)$, $a=\tfrac12(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)$. From the transformation formulas, $\sigma_{x'}-c=a\cos2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta$ and $\tau_{x'y'}=-a\sin2\theta+\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta$. Squaring and adding, the cross terms cancel and $(\sigma_{x'}-c)^2+\tau_{x'y'}^2=a^2+\tau_{xy}^2=R^2$. A physical rotation of $\theta$ moves the point by $2\theta$ round the circle, which is why Mohr's circle doubles angles.

Lecture 4. Stress#

Traction and the stress tensor#

Cut a body by an imaginary plane with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$. The force per unit area transmitted across the plane (the traction) is $$t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j,$$ where $\sigma_{ij}$ is the Cauchy stress tensor: $\sigma_{ij}$ is the $i$-th component of the force per area on the plane whose normal is the $j$-th axis. The diagonal entries are normal stresses (positive in tension in the convention used here) and the off-diagonal entries are shear stresses. Balance of torque on a small cube requires $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$ (absent body couples), so only six components are independent. Force balance on a small volume gives the equilibrium equation $\partial_j\sigma_{ij}+f_i=0$ with body force density $f_i$, the starting point of the finite element method (Finite element method). The normal and shear components on a plane with normal $\mathbf n$ are $\sigma_n=\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n$ and $\tau=\sqrt{|\mathbf t|^2-\sigma_n^2}$.

Principal stresses, Mohr’s circle and invariants#

Because $\sigma$ is symmetric, it has three real eigenvalues $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\sigma_3$ (the principal stresses) with orthogonal eigenvectors (principal directions), on which the shear vanishes. The maximum shear stress anywhere is $\tau_\mathrm{max}=\tfrac12(\sigma_1-\sigma_3)$, acting on planes at $45^\circ$ to the extreme principal directions. In two dimensions, with $\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\tau_{xy}$, the stress on a plane at angle $\theta$ is $$\sigma_{x'}=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2+\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\cos2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta,\qquad\tau_{x'y'}=-\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\sin2\theta+\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta,$$ and these trace a circle (Mohr’s circle) in the $(\sigma,\tau)$ plane with centre $\tfrac12(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)$ and radius $R=\sqrt{\bigl(\tfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\bigr)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}$; the principal stresses are $\tfrac12(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)\pm R$, at the angle $\tan2\theta_p=2\tau_{xy}/(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)$.

Hydrostatic and deviatoric parts, yield criteria#

Split the stress into a part that changes volume and a part that changes shape: $$\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_m\delta_{ij}+s_{ij},\qquad\sigma_m=\tfrac13\sigma_{kk}\ \text{(mean stress, equal to minus the pressure)},\quad s_{kk}=0 .$$ Plastic flow in metals is driven by shear, not pressure, so yield criteria use only the deviator $s_{ij}$. The von Mises equivalent stress and the Tresca criterion are $$\sigma_\mathrm{vM}=\sqrt{\tfrac32\,s_{ij}s_{ij}}=\sqrt{\tfrac12\bigl[(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2+(\sigma_2-\sigma_3)^2+(\sigma_3-\sigma_1)^2\bigr]},\qquad\sigma_\mathrm{Tresca}=\sigma_1-\sigma_3,$$ and yielding is predicted when either reaches the uniaxial yield stress $\sigma_Y$. For uniaxial tension both reduce to $\sigma_1$. Equal triaxial tension (as at a crack tip or in a thick plate) has $s_{ij}=0$ and $\sigma_\mathrm{vM}=0$, which is why such states promote brittle fracture without yielding.

Worked example 4.2: von Mises and Tresca

A steel with $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa carries principal stresses $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3)=(120,60,0)$ MPa. Does it yield?

$\sigma_\mathrm{vM}=\sqrt{\tfrac12[(120-60)^2+(60-0)^2+(0-120)^2]}=\sqrt{\tfrac12(3600+3600+14400)}=\sqrt{10800}=103.9$ MPa and $\sigma_\mathrm{Tresca}=120$ MPa, both well below $250$, so the steel stays elastic (safety factors $2.41$ and $2.08$). Tresca is the more conservative criterion, never smaller than von Mises: they agree for uniaxial tension and differ by at most $15\%$ (pure shear: $\sigma_\mathrm{vM}=\sqrt3\,\tau$ against $\sigma_\mathrm{Tresca}=2\tau$).

Lecture 5. Strain#

Deformation is described by the displacement field $\mathbf u(\mathbf x)$. Its gradient $\partial_ju_i$ contains rigid rotation, which stores no energy, and true deformation. Splitting into symmetric and antisymmetric parts, $$\partial_ju_i=\underbrace{\tfrac12(\partial_ju_i+\partial_iu_j)}_{\varepsilon_{ij}\ \text{(strain)}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12(\partial_ju_i-\partial_iu_j)}_{\omega_{ij}\ \text{(rotation)}} .$$ The small-strain tensor $\varepsilon_{ij}$ is symmetric. Its diagonal entries are fractional stretches along the axes; the off-diagonal entries are half the change in angle, so the engineering shear strain is $\gamma_{ij}=2\varepsilon_{ij}$. The trace $\varepsilon_{kk}=\Delta V/V$ is the volumetric strain. A strain with zero trace is pure shape change; $\varepsilon_{ij}=\tfrac13\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij}+e_{ij}$. Strain has principal values and axes exactly as stress does. A sanity check for a measured field is the compatibility condition (the six components of $\varepsilon$ cannot be arbitrary functions, since they derive from three displacement components).

When small strain is not enough

For rotations or stretches beyond about a percent, the linear $\varepsilon_{ij}$ misjudges deformation (a pure rotation would produce spurious strain at second order). Finite-strain theory uses the deformation gradient $F_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\partial_ju_i$ (so $\mathbf x_\mathrm{new}=F\mathbf x_\mathrm{old}$, $\det F=V/V_0$) and the Green–Lagrange strain $E=\tfrac12(F^TF-I)$, which vanishes for any rigid rotation. Simulations that impose a large shear or compress a cell to several GPa use these (Problem 12).

Worked example 5.1: strain from a displacement field

A displacement field is $u_x=0.002x+0.004y$, $u_y=0.001y$ (all lengths in the same unit, so $u$ is dimensionless per unit length). Find $\varepsilon_{ij}$, the rotation and the area change.

$\partial_xu_x=0.002$, $\partial_yu_x=0.004$, $\partial_xu_y=0$, $\partial_yu_y=0.001$. Strain: $\varepsilon_{xx}=0.002$, $\varepsilon_{yy}=0.001$, $\varepsilon_{xy}=\tfrac12(0.004+0)=0.002$ (engineering shear $\gamma_{xy}=0.004$). Rotation: $\omega_{xy}=\tfrac12(\partial_yu_x-\partial_xu_y)=0.002$ rad. Area change: $\varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{yy}=0.003$, i.e. $0.3\%$. The $0.004$ of “shear” in the gradient is split equally between a pure strain $0.002$ and a rigid rotation $0.002$ rad, which is exactly what the decomposition is for.

Worked example 5.2: finite shear

Apply a simple shear $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Compare the linear and Green–Lagrange strains.

Linear: $\varepsilon_{xy}=0.1$, all else zero. Green–Lagrange: $F^TF=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.2\\0.2&1.04\end{pmatrix}$, so $E=\tfrac12(F^TF-I)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.1\\0.1&0.02\end{pmatrix}$. The extra $E_{yy}=\gamma^2/2=0.02$ is second order and is missed by the linear theory; $\det F=1$, so the shear conserves volume. At $\gamma=0.2$ the error is already $20\%$ of the shear term in one component.

Lecture 6. Hooke’s law and elastic constants#

For small strains the stress is a linear function of the strain: the generalised Hooke’s law $$\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\,\varepsilon_{kl},\qquad\varepsilon_{ij}=S_{ijkl}\,\sigma_{kl},$$ with the stiffness tensor $C$ and its inverse, the compliance $S$. The elastic energy per unit volume is $W=\tfrac12\sigma_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}=\tfrac12C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{ij}\varepsilon_{kl}$, and it must be positive for every nonzero strain for the crystal to be stable. A rank-4 tensor has $3^4=81$ components, but:

  • $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$ implies $C_{ijkl}=C_{jikl}$, and symmetry of $\varepsilon$ implies $C_{ijkl}=C_{ijlk}$: this leaves $6\times6=36$ components;
  • existence of an energy function implies $C_{ijkl}=C_{klij}$ (the matrix is symmetric): $21$ independent constants for the lowest-symmetry (triclinic) crystal.

Voigt notation#

Pair the symmetric index pairs into one index: $11\to1$, $22\to2$, $33\to3$, $23\to4$, $13\to5$, $12\to6$. Write stress and (engineering) strain as six-vectors, $\boldsymbol\sigma=(\sigma_{11},\sigma_{22},\sigma_{33},\sigma_{23},\sigma_{13},\sigma_{12})$ and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{11},\varepsilon_{22},\varepsilon_{33},2\varepsilon_{23},2\varepsilon_{13},2\varepsilon_{12})$, and $C_{IJ}$ as a symmetric $6\times6$ matrix. Then $\boldsymbol\sigma=C\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ is ordinary matrix multiplication and $W=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\varepsilon^TC\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, so stability means that the $6\times6$ matrix $C$ is positive definite (all eigenvalues positive, as in M5). The factor of $2$ for shear is the one thing to remember. Voigt notation is for $C$ and $\sigma$, $\varepsilon$ as vectors only; it is not a tensor transformation law, so to rotate $C$ return to the full tensor form.

Cubic and isotropic materials#

Crystal symmetry removes most of the constants (section 7). For a cubic crystal, with axes along the cube edges, only three remain: $$C=\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}&C_{12}&0&0&0\\C_{12}&C_{11}&C_{12}&0&0&0\\C_{12}&C_{12}&C_{11}&0&0&0\\0&0&0&C_{44}&0&0\\0&0&0&0&C_{44}&0\\0&0&0&0&0&C_{44}\end{pmatrix},$$ with eigenvalues $C_{11}+2C_{12}$ (once, the bulk mode), $C_{11}-C_{12}$ (twice, tetragonal shear) and $C_{44}$ (three times, shear), as found in Problem 5.5 of M5. The cubic compliances are $$S_{11}=\frac{C_{11}+C_{12}}{(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})},\quad S_{12}=\frac{-C_{12}}{(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})},\quad S_{44}=\frac1{C_{44}},$$ and the bulk modulus is $B=\tfrac13(C_{11}+2C_{12})$. The Zener anisotropy ratio $A=2C_{44}/(C_{11}-C_{12})$ compares the two shear moduli; $A=1$ is elastic isotropy. Aluminium has $A\approx1.2$ (nearly isotropic), copper $A\approx3.2$, and the Young’s modulus of a cubic crystal in the direction $\mathbf n$ is $$\frac1{E(\mathbf n)}=S_{11}-2\Bigl(S_{11}-S_{12}-\tfrac12S_{44}\Bigr)\bigl(n_1^2n_2^2+n_2^2n_3^2+n_3^2n_1^2\bigr).$$ An isotropic solid, such as an untextured polycrystal or glass, has only two independent constants, because isotropy forces $C_{44}=\tfrac12(C_{11}-C_{12})$ ($A=1$). The two are conventionally the Lamé constants $\lambda,\mu$ ($\mu$ is the shear modulus $G$), giving the simple law $\sigma_{ij}=\lambda\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij}+2\mu\varepsilon_{ij}$, with $C_{11}=\lambda+2\mu$, $C_{12}=\lambda$, $C_{44}=\mu$. Other pairs are used in practice:

QuantityFrom $\lambda,\mu$From $E,\nu$
Young’s modulus $E$$\dfrac{\mu(3\lambda+2\mu)}{\lambda+\mu}$$E$

In uniaxial tension $\sigma_{xx}=\sigma$, the strains are $\varepsilon_{xx}=\sigma/E$ and $\varepsilon_{yy}=\varepsilon_{zz}=-\nu\sigma/E$. Stability requires $E>0$, $\mu>0$, $K>0$, which bounds Poisson’s ratio to $-1<\nu<\tfrac12$.

In materials science: where the constants come from

Elastic constants are computed from simulations by applying small strains to a cell and fitting the resulting stresses (or energies, $E=\tfrac12V\boldsymbol\varepsilon^TC\boldsymbol\varepsilon$) with DFT or interatomic potentials, one strain pattern per independent constant. The $6\times6$ matrix is then checked for positive definiteness (Born stability), and averaged over orientations for a polycrystal: the Voigt estimate $G_V=\tfrac15(C_{11}-C_{12}+3C_{44})$ assumes uniform strain and is an upper bound, the Reuss estimate $G_R=5/[4(S_{11}-S_{12})+3S_{44}]$ assumes uniform stress and is a lower bound, and their mean (Hill) is a common estimate (Problem 7).

Worked example 6.1: constants of steel

Steel has $E=200$ GPa and $\nu=0.3$. Find $\lambda$, $\mu$ and $K$, and the stress in a bar strained $\varepsilon_{xx}=0.001$ in uniaxial tension.

$\mu=E/[2(1+\nu)]=76.9$ GPa; $\lambda=E\nu/[(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)]=60/(1.3\times0.4)=115.4$ GPa; $K=E/[3(1-2\nu)]=166.7$ GPa. Check: $\lambda+\tfrac23\mu=115.4+51.3=166.7$ ✓. The stress is $\sigma_{xx}=E\varepsilon_{xx}=200$ MPa, the lateral strains are $-\nu\varepsilon_{xx}=-0.0003$, and the volumetric strain is $0.001-0.0006=0.0004$, giving $\Delta V/V=0.04\%$ (it is $(1-2\nu)\varepsilon_{xx}$, vanishing as $\nu\to\tfrac12$ for rubber).

Worked example 6.2: copper is strongly anisotropic

For copper, $C_{11}=168.4$, $C_{12}=121.4$, $C_{44}=75.4$ GPa. Find $A$, $B$, $E_{[100]}$ and $E_{[111]}$.

$A=2(75.4)/(168.4-121.4)=3.21$; $B=\tfrac13(168.4+242.8)=137.1$ GPa. The compliances are $S_{11}=0.015031$, $S_{12}=-0.006297$ and $S_{44}=0.013263$ GPa$^{-1}$. For $[100]$ the direction sum $n_1^2n_2^2+\dots$ is $0$, so $E=1/S_{11}=66.7$ GPa. For $[111]$ the sum is $\tfrac13$, so $1/E=S_{11}-\tfrac23(S_{11}-S_{12}-\tfrac12S_{44})=0.005233$ and $E=191.1$ GPa, nearly three times stiffer. A polycrystal averages these: the Voigt shear modulus is $54.6$ GPa and the Reuss value $40.0$ GPa, so an isotropic estimate of $G$ for Cu lies between them (Hill takes the mean, about $47$ GPa).

Lecture 7. Crystal symmetry and property tensors#

A crystal looks the same after the operations of its point group (rotations, reflections, inversion), each an orthogonal matrix $Q$. Neumann’s principle: any physical property of a crystal must be unchanged by every symmetry operation of the crystal. For a tensor property, this means $T=T'$ with $T'$ obtained from $T$ by the transformation of section 3 using each symmetry matrix $Q$. Each such condition cuts the number of independent components. For example, a symmetric second-rank tensor (conductivity, thermal expansion, diffusion) has six components for triclinic crystals, four for monoclinic, three for orthorhombic, two for tetragonal, hexagonal and trigonal crystals ($T=\operatorname{diag}(a,a,c)$), and one for cubic crystals: $T=a\,I$, isotropic, even though the crystal is not (Problem 10). So a cubic crystal conducts heat equally well in every direction, but a hexagonal one (zinc, graphite, titanium) has conductivities $\kappa_\perp\neq\kappa_\parallel$ along and across the unique axis, and thermal expansion $\alpha_\perp\ne\alpha_\parallel$, which is why such materials develop internal stresses on heating.

An important special case is inversion, $Q=-I$. A tensor of odd rank $n$ changes sign, $T'=(-1)^nT$. In a crystal with a centre of symmetry, invariance demands $T=-T$, so $T=0$: odd-rank tensors vanish in centrosymmetric crystals. The piezoelectric tensor $d_{ijk}$ (rank 3), which links stress to polarisation, is therefore zero in Si, NaCl and every other centrosymmetric structure, and non-zero in quartz, GaAs or ZnO (Problem 11). Even-rank tensors (conductivity, stiffness) are unaffected by inversion.

Worked example 7.1: symmetry forces a cubic conductivity to be a scalar

Show that a symmetric second-rank tensor property $\kappa_{ij}$ of a cubic crystal is isotropic.

A fourfold rotation about $z$ is a symmetry operation: $Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Invariance $\kappa=Q\kappa Q^T$ gives, component by component, $\kappa_{xx}=\kappa_{yy}$; $\kappa_{xy}=-\kappa_{xy}\Rightarrow\kappa_{xy}=0$; and $\kappa_{xz}=\kappa_{yz}=-\kappa_{xz}\Rightarrow\kappa_{xz}=\kappa_{yz}=0$. The same argument about $x$ gives $\kappa_{yy}=\kappa_{zz}$. Hence $\kappa=\kappa_0I$: cubic crystals conduct heat and expand identically in all directions even though their stiffness is anisotropic (rank 4 allows three constants, rank 2 allows one). Uniaxial crystals (tetragonal, hexagonal) keep two, $\kappa_\perp=\kappa_{xx}$ and $\kappa_\parallel=\kappa_{zz}$.

Summary of the unit#

A tensor is a direction-dependent linear response described by components that transform under rotation. Stress and strain are symmetric second-rank tensors whose eigen-decompositions give principal values and invariants; the physics of yield and failure lives in those invariants. Hooke’s law connects them through a fourth-rank stiffness tensor, compactly handled as a positive-definite $6\times6$ matrix, whose number of independent entries is fixed by crystal symmetry. With the tools of vectors, matrices and tensors in place, we turn to time: ordinary differential equations describe how any of these quantities evolve.

Key takeaways
  • A tensor is defined by how its components transform when the axes rotate, $T'_{ij}=Q_{ik}Q_{jl}T_{kl}$, not merely by being an array of numbers; scalars, vectors, stress and the stiffness tensor are tensors of rank 0, 1, 2 and 4.
  • In index notation a repeated index is summed (Einstein convention); with the Kronecker delta $\delta_{ij}$ and the permutation symbol $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ it expresses dot and cross products, traces and determinants compactly.
  • Stress $\sigma_{ij}$ gives the traction on any plane, $t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j$; it is symmetric, its eigenvalues are the principal stresses, and failure criteria depend only on invariants such as the von Mises stress $\sqrt{\tfrac32 s_{ij}s_{ij}}$ of the deviatoric part.
  • Small strain is the symmetric part of the displacement gradient, $\varepsilon_{ij}=\tfrac12(\partial_ju_i+\partial_iu_j)$; the trace is the volume change and the antisymmetric part is a rigid rotation that stores no energy.
  • Hooke's law $\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}$ has 81 components but only 21 independent constants in general, 3 for cubic crystals and 2 for isotropic solids; Voigt notation turns it into a $6\times6$ matrix, which must be positive definite for stability.
  • Neumann's principle: a crystal property tensor must be invariant under the crystal's point-group operations, which reduces the number of independent components and forces odd-rank tensors such as piezoelectricity to vanish in centrosymmetric crystals.

Problem 6.1Basic

Using index notation, show: (a) $\delta_{ij}\delta_{ij}=3$; (b) $\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{ijk}=6$; (c) $\varepsilon_{ijk}a_ja_k=0$ (so $\mathbf a\times\mathbf a=\mathbf 0$); (d) $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\varepsilon_{ijk}a_ib_jc_k$ equals the determinant of the matrix with rows $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$.

Hint

(a) $\delta_{ij}\delta_{ij}=\delta_{ii}$. (b) Use $\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{ilm}=\delta_{jl}\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}\delta_{kl}$ and set $l=j$, $m=k$. (c) Swap the dummy indices $j\leftrightarrow k$.

Full solution

(a) $\delta_{ij}\delta_{ij}=\delta_{ii}=1+1+1=3$.

(b) From the $\varepsilon\varepsilon$ rule with $l=j$ and $m=k$ (and summing): $\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{ijk}=\delta_{jj}\delta_{kk}-\delta_{jk}\delta_{kj}=3\cdot3-3=6$. (Directly: six non-zero permutations, each contributing $(\pm1)^2=1$.)

(c) $S=\varepsilon_{ijk}a_ja_k$. Relabel the dummy indices ($j\leftrightarrow k$): $S=\varepsilon_{ikj}a_ka_j=-\varepsilon_{ijk}a_ja_k=-S$, so $S=0$.

(d) Writing out the six non-zero terms of $\varepsilon_{ijk}a_ib_jc_k$: $a_1b_2c_3-a_1b_3c_2+a_2b_3c_1-a_2b_1c_3+a_3b_1c_2-a_3b_2c_1$, which is the expansion of the $3\times3$ determinant. So the triple product is the determinant and the signed volume of the parallelepiped.

Problem 6.2Basic

Prove the vector identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)-\nabla^2\mathbf F$ in index notation, and show $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0$.

Hint

$[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf F)]_i=\varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_j\varepsilon_{klm}\partial_lF_m$. Use the $\varepsilon\varepsilon$ rule (note $\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{klm}=\varepsilon_{kij}\varepsilon_{klm}$).

Full solution

$\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{klm}=\varepsilon_{kij}\varepsilon_{klm}=\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl}$. Then $$[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf F)]_i=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})\partial_j\partial_lF_m=\partial_j\partial_iF_j-\partial_j\partial_jF_i=\partial_i(\partial_jF_j)-\partial_j\partial_jF_i,$$ using the commuting of partial derivatives. In vector form: $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)-\nabla^2\mathbf F$. ✓

For the divergence of a curl: $\partial_i\bigl(\varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_jF_k\bigr)=\varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_i\partial_jF_k$. The derivative factor $\partial_i\partial_j$ is symmetric in $i,j$ while $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ is antisymmetric, so their contraction vanishes: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0$.

Problem 6.8Core

Copper has $C_{11}=168.4$, $C_{12}=121.4$, $C_{44}=75.4$ GPa. (a) Compute the Zener ratio and the compliances $S_{11},S_{12},S_{44}$. (b) Find the Young’s modulus along $[100]$, $[110]$ and $[111]$. (c) Which direction is stiffest and which is softest? Why does this matter for a polycrystal under load?

Hint

$d=(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})=47.0\times411.2$. For $\mathbf n=[110]/\sqrt2$, $n_1^2n_2^2+\ldots=\tfrac14$; for $[111]/\sqrt3$ it is $\tfrac13$; for $[100]$ it is $0$.

Full solution

(a) $A=2(75.4)/(168.4-121.4)=150.8/47.0=3.21$. $d=47.0\times411.2=19\,326$ GPa$^2$. $S_{11}=\dfrac{289.8}{19\,326}=0.014995$, $S_{12}=-\dfrac{121.4}{19\,326}=-0.006282$, $S_{44}=\dfrac1{75.4}=0.013263$ (all GPa$^{-1}$).

The polycrystal modulus lies between ($\approx130$ GPa for copper): for such anisotropy, the single-crystal constants, not a single $E$, are needed in finite-element crystal-plasticity models.

Problem 6.9Core

A hexagonal single crystal has thermal conductivity tensor $\kappa=\operatorname{diag}(150,150,100)$ W m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ in axes with $z$ along the $c$-axis. A temperature gradient of magnitude $G=1000$ K/m is applied in the $xz$-plane at $45^\circ$ to the $c$-axis, i.e. $\nabla T=G(\cos45^\circ,0,\sin45^\circ)$. (a) Find the heat flux $\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla T$. (b) By what angle does $\mathbf q$ deviate from $-\nabla T$? (c) Verify that the entropy production rate $-\mathbf q\cdot\nabla T/T^2$ is positive and explain what condition on $\kappa$ guarantees it.

Hint

$q_i=-\kappa_{ij}\partial_jT$. The angle of $-\nabla T$ from the $-x$ direction is $45^\circ$.

Full solution

(a) $\nabla T=1000(0.7071,0,0.7071)=(707.1,0,707.1)$ K/m. $\mathbf q=-\kappa\nabla T=-(150\times707.1,\ 0,\ 100\times707.1)=(-106.1,\ 0,\ -70.7)$ kW/m$^2$, magnitude $127.5$ kW/m$^2$.

(b) The direction of $-\nabla T$ is at $\arctan(1)=45^\circ$ from the $x$-axis (in the $xz$-plane), whereas $\mathbf q$ is at $\arctan(70.7/106.1)=33.7^\circ$. The heat flux is turned $11.3^\circ$ towards the direction of higher conductivity ($x$). Heat does not flow straight down the gradient in an anisotropic crystal; it prefers the easy direction.

(c) $-\mathbf q\cdot\nabla T=\kappa_{ij}\partial_iT\,\allowbreak \partial_jT=150(707.1)^2+100(707.1)^2=1.25\times10^8$ W K m$^{-3}$ $>0$. In general $\kappa_{ij}\partial_iT\partial_jT>0$ for every nonzero gradient exactly when $\kappa$ is positive definite (all principal conductivities positive), which the second law requires. (The symmetric part of $\kappa$ is what matters here; Onsager’s relations show $\kappa$ is symmetric.) Note the symmetry parallel: this is the same positive-definiteness condition as the stability of the elastic energy.

Problem 6.10Challenge

Apply Neumann’s principle to a symmetric second-rank property tensor $T$ (thermal expansion, say). (a) Show that invariance under a $90^\circ$ rotation about $z$ forces $T=\operatorname{diag}(a,a,c)$. (b) Show that additionally requiring invariance under the cyclic permutation $x\to y\to z\to x$ (the $120^\circ$ rotation about $[111]$ present in cubic crystals) forces $T=aI$. (c) What is the consequence for cubic crystals?

Hint

Use $T=QTQ^T$ with $Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and write $T$ with entries $a,b,c$ on the diagonal and $d=T_{12}$, $e=T_{13}$, $f=T_{23}$.

Full solution

(a) With this $Q$, $(QTQ^T)_{ij}=Q_{ik}Q_{jl}T_{kl}$. The non-zero entries of $Q$ are $Q_{12}=-1$, $Q_{21}=1$, $Q_{33}=1$. Hence $T'_{11}=Q_{12}Q_{12}T_{22}=b$, so invariance gives $a=b$. $T'_{12}=Q_{12}Q_{21}T_{21}=-d$, so $d=-d$ and $d=0$. $T'_{13}=Q_{12}Q_{33}T_{23}=-f$ and $T'_{23}=Q_{21}Q_{33}T_{13}=e$, so $e=-f$ and $f=e$, hence $e=f=0$. The result is $T=\operatorname{diag}(a,a,c)$.

(b) The cyclic permutation maps component $T_{xx}\to T_{yy}\to T_{zz}$, so invariance gives $T_{xx}=T_{yy}=T_{zz}$, i.e. $c=a$ (the off-diagonals are already zero). So $T=aI$.

(c) A cubic crystal has an isotropic second-rank property tensor: a single number describes its thermal conductivity, thermal expansion, diffusivity, dielectric constant and refractive index. It is only the fourth-rank tensors (stiffness) that retain anisotropy in cubic crystals ($A\ne1$). Hence cubic crystals cannot be distinguished from isotropic ones by transport or thermal expansion measurements, but they can by elastic ones. Conversely a tetragonal crystal (step (a) only), and by a similar argument a hexagonal or trigonal one (a $6$-fold or $3$-fold axis), has two independent values $a,c$.

Problem 6.11Challenge

The piezoelectric tensor $d_{ijk}$ relates the polarisation $P_i=d_{ijk}\sigma_{jk}$. (a) Show that a crystal with inversion symmetry has $d_{ijk}=0$. (b) How many independent components can $d_{ijk}$ have, given the symmetry $d_{ijk}=d_{ikj}$? (c) Explain why this means that silicon and NaCl are not piezoelectric but quartz and GaAs can be, and why even-rank tensors are unaffected.

Hint

Inversion: $Q=-I$, so each index contributes a factor $-1$.

Full solution

(a) Under inversion $Q_{ij}=-\delta_{ij}$, so $d'_{ijk}=Q_{ip}Q_{jq}Q_{kr}d_{pqr}=(-1)^3d_{ijk}=-d_{ijk}$. If the crystal is invariant under inversion, then $d'=d$, so $d=-d$ and $d_{ijk}=0$ for every component.

(b) The index $i$ takes $3$ values and the symmetric pair $(jk)$ has $6$ values: at most $3\times6=18$ independent components, the form of a $3\times6$ matrix $d_{iJ}$ (Voigt-style). Symmetry of the crystal reduces this: for example zinc blende (GaAs, ZnS) has the point group $\bar43m$ and a single independent constant $d_{14}$; quartz has two.

(c) Silicon (diamond structure) and NaCl (rock salt) have inversion centres, so $d=0$: squeezing them produces no polarisation. Quartz and GaAs lack an inversion centre, so $d\ne0$ is allowed (and, for these, occurs): a stress shifts positive and negative ions asymmetrically, giving a net dipole. For even-rank tensors the factor is $(-1)^{2n}=+1$, so inversion imposes no condition: the stiffness, conductivity and dielectric tensors are non-zero in all crystals. The same argument kills all odd-rank properties (including pyroelectricity, second-harmonic generation and the linear electro-optic effect) in centrosymmetric crystals, a quick screen for functional materials.

Problem 6.12Challenge

Simple shear of magnitude $\gamma$ is the deformation $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Compute $\det F$, the small-strain tensor and the Green–Lagrange strain $E=\tfrac12(F^TF-I)$. (b) For $\gamma=1$ find the principal stretches (square roots of the eigenvalues of $F^TF$). (c) Show that a rigid rotation $R(\phi)$ has $\varepsilon\ne0$ at second order in $\phi$ but $E=0$ exactly.

Hint

Small strain: $\varepsilon=\tfrac12(F+F^T)-I$. For (b), $F^TF=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\\gamma&1+\gamma^2\end{pmatrix}$.

Full solution

(a) $\det F=1$: volume-preserving. $\varepsilon=\tfrac12(F+F^T)-I=\begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma/2\\\gamma/2&0\end{pmatrix}$. $F^TF=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\gamma&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\\gamma&1+\gamma^2\end{pmatrix}$, so $E=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma\\\gamma&\gamma^2\end{pmatrix}$. The two differ in the $yy$ entry: $E_{yy}=\gamma^2/2$ is a second-order stretch, which the linear $\varepsilon$ misses.

(b) For $\gamma=1$: $F^TF=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\dfrac{3\pm\sqrt5}2=2.618,\ 0.382$. Principal stretches $\sqrt{2.618}=1.618$ and $\sqrt{0.382}=0.618$ (the golden ratio and its reciprocal!); their product is $1=\det F$. So a shear strain of $100\%$ stretches material by $62\%$ along one direction and squeezes it to $62\%$ of its length along the other: far from the small-strain picture $\varepsilon=\pm\gamma/2=\pm0.5$ (stretches $1.5$ and $0.5$).

(c) $F=R(\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}$. Small strain: $\varepsilon=\tfrac12(F+F^T)-I=(\cos\phi-1)I\approx-\tfrac12\phi^2I$, a spurious isotropic compression at second order. Green–Lagrange: $F^TF=R^TR=I$, so $E=0$ exactly. This is why simulations of large rotations, buckling or rotating samples must use $E$ (or the stretch from the polar decomposition $F=RU$) rather than $\varepsilon$.

Principal stretches $1.618$ and $0.618$ (product 1); $E=0$ for any rotation

Quiz 6Symmetry

Why is the piezoelectric tensor zero in a crystal with a centre of symmetry, but the stiffness tensor is not?

Solution

Inversion is $Q=-I$ and a rank-$n$ tensor picks up $(-1)^n$. For the rank-3 piezoelectric tensor $d=-d$, so $d=0$. For the rank-4 stiffness $C=(+1)C$, which gives no constraint.
