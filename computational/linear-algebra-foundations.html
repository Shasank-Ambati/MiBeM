Linear algebra I: vectors, matrices and lattices

Crystals are lattices of points generated by three vectors; diffraction patterns are the reciprocal of that lattice; the stiffness of a solid is a matrix; the electronic structure problem is a matrix. Linear algebra is the mathematics of all of these. This course builds it from the ground up with a materials emphasis: lattices, coordinate changes, reciprocal space and X-ray diffraction appear throughout, and the second part of the subject (eigenvalues, in MATH 202) then rests on firm foundations.

Lecture 1. Vectors#

Vectors and the dot product#

A vector $\mathbf v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ in $\mathbb R^n$ is an ordered list of numbers; geometrically, an arrow from the origin. Vectors add component by component and can be multiplied by a scalar. The dot product and norm are $$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\sum_ia_ib_i=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta,\qquad|\mathbf a|=\sqrt{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}.$$ Two vectors are orthogonal if $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=0$. The angle between them is $\theta=\arccos\dfrac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|}$; the projection of $\mathbf b$ onto $\mathbf a$ is $\dfrac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}\,\allowbreak \mathbf a$, the component of $\mathbf b$ along $\mathbf a$. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b|\le|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|$ guarantees that the cosine lies in $[-1,1]$.

Cross product and triple product#

In three dimensions the cross product is $$\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=(a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2-a_2b_1),$$ a vector perpendicular to both, with length $|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta$ equal to the area of the parallelogram they span, and direction given by the right-hand rule. It is anticommutative: $\mathbf b\times\mathbf a=-\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$. The scalar triple product $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)$ is the signed volume of the parallelepiped with edges $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ (positive if they form a right-handed set). The area normal $\mathbf n=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b/|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|$ gives the normal to a crystal plane spanned by two vectors.

In materials science

Bond lengths and angles from atomic coordinates are dot products: for an atom at the origin bonded to atoms at $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$, the bond angle is $\arccos\dfrac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|}$ (Problem 1). The volume of a unit cell with edge vectors $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$ is $V=|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)|$, which gives the density of any crystal (Problem 7).

Worked example 1.2: dot product, cross product and projection

For $\mathbf a=(1,2,3)$ and $\mathbf b=(4,5,6)$ find $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$, $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ and the area of the parallelogram they span. Then project $\mathbf p=(3,0,4)$ onto $\mathbf q=(1,2,2)$.

$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=4+10+18=32$. $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=(2\cdot6-3\cdot5,\ 3\cdot4-1\cdot6,\ 1\cdot5-2\cdot4)=(-3,6,-3)$, of length $\sqrt{54}=7.35$, the parallelogram area (and the vector is perpendicular to both: $(-3,6,-3)\cdot(1,2,3)=0$). Projection: $\dfrac{\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q}{\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf q}\mathbf q=\dfrac{11}9(1,2,2)=(1.22,2.44,2.44)$, and the remainder $\mathbf p-\text{proj}=(1.78,-2.44,1.56)$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf q$.

Lecture 2. Linear systems and Gaussian elimination#

A linear system of $m$ equations in $n$ unknowns, $$a_{11}x_1+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n=b_1,\ \ \ldots,\ \ a_{m1}x_1+\cdots+a_{mn}x_n=b_m,$$ is written $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$, with $A$ the $m\times n$ matrix of coefficients. Linear systems are everywhere: balancing chemical reactions, mass balance in a flow network, fitting a model, finite-element equations, and the self-consistent equations in electronic-structure codes.

The algorithm#

The three elementary row operations, which do not change the solution set, are: swap two rows; multiply a row by a nonzero number; add a multiple of one row to another. Gaussian elimination uses them to reduce the augmented matrix $[A\,|\,\mathbf b]$ to an upper-triangular (row echelon) form from which the unknowns are found by back-substitution. In a numerical code one chooses the largest available pivot (“partial pivoting”) to limit rounding error. Elimination costs about $\tfrac23n^3$ operations, which is why solving a dense system of $10^5$ unknowns is already expensive and why sparse structure matters in finite-element codes.

Worked example 2.1: elimination

Solve $x+2y+z=4$, $2x+5y+z=9$, $x+y+3z=8$.

Augmented matrix $\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&2&1&4\\2&5&1&9\\1&1&3&8\end{array}\right]$. Replace $R_2\to R_2-2R_1$ and $R_3\to R_3-R_1$: $\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&2&1&4\\0&1&-1&1\\0&-1&2&4\end{array}\right]$. Then $R_3\to R_3+R_2$: $\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&2&1&4\\0&1&-1&1\\0&0&1&5\end{array}\right]$. Back-substitute: $z=5$; $y=1+z=6$; $x=4-2y-z=4-12-5=-13$. Check in the second equation: $2(-13)+5(6)+5=9$. ✓

Rank and the solution set#

The rank of $A$ is the number of nonzero rows after elimination (the number of independent equations, equal to the number of independent columns). Three cases arise for a square $n\times n$ system:

  • $\operatorname{rank}A=n$: a unique solution for every $\mathbf b$ ($A$ is invertible).
  • $\operatorname{rank}A<n$ and $\mathbf b$ is consistent: infinitely many solutions, forming an affine family with $n-\operatorname{rank}A$ free parameters.
  • $\operatorname{rank}A<n$ and $\mathbf b$ inconsistent (a row reads $0=c\neq0$): no solution.

For a homogeneous system $A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ the solutions form the null space, of dimension $n-\operatorname{rank}A$ (rank–nullity theorem). Balancing a chemical equation is exactly a null-space problem (Problem 3).

Elimination as a factorisation#

Elimination is equivalent to writing $A=LU$ with $L$ lower-triangular (holding the multipliers, with ones on the diagonal) and $U$ upper-triangular (the echelon form). Solving $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ then needs two cheap triangular solves, $L\mathbf y=\mathbf b$ (forward) and $U\mathbf x=\mathbf y$ (back), and the expensive factorisation ($\approx\tfrac13n^3$ operations) is done once for many right-hand sides.

Worked example 2.2: an LU factorisation

Factorise $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\4&3&3\\8&7&9\end{pmatrix}$ and solve $A\mathbf x=(4,10,24)^T$.

Eliminate column 1 with multipliers $2$ and $4$: rows become $(0,1,1)$ and $(0,3,5)$. Then eliminate with multiplier $3$: $(0,0,2)$. So $L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\2&1&0\\4&3&1\end{pmatrix}$, $U=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$. Forward: $y_1=4$, $y_2=10-2(4)=2$, $y_3=24-4(4)-3(2)=2$. Back: $x_3=1$, $x_2=2-1=1$, $x_1=(4-1-1)/2=1$. So $\mathbf x=(1,1,1)$, and $\det A=\prod u_{ii}=2\cdot1\cdot2=4$.

Lecture 3. Matrices as linear maps#

An $m\times n$ matrix $A$ defines a map $\mathbf x\mapsto A\mathbf x$ from $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^m$ that is linear: $A(\alpha\mathbf x+\beta\mathbf y)=\alpha A\mathbf x+\beta A\mathbf y$. The columns of $A$ are the images of the standard basis vectors, which is the quickest way to write down a matrix for a given transformation. The product of an $m\times n$ and an $n\times p$ matrix is $(AB)_{ij}=\sum_kA_{ik}B_{kj}$, and it represents doing $B$ first, then $A$. Matrix multiplication is associative, $(AB)C=A(BC)$, and distributive, but not commutative: rotating then reflecting is generally not the same as reflecting then rotating.

The transpose $A^T$ swaps rows and columns, with $(AB)^T=B^TA^T$; a matrix is symmetric if $A^T=A$. The identity $I$ leaves everything unchanged. The inverse $A^{-1}$, if it exists, undoes $A$: $A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I$, and $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$. For a $2\times2$ matrix, $$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix},$$ which fails exactly when $ad-bc=0$.

Standard 2D transformations: rotation by $\theta$ (anticlockwise), reflection in the $x$-axis, shear and scaling: $$R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\quad M_x=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad D=\begin{pmatrix}s_x&0\\0&s_y\end{pmatrix}.$$ The shear $S$ with $\gamma=\tan(\text{angle})$ is the deformation of simple shear tests, and deforming a simulation cell by applying a matrix to its lattice vectors is how strain is imposed in simulations.

Do not invert a matrix to solve an equation

Mathematically $\mathbf x=A^{-1}\mathbf b$. Numerically, computing $A^{-1}$ is slower and less accurate than solving $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ by elimination (LU factorisation), and it destroys sparsity. Write “solve” in your code, not “inv”. If you need to solve with many right-hand sides, factorise once and reuse.

Lecture 4. Determinants#

For a square matrix the determinant is a single number. For $2\times2$ and $3\times3$: $$\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-bc,\qquad\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\g&h&i\end{pmatrix}=a(ei-fh)-b(di-fg)+c(dh-eg).$$ In general one expands along a row or column, or (better) reduces to triangular form by elimination and multiplies the diagonal. Key properties: $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$; $\det A^T=\det A$; swapping two rows flips the sign; a row of zeros or two proportional rows gives $0$; $\det A^{-1}=1/\det A$; the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal.

Geometric meaning

$|\det A|$ is the factor by which the map $\mathbf x\mapsto A\mathbf x$ multiplies areas (2D) or volumes (3D); the sign says whether orientation is preserved ($+$) or reversed ($-$, a reflection). The columns of $A$ span a parallelepiped of volume $|\det A|$. $\det A=0$ exactly when the map flattens space onto a lower dimension, equivalently when $A$ is singular (not invertible) and its columns are linearly dependent.

This is why the cell volume of a crystal is $V=|\det[\mathbf a_1\ \mathbf a_2\ \mathbf a_3]|=|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)|$, and why a deformation gradient $F$ has $\det F=V/V_0$ (the volume ratio) and a physically admissible one has $\det F>0$.

Worked example 4.1: determinant, inverse and rank

(a) Evaluate $\det A$ for $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\2&1&1\\3&-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. (b) Invert $B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$. (c) Find the rank of $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

(a) Expand along row 1: $1(1\cdot2-1\cdot(-1))-2(2\cdot2-1\cdot3)+(-1)(2\cdot(-1)-1\cdot3)=3-2+5=6\neq0$, so $A$ is invertible. (b) $\det B=5$, $B^{-1}=\tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (check: $BB^{-1}=I$). (c) Row 2 is twice row 1, so the rows are dependent: eliminating gives two non-zero rows, rank $2$, and the null space is one-dimensional, spanned by $(-1,-1,1)$ (check: $C(-1,-1,1)^T=\mathbf0$).

Lecture 5. Bases and coordinates#

Linear independence and basis#

Vectors $\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k$ are linearly independent if the only solution of $c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k=\mathbf 0$ is $c_i=0$ for all $i$; none is a combination of the others. Their span is the set of all combinations. A basis of a space is a set of independent vectors that spans it, and the number of vectors is the dimension. For $n$ vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ placed as the columns of a matrix, independence is equivalent to $\det\neq0$. Every vector then has unique coordinates in the basis.

Crystal lattices#

A crystal lattice is the set of points $\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3$ with integers $n_i$, where $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$ are the primitive lattice vectors: a (non-orthogonal) basis of $\mathbb R^3$. Collect them as the columns of the lattice matrix $A=[\mathbf a_1\ \mathbf a_2\ \mathbf a_3]$. A position written with fractional coordinates $\mathbf f=(f_1,f_2,f_3)$ has Cartesian position $$\mathbf r=A\mathbf f=f_1\mathbf a_1+f_2\mathbf a_2+f_3\mathbf a_3,\qquad\mathbf f=A^{-1}\mathbf r .$$ Structure files (POSCAR, CIF) store fractional coordinates because they are the same in every strained version of the cell, and fractional coordinates inside the unit cell lie between $0$ and $1$. The squared distance between two points with fractional separation $\Delta\mathbf f$ is $$|\Delta\mathbf r|^2=\Delta\mathbf f^{T}(A^TA)\Delta\mathbf f=\Delta\mathbf f^{T}G\,\Delta\mathbf f,\qquad G_{ij}=\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf a_j,$$ where $G$ is the metric tensor: its diagonal holds the squared lattice parameters $a^2,b^2,c^2$ and its off-diagonal terms involve the cell angles.

Worked example 5.1: the FCC primitive cell

The primitive vectors of the face-centred cubic lattice with cubic edge $a$ are $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$. Find the primitive-cell volume and the number of atoms per conventional cube.

$V=|\det A|$ with $A=\tfrac a2\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. The determinant of the integer matrix is $0(0-1)-1(0-1)+1(1-0)=2$, and scaling a $3\times3$ matrix by $a/2$ multiplies the determinant by $(a/2)^3$: $V=\dfrac{a^3}{8}\times2=\dfrac{a^3}{4}$. The conventional cube has volume $a^3$, which therefore contains $a^3/(a^3/4)=4$ primitive cells, hence $4$ lattice points. Since FCC has one atom per lattice point, the cube holds $4$ atoms: $8$ corners shared by $8$ cubes and $6$ face centres shared by $2$ gives $8\cdot\tfrac18+6\cdot\tfrac12=4$. ✓

Worked example 5.2: fractional coordinates in a triclinic cell

A cell has $\mathbf a=(3,0,0)$, $\mathbf b=(1,3,0)$, $\mathbf c=(0.5,0.5,4)$ Å. Find its volume and the fractional coordinates of the point $\mathbf r=(2,2,2)$.

$A$ is triangular, so $V=\det A=3\cdot3\cdot4=36$ Å$^3$. Solve $A\mathbf f=\mathbf r$ from the bottom: $4f_3=2\Rightarrow f_3=0.5$; $3f_2+0.5f_3=2\Rightarrow f_2=0.583$; $3f_1+f_2+0.5f_3=2\Rightarrow f_1=0.389$. So $\mathbf f=(0.389,0.583,0.5)$, inside the cell (all components between $0$ and $1$).

Lecture 6. The reciprocal lattice#

Diffraction experiments and plane-wave electronic-structure codes work in reciprocal space. The reciprocal lattice has basis vectors $\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2,\mathbf b_3$ defined by $$\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\,\delta_{ij},\qquad\text{explicitly}\quad\mathbf b_1=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)}\ \text{(and cyclic)},$$ or in matrix form, with columns $\mathbf b_j$ in $B$: $B^TA=2\pi I$, so $B=2\pi\,A^{-T}$. A reciprocal lattice vector $\mathbf G=h\mathbf b_1+k\mathbf b_2+l\mathbf b_3$ (integers $h,k,l$) is perpendicular to the family of lattice planes with Miller indices $(hkl)$, and the spacing between adjacent planes is $$d_{hkl}=\frac{2\pi}{|\mathbf G_{hkl}|}.$$ For a cubic lattice, $\mathbf G=\tfrac{2\pi}{a}(h,k,l)$ and so $d_{hkl}=\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}$.

In materials science: Bragg’s law and the Brillouin zone

X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$ reflect strongly from planes of spacing $d$ at angles satisfying Bragg’s law $\lambda=2d\sin\theta$. In reciprocal-space language this is the Laue condition that the change in wavevector equals a reciprocal lattice vector. The reciprocal of FCC is body-centred cubic and vice versa (Problem 9), and the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice is the first Brillouin zone, the region in which $\mathbf k$-points are sampled in DFT (Density functional theory) and phonon calculations. Doubling a real-space cell halves the reciprocal one, which is why larger supercells need fewer $\mathbf k$-points.

Worked example 6.2: reciprocal lattice of a hexagonal net

A 2D hexagonal lattice has $\mathbf a_1=(1,0)$, $\mathbf a_2=(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)$. Find $\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2$ and the spacing of the $(10)$ planes.

With the vectors as columns, $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\tfrac12\\0&\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\end{pmatrix}$ and $A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\\0&\tfrac2{\sqrt3}\end{pmatrix}$. $B=2\pi A^{-T}$ has columns equal to the rows of $2\pi A^{-1}$: $\mathbf b_1=2\pi\bigl(1,\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\bigr)$, $\mathbf b_2=2\pi\bigl(0,\tfrac2{\sqrt3}\bigr)$, both of length $2\pi\cdot\tfrac2{\sqrt3}=7.26$, at $60^\circ$ to each other: a hexagonal lattice again, rotated by $30^\circ$. Check: $\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf b_2=0$, $\mathbf a_2\cdot\mathbf b_2=2\pi$. ✓ The $(10)$ spacing is $d=2\pi/|\mathbf b_1|=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2=0.866$ (in units of $|\mathbf a_1|$), as in the interactive figure above.

Lecture 7. Orthogonality and rotations#

A set of vectors is orthonormal if they are mutually orthogonal unit vectors. A matrix $Q$ whose columns are orthonormal is an orthogonal matrix: $Q^TQ=I$, so $Q^{-1}=Q^T$, and it preserves lengths and angles, $|Q\mathbf x|=|\mathbf x|$. Its determinant is $\pm1$: $+1$ for a proper rotation, $-1$ for a reflection or inversion. The point-group operations of a crystal are orthogonal matrices, which is why symmetry analysis is linear algebra. The rotation matrices of section 3 are orthogonal; a rotation by $\theta$ about the $z$-axis in three dimensions is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Given any independent set, the Gram–Schmidt process builds an orthonormal one: take $\mathbf e_1=\mathbf v_1/|\mathbf v_1|$, then subtract from each subsequent vector its projections on the earlier $\mathbf e$’s and normalise: $$\mathbf w_k=\mathbf v_k-\sum_{j<k}(\mathbf e_j\cdot\mathbf v_k)\mathbf e_j,\qquad\mathbf e_k=\mathbf w_k/|\mathbf w_k| .$$ Applied to the columns of $A$ this gives the QR factorisation $A=QR$, with $R$ upper-triangular, which is how lattice vectors are rotated into the standard orientation used by codes (first vector along $x$, second in the $xy$-plane) and a stable way to solve least-squares problems (MATH 303). The orthogonal projection onto a unit vector $\mathbf e$ is the matrix $P=\mathbf e\mathbf e^T$ ($P^2=P$).

Worked example 7.2: decomposing a displacement

An atom is displaced by $\mathbf d=(0.3,0.1,0.2)$ Å relative to a bond along $\hat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,1)$. Split $\mathbf d$ into components parallel and perpendicular to the bond.

Parallel part: $(\mathbf d\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})\hat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,0.2)$ (a bond stretch of $0.2$ Å). Perpendicular part: $\mathbf d-\mathbf d_\parallel=(0.3,0.1,0)$, magnitude $0.316$ Å (a bending or shear displacement). Stretch and bend have very different force constants, so splitting a displacement this way is the first step of a vibrational analysis.

Lecture 8. Conditioning: when small errors are amplified#

Solving $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ on a computer is exact only up to rounding, and the data $\mathbf b$ are usually uncertain anyway. A system is ill-conditioned if a tiny change in $\mathbf b$ (or $A$) causes a large change in $\mathbf x$. The sensitivity is measured by the condition number $\kappa(A)=\sigma_\mathrm{max}/\sigma_\mathrm{min}$, the ratio of the largest to the smallest stretching factor of the map (the singular values, see MATH 202). Roughly, the relative error in $\mathbf x$ can be $\kappa$ times the relative error in $\mathbf b$, so you lose about $\log_{10}\kappa$ digits of accuracy. Nearly dependent columns make $\kappa$ huge: nearly parallel lattice vectors, nearly collinear descriptors in a regression, or an over-complete basis set in an electronic-structure code (a nearly singular overlap matrix). Problem 12 shows an example.

Summary of the unit#

Linear systems and their rank classify when equations can be solved; matrices are maps whose determinant measures volume scaling; a basis of lattice vectors gives coordinates, cell volume and the metric; the reciprocal lattice is the inverse-transpose of the lattice matrix and governs diffraction and Brillouin zones; orthogonal matrices are rotations and reflections; conditioning warns when numbers cannot be trusted. The sequel asks the central question about a matrix: which directions does it only stretch, and by how much?

Key takeaways
  • A system $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ is solved by Gaussian elimination in $O(n^3)$ operations; it has a unique solution, none, or infinitely many, according to the rank of $A$, and in practice one solves rather than inverts.
  • A matrix is a linear map and multiplication is composition, so in general $AB\neq BA$; the determinant is the factor by which the map scales volume, and it vanishes exactly when the map collapses space (the matrix is singular).
  • Columns of the lattice matrix $A$ are the basis vectors $\mathbf{a}_i$: Cartesian position is $\mathbf{r}=A\mathbf{f}$ for fractional coordinates $\mathbf{f}$, the cell volume is $|\det A|$, and distances use the metric $G=A^{T}A$.
  • The reciprocal lattice matrix is $B=2\pi A^{-T}$ so that $\mathbf{a}_i\cdot\mathbf{b}_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$; a plane $(hkl)$ has normal $\mathbf{G}_{hkl}$ and spacing $d=2\pi/|\mathbf{G}_{hkl}|$, which with Bragg's law explains diffraction patterns.
  • Orthogonal matrices ($Q^{T}Q=I$) preserve lengths and angles and represent rotations and reflections; an ill-conditioned matrix (huge ratio of largest to smallest stretch) makes solutions hypersensitive to small errors.

Problem 4.1Basic

In a water molecule, with the oxygen atom at the origin, the two hydrogen atoms lie at $\mathbf u=(0.757,\,0.586,\,0)$ Å and $\mathbf v=(-0.757,\,0.586,\,0)$ Å. Find the O–H bond length and the H–O–H bond angle. Also find a vector perpendicular to the molecular plane.

Hint

Bond angle: $\cos\theta=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v/(|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|)$. A normal to the plane: $\mathbf u\times\mathbf v$.

Full solution

$|\mathbf u|^2=0.757^2+0.586^2=0.5730+0.3434=0.9164$, so the bond length is $|\mathbf u|=|\mathbf v|=0.957$ Å. The dot product is $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=-0.5730+0.3434=-0.2296$. Hence $\cos\theta=-0.2296/0.9164=-0.2506$ and $\theta=\arccos(-0.2506)=104.5^\circ$, the experimental water angle.

$\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(0.586\cdot0-0\cdot0.586,\ 0\cdot(-0.757)-0.757\cdot0,\ 0.757\cdot0.586-0.586\cdot(-0.757))=(0,\,0,\,0.887)$, along $z$: perpendicular to the $xy$-plane of the molecule, with length $|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\sin\theta=0.9164\times0.968=0.887$, as expected.

0.957 Å, 104.5°, normal along $z$

Problem 4.2Basic

Solve by Gaussian elimination: $2x+y-z=8$, $-3x-y+2z=-11$, $-2x+y+2z=-3$. Then compute the determinant of the coefficient matrix from the elimination.

Hint

Eliminate $x$ from rows 2 and 3 using row 1 (multipliers $\tfrac32$ and $1$). The determinant is the product of the pivots (with a sign change for each row swap; there are none here).

Full solution

Augmented matrix: $\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}2&1&-1&8\\-3&-1&2&-11\\-2&1&2&-3\end{array}\right]$. $R_2\to R_2+\tfrac32R_1$: $(0,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac12\,|\,1)$. $R_3\to R_3+R_1$: $(0,\ 2,\ 1\,|\,5)$. Next eliminate below the second pivot $\tfrac12$: $R_3\to R_3-4R_2$: $(0,\ 0,\ 1-2\,|\,5-4)=(0,0,-1\,|\,1)$.

Back-substitution: $-z=1\Rightarrow z=-1$; $\tfrac12y+\tfrac12z=1\Rightarrow y=2-z=3$; $2x+y-z=8\Rightarrow2x=8-3-1=4\Rightarrow x=2$. Solution $(x,y,z)=(2,3,-1)$. Check row 3: $-4+3-2=-3$. ✓

Pivots are $2$, $\tfrac12$, $-1$, so $\det=2\times\tfrac12\times(-1)=-1$. Non-zero, so the system has a unique solution for every right-hand side.

$(2,3,-1)$; $\det=-1$

Problem 4.3Core

Balance the reaction $a\,\allowbreak \mathrm{Fe}+b\,\allowbreak \mathrm{O}_2\to c\,\allowbreak \mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3$ by writing atom conservation as a homogeneous linear system and finding its null space. Why is there a one-parameter family of solutions, and how do you choose the physical one? Then do the same for $a\,\allowbreak \mathrm{Al}+b\,\allowbreak \mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3\to c\,\allowbreak \mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3+d\,\allowbreak \mathrm{Fe}$ (the thermite reaction).

Hint

One equation per element: count atoms on the left minus atoms on the right. Choose the smallest positive integers.

Full solution

Fe: $a-2c=0$. O: $2b-3c=0$. So $A\mathbf x=\mathbf0$ with unknowns $(a,b,c)$ and $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-2\\0&2&-3\end{pmatrix}$, rank $2$ and $3$ unknowns, so the null space has dimension $3-2=1$: all solutions are multiples of one vector. Taking $c=t$: $a=2t$, $b=\tfrac32t$. The family is $t\,(2,\tfrac32,1)$, because multiplying a balanced equation by any factor leaves it balanced. Choose $t=2$ for the smallest integers: $(a,b,c)=(4,3,2)$, i.e. $4\mathrm{Fe}+3\mathrm{O}_2\to2\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3$.

Thermite: Al: $a-2c=0$; Fe: $2b-d=0$; O: $3b-3c=0$. Unknowns $(a,b,c,d)$: three equations, rank $3$, one-dimensional null space. From O: $b=c$; from Al: $a=2c$; from Fe: $d=2b=2c$. Take $c=1$: $(a,b,c,d)=(2,1,1,2)$: $2\mathrm{Al}+\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3\to\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3+2\mathrm{Fe}$.

$4\mathrm{Fe}+3\mathrm{O}_2\to2\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3$; $2\mathrm{Al}+\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3\to\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3+2\mathrm{Fe}$

Problem 4.4Basic

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Compute $AB$ and $BA$ and describe what $B$ does. (b) Find $\det A$ and $A^{-1}$. (c) Solve $A\mathbf x=(5,6)^T$. (d) Verify $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$.

Hint

$B$ swaps coordinates. For the inverse use the $2\times2$ formula.

Full solution

(a) $AB=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&3\end{pmatrix}$ (columns of $A$ swapped) and $BA=\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (rows of $A$ swapped). They differ, so multiplication is not commutative. $B$ reflects in the line $y=x$.

(b) $\det A=1\cdot4-2\cdot3=-2$ (negative: $A$ reverses orientation, and scales area by $2$). $A^{-1}=\dfrac1{-2}\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-3&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\1.5&-0.5\end{pmatrix}$.

(c) $\mathbf x=A^{-1}(5,6)^T=(-10+6,\ 7.5-3)=(-4,\ 4.5)$. Check: $-4+2(4.5)=5$ and $3(-4)+4(4.5)=6$. ✓

(d) $B^{-1}=B$ (swapping twice is the identity). $B^{-1}A^{-1}=B\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\1.5&-0.5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1.5&-0.5\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}$. And $\det(AB)=2\cdot3-1\cdot4=2$, so $(AB)^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-4&2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1.5&-0.5\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Equal. ✓

Problem 4.5Core

(a) Show that $R(\alpha)R(\beta)=R(\alpha+\beta)$ for 2D rotation matrices, and hence recover the angle-addition formulas for $\sin$ and $\cos$. (b) Show that $R(\theta)$ is orthogonal with $\det=+1$. (c) Rotate the point $(1,0,0)$ by $60^\circ$ about the $z$-axis. (d) A cubic crystal is rotated by $45^\circ$ about $z$. Where does the $[100]$ direction go, and what is the new direction of the $[110]$ direction?

Hint

Multiply the matrices and compare entries with $R(\alpha+\beta)$.

Full solution

(a) $R(\alpha)R(\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta&-(\cos\alpha\sin\beta+\sin\alpha\cos\beta)\\\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta&\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\end{pmatrix}$. Comparing with $R(\alpha+\beta)$ entrywise gives $\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta$ and $\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta$. Composition of rotations is a rotation by the sum of angles, and rotations in a plane commute.

(b) $R^TR=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\det R=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$.

(c) The first column of the $z$-rotation matrix: $(\cos60^\circ,\sin60^\circ,0)=(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac{\sqrt3}2,\ 0)$.

(d) $[100]=(1,0,0)\to(\tfrac{\sqrt2}2,\tfrac{\sqrt2}2,0)$, i.e. it now lies along the old $[110]$ direction. The old $[110]$, i.e. $(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$, becomes $R(45^\circ)(1,1)/\sqrt2=\bigl(\cos45^\circ-\sin45^\circ,\ \sin45^\circ+\cos45^\circ\bigr)/\sqrt2=(0,1)$, i.e. the old $[010]$ direction of the lab frame. Both results show how a rotation of the crystal relative to the lab changes the indices of a direction when measured in the lab frame, the issue behind texture and orientation-matrix conventions.

Problem 4.6Core

Show that the three vectors $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$ are linearly independent, and write the lattice point $(a,a,0)$ as an integer combination of them. Compute the nearest-neighbour distance and the number of nearest neighbours of a lattice point, and verify the packing fraction of FCC, $\pi/(3\sqrt2)\approx0.7405$.

Hint

Nearest neighbours are the lattice vectors of shortest length. Hard spheres touch along a face diagonal.

Full solution

Independent: $\det\bigl[\tfrac a2\,(\ldots)\bigr]=a^3/4\neq0$ (Example 5.1).

Express $(a,a,0)=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3$: $x$: $\tfrac a2(n_2+n_3)=a$; $y$: $\tfrac a2(n_1+n_3)=a$; $z$: $\tfrac a2(n_1+n_2)=0$. So $n_2+n_3=2$, $n_1+n_3=2$, $n_1+n_2=0$: adding the first two and subtracting the third, $2n_3=4$, $n_3=2$, $n_2=0$, $n_1=0$. So $(a,a,0)=2\mathbf a_3$: integers, as it must be for a lattice point.

Shortest vectors: $|\mathbf a_i|=\tfrac a2\sqrt2=a/\sqrt2$, and the same length for $\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j$, e.g. $\mathbf a_1-\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(-1,1,0)$. The twelve vectors $\tfrac a2(\pm1,\pm1,0)$ and permutations all have length $a/\sqrt2$: the coordination number is $12$ and the nearest-neighbour distance $d=a/\sqrt2=0.707a$.

Packing: spheres of radius $d/2=a/(2\sqrt2)$ touch; sphere volume $\tfrac43\pi\bigl(a/(2\sqrt2)\bigr)^3=\tfrac43\pi\,\allowbreak \dfrac{a^3}{16\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\pi a^3}{12\sqrt2}$. Four per cube: $\dfrac{4\pi a^3}{12\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\pi a^3}{3\sqrt2}$. Divided by $a^3$: $\dfrac\pi{3\sqrt2}=0.7405$. ✓

Problem 4.7Core

Hexagonal close-packed titanium has the cell vectors $\mathbf a_1=(a,0,0)$, $\mathbf a_2=(-\tfrac a2,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2a,0)$, $\mathbf a_3=(0,0,c)$ with $a=2.95$ Å and $c=4.68$ Å. (a) Compute the cell volume as a determinant. (b) With $2$ atoms per cell and atomic mass $47.87$ u, find the density in g/cm$^3$. (c) Find the angle between $\mathbf a_1$ and $\mathbf a_2$ and the metric tensor $G$. ($1$ u $=1.6605\times10^{-24}$ g.)

Hint

The matrix is block-triangular, so $\det=\bigl(a\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2a\bigr)c$. Convert Å$^3$ to cm$^3$ with $1$ Å$^3=10^{-24}$ cm$^3$.

Full solution

(a) $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&-a/2&0\\0&\sqrt3a/2&0\\0&0&c\end{pmatrix}$ is upper triangular, so $\det A=a\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2a\cdot c=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2a^2c=0.8660\times8.7025\times4.68=35.27$ Å$^3$.

(b) Mass per cell: $2\times47.87\times1.6605\times10^{-24}$ g $=1.590\times10^{-22}$ g. Volume: $35.27\times10^{-24}$ cm$^3$. Density $=1.590\times10^{-22}/3.527\times10^{-23}=4.51$ g/cm$^3$ (experiment: $4.506$).

(c) $\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf a_2=-\tfrac a2\cdot a=-a^2/2$, $|\mathbf a_1|=|\mathbf a_2|=a$, so $\cos\gamma=-\tfrac12$ and $\gamma=120^\circ$. Metric: $G=A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}a^2&-a^2/2&0\\-a^2/2&a^2&0\\0&0&c^2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}8.70&-4.35&0\\-4.35&8.70&0\\0&0&21.90\end{pmatrix}$ Å$^2$.

$V=35.27$ Å³; $\rho=4.51$ g/cm³; $\gamma=120^\circ$

Problem 4.8Core

A 2D hexagonal supercell has $\mathbf a_1=(4,0)$ Å and $\mathbf a_2=(-2,\,2\sqrt3)$ Å (a $120^\circ$ cell). Two atoms have fractional coordinates $\mathbf f_A=(0.1,0.1)$ and $\mathbf f_B=(0.9,0.8)$. (a) What is the distance between them without using periodicity? (b) Apply the minimum-image convention by wrapping $\Delta\mathbf f$ into $[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and find the distance. (c) Check the neighbouring images to confirm this is the minimum, and explain why in skewed cells one should check neighbours.

Hint

$\Delta\mathbf f=\mathbf f_B-\mathbf f_A=(0.8,0.7)$. Convert to Cartesian by multiplying by the lattice matrix.

Full solution

(a) $\Delta\mathbf f=(0.8,0.7)$, so $\Delta\mathbf r=0.8(4,0)+0.7(-2,3.4641)=(3.2-1.4,\ 2.4249)=(1.8,\ 2.4249)$, length $\sqrt{3.24+5.88}=3.02$ Å.

(b) Wrap: $\Delta\mathbf f\to(0.8-1,\ 0.7-1)=(-0.2,\ -0.3)$. Then $\Delta\mathbf r=-0.2(4,0)-0.3(-2,3.4641)=(-0.8+0.6,\ -1.0392)=(-0.2,\ -1.0392)$, length $\sqrt{0.04+1.0800}=1.058$ Å. The atoms are actually very close across the boundary.

(c) Other images: $(0.8,-0.3)\to(3.2+0.6,\ -1.039)=(3.8,-1.04)$, length $3.94$; $(-0.2,0.7)\to(-0.8-1.4,\ 2.425)=(-2.2,2.425)$, length $3.27$. All longer than $1.058$, so the wrapped vector is the minimum image here. In a strongly skewed cell, though, wrapping each fractional coordinate independently can pick an image that is not the shortest, because the cell is not a rectangle in Cartesian space; robust codes test the $27$ ($3^3$) neighbouring images, or reduce the cell first (Niggli or Minkowski reduction).

3.02 Å without periodicity; 1.058 Å with minimum image

Problem 4.9Challenge

(a) For the FCC primitive vectors of Problem 4.6, compute the reciprocal vectors $\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2,\mathbf b_3$. (b) Show they are the primitive vectors of a body-centred cubic lattice and find the side of its conventional cube. (c) Verify $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$ for $i=j=1$ and $i=1$, $j=2$. (d) Find the volume of the reciprocal primitive cell and relate it to $V=a^3/4$.

Hint

$\mathbf b_1=2\pi(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)/V$, then cycle the indices. A BCC lattice with cube side $s$ has primitive vectors such as $\tfrac s2(-1,1,1)$, $\tfrac s2(1,-1,1)$, $\tfrac s2(1,1,-1)$.

Full solution

(a) $\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3=\tfrac{a^2}4(1,0,1)\times(1,1,0)=\tfrac{a^2}4(0\cdot0-1\cdot1,\ 1\cdot1-1\cdot0,\ 1\cdot1-0\cdot1)=\tfrac{a^2}4(-1,1,1)$. With $V=a^3/4$: $\mathbf b_1=2\pi\dfrac{a^2/4}{a^3/4}(-1,1,1)=\dfrac{2\pi}a(-1,1,1)$. Cycling the indices (or computing $\mathbf a_3\times\mathbf a_1$ and $\mathbf a_1\times\mathbf a_2$): $\mathbf b_2=\dfrac{2\pi}a(1,-1,1)$ and $\mathbf b_3=\dfrac{2\pi}a(1,1,-1)$.

(b) These have the form $\tfrac s2(-1,1,1)$, etc., with $\tfrac s2=\dfrac{2\pi}a$, i.e. $s=\dfrac{4\pi}a$: a BCC lattice (points at cube corners and centre) with conventional cube of side $4\pi/a$.

(c) $\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf b_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)\cdot\tfrac{2\pi}a(-1,1,1)=\pi(0+1+1)=2\pi$. ✓ $\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf b_2=\pi(0,1,1)\cdot(1,-1,1)=\pi(0-1+1)=0$. ✓

(d) The reciprocal primitive volume is $(2\pi)^3/V=(2\pi)^3\cdot4/a^3$. Directly: the conventional BCC cube has volume $(4\pi/a)^3=64\pi^3/a^3$ and holds $2$ lattice points, so the primitive volume is $32\pi^3/a^3=(2\pi)^3\cdot4/a^3$. ✓ In general $V_\mathrm{recip}=(2\pi)^3/V$: the larger the real cell, the smaller its Brillouin zone.

Problem 4.10Core

Aluminium is FCC with $a=4.05$ Å. (a) Show that for a cubic lattice $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$ using $d=2\pi/|\mathbf G|$. (b) Compute $d_{111}$ and $d_{200}$. (c) For Cu K$\alpha$ X-rays ($\lambda=1.5406$ Å) find the Bragg angles $\theta$ and the diffraction angles $2\theta$. (d) Why are the reflections $(100)$ and $(110)$ absent for FCC?

Hint

Bragg: $\lambda=2d\sin\theta$. For (d), the FCC structure factor is non-zero only if $h,k,l$ are all odd or all even.

Full solution

(a) For a simple cubic lattice, $\mathbf b_i=\tfrac{2\pi}a\hat{\mathbf x}_i$, so $\mathbf G=\tfrac{2\pi}a(h,k,l)$, $|\mathbf G|=\tfrac{2\pi}a\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$, and $d=2\pi/|\mathbf G|=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$. (Using the conventional cubic cell with Miller indices defined relative to it.)

(b) $d_{111}=4.05/\sqrt3=2.338$ Å; $d_{200}=4.05/2=2.025$ Å.

(c) $\sin\theta_{111}=\dfrac{1.5406}{2(2.338)}=0.3295\Rightarrow\theta=19.24^\circ$, $2\theta=38.5^\circ$. $\sin\theta_{200}=\dfrac{1.5406}{2(2.025)}=0.3804\Rightarrow\theta=22.36^\circ$, $2\theta=44.7^\circ$. These are the two strongest peaks in a powder pattern of aluminium.

(d) In FCC the conventional cube contains lattice points at $(0,0,0)$, $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$, $(\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12)$, $(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$. The structure factor is $F=1+e^{i\pi(h+k)}+e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(k+l)}$. For $(100)$: $1-1-1+1=0$. For $(110)$: $1+1-1-1=0$. For $(111)$: $1+1+1+1=4$, and for $(200)$: $4$. So only reflections with $h,k,l$ all odd or all even appear: the first FCC peaks are $(111),(200),(220),(311),\ldots$, and a quick check of the index sequence of a pattern tells you whether a cubic metal is FCC or BCC.

$d_{111}=2.338$ Å, $d_{200}=2.025$ Å; $2\theta=38.5^\circ,\ 44.7^\circ$

Problem 4.11Core

Apply Gram–Schmidt to $\mathbf v_1=(1,1,0)$, $\mathbf v_2=(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf v_3=(0,1,1)$ to obtain an orthonormal basis $\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3$. Check orthonormality, and find the projection of $\mathbf p=(2,0,0)$ onto $\mathbf e_1$ and the remaining perpendicular component.

$\mathbf e_1=\mathbf v_1/\sqrt2$. Each subsequent step subtracts projections onto the previous $\mathbf e$’s.

Problem 4.12Challenge

Consider $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1.0001\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Solve $A\mathbf x=(2,\ 2.0001)^T$. (b) Solve it again with $\mathbf b'=(2,\ 2.0002)^T$ and compare the changes in $\mathbf b$ and $\mathbf x$. (c) Estimate the condition number from the determinant and the matrix size, and interpret the result in terms of a regression with two nearly identical descriptors.

Hint

Subtract the first equation from the second to isolate $y$. The relative change of $\mathbf b$ is tiny; compare it with the relative change in $\mathbf x$.

Full solution

(a) Subtract equation 1 from equation 2: $0.0001\,y=0.0001\Rightarrow y=1$, then $x=2-y=1$. So $\mathbf x=(1,1)$.

(b) Now $0.0001\,y=0.0002\Rightarrow y=2$, $x=0$: $\mathbf x'=(0,2)$. The relative change in $\mathbf b$ is $|\Delta\mathbf b|/|\mathbf b|=0.0001/2.83\approx3.5\times10^{-5}$, but the relative change in $\mathbf x$ is $|\Delta\mathbf x|/|\mathbf x|=\sqrt2/\sqrt2=1$ (a 100% change): amplification by about $3\times10^4$.

(c) $\det A=1.0001-1=10^{-4}$, tiny compared with the size of the entries, so $A$ is nearly singular: its eigenvalues are $\approx2.00005$ and $\approx5.0\times10^{-5}$, giving $\kappa\approx2/(5\times10^{-5})=4\times10^4$, consistent with the amplification seen (about 4 digits lost). In a regression with two nearly collinear descriptors (columns $(1,1)$ and $(1,1.0001)$), the data cannot tell the two contributions apart: tiny noise swaps weight between them, producing huge, opposite-signed coefficients (the sum is stable, the difference is not). Remedies: drop or combine descriptors, regularise (ridge regression, MATH 303), or collect data that separates them.

$(1,1)\to(0,2)$; $\kappa\approx4\times10^4$

Quiz 1Determinant

Compute $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\2&1&1\\3&-1&2\end{pmatrix}$ and say what a zero value would mean.

Solution

$1(3)-2(1)+(-1)(-5)=6$. A zero determinant would mean the columns are linearly dependent: the map flattens space, the matrix is not invertible, and $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ has either no or infinitely many solutions.

Quiz 2Linear system

Solve $2x+y=5$, $x+3y=5$ using the inverse.

Solution

$B^{-1}=\tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$, so $\mathbf x=\tfrac15(15-5,\ -5+10)=(2,1)$. Check: $4+1=5$, $2+3=5$. ✓

Quiz 3Rank

Find the rank and a null-space vector of $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Solution

Rank $2$ (row 2 $=2\times$ row 1). Null space dimension $3-2=1$: solving $x+2y+3z=0$, $x+z=0$ gives $\mathbf v=(-1,-1,1)$.

Quiz 4Cell volume

A cell has edges $(3,0,0)$, $(1,3,0)$, $(0.5,0.5,4)$ Å and contains $4$ atoms of mass $50$ u. Find the volume and the density ($1$ u $=1.6605\times10^{-24}$ g).
