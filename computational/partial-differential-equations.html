Partial differential equations

Diffusion, heat conduction, elastic waves, electrostatics, phase separation and electron wavefunctions are all described by partial differential equations (PDEs), equations for a field that varies in both space and time. The phase-field and finite element methods of later topics are numerical schemes for exactly these equations. This module develops the classical solution techniques (similarity solutions, separation of variables, Fourier methods), the physical meaning of boundary conditions, and the basic theory of how numerical schemes can go wrong. It also shows how a stability analysis in Fourier space explains spinodal decomposition.

1. Types of PDE#

A PDE relates a function of several variables, such as $c(x,t)$ or $\phi(x,y,z)$, to its partial derivatives. The general linear second-order equation in two variables, $$A\,u_{xx}+B\,u_{xy}+C\,u_{yy}+(\text{lower-order terms})=0,$$ is classified by the sign of the discriminant $B^2-4AC$, exactly as conic sections are (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola):

TypeConditionPrototypeCharacter and materials examples
Elliptic$B^2-4AC<0$Laplace $\nabla^2u=0$; Poisson $\nabla^2u=f$Equilibrium, no time: steady heat flow, electrostatics, static elasticity, steady diffusion. Solution at a point depends on all the boundary.
Parabolic$B^2-4AC=0$Diffusion $u_t=Du_{xx}$Smoothing, irreversible evolution toward equilibrium: diffusion, heat conduction, Allen–Cahn, Cahn–Hilliard. Information spreads instantly but is damped.
Hyperbolic$B^2-4AC>0$Wave $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$Propagation at finite speed without damping: sound, elastic waves, light. Reversible; discontinuities travel.

For example $u_{xx}+2u_{xy}+5u_{yy}=0$ has $B^2-4AC=4-20<0$ (elliptic), and $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$, written with $x\to$ first variable, has $A=-c^2$, $C=1$, so $-4AC=4c^2>0$ (hyperbolic). Nonlinear and higher-order equations are often described by analogy: Cahn–Hilliard is a fourth-order parabolic equation, and the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\psi_t=-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_{xx}+V\psi$ is parabolic-like with an imaginary coefficient, so it propagates waves while conserving probability.

2. Initial and boundary conditions#

A PDE alone has many solutions; physical problems specify enough data to select one. The problem is well-posed (Hadamard) if a solution exists, is unique and depends continuously on the data. Diffusion needs one initial condition $c(x,0)$ and a condition at each boundary; waves need $u$ and $u_t$ initially; elliptic problems need only boundary data. The common boundary conditions on a boundary with outward normal $\mathbf n$:

NameConditionPhysical meaning
Dirichlet$u=g$fixed value: a surface held at fixed temperature, or in equilibrium with a gas ($c_s$ set by the chemical potential)
Neumann$\partial u/\partial n=q$fixed flux; $q=0$ is an insulated (no-flux, reflecting) boundary and also a symmetry plane
Robin (mixed)$-\kappa\,\partial T/\partial n=h\,(T-T_\infty)$convective or surface-reaction exchange with an environment, with transfer coefficient $h$
Periodic$u(x+L)=u(x)$an infinite crystal or a periodic simulation cell

Dimensionless groups say which process controls. The Fourier number $\mathrm{Fo}=Dt/L^2$ compares elapsed time with the diffusion time across length $L$ (equilibrium is reached when $\mathrm{Fo}\gtrsim1$). The Biot number $\mathrm{Bi}=hL/\kappa$ compares the resistance of the body ($L/\kappa$) with the resistance of the surface exchange ($1/h$): for $\mathrm{Bi}\ll1$ the interior is uniform and the body cools as a lumped object with $T-T_\infty\propto e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau=\rho c_pV/(hA)$ (Problem 6); for $\mathrm{Bi}\gg1$ the surface is effectively at $T_\infty$ and conduction inside controls.

3. Diffusion in infinite and semi-infinite bodies#

Similarity solutions#

The equation $c_t=Dc_{xx}$ has no intrinsic length scale in an unbounded domain, so the solution can depend on $x$ and $t$ only through the combination $\eta=x/\sqrt{4Dt}$. Putting $c=f(\eta)$ reduces the PDE to an ODE and gives the solutions of the most common diffusion problems.

Worked example 3.1: constant surface concentration (the erf solution)

A semi-infinite solid $x>0$ with uniform initial concentration $c_0$ has its surface held at $c_s$ from $t=0$ (carburising, doping, hydrogen charging). Find $c(x,t)$.

With $\eta=x/\sqrt{4Dt}$: $c_t=f'\cdot\bigl(-\tfrac{\eta}{2t}\bigr)$ and $c_{xx}=f''/(4Dt)$. The equation $c_t=Dc_{xx}$ becomes $-\dfrac\eta{2t}f'=\dfrac{f''}{4t}$, i.e. $f''=-2\eta f'$. Then $f'=Ae^{-\eta^2}$ and $f=B+A\int_0^\eta e^{-s^2}\mathrm ds=B+\tfrac{A\sqrt\pi}{2}\operatorname{erf}\eta$, where $\operatorname{erf}\eta=\tfrac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^\eta e^{-s^2}\mathrm ds$ is the error function. The boundary condition $c(0,t)=c_s$ means $f(0)=c_s=B$; $c(\infty)=c_0$ means $B+\tfrac{A\sqrt\pi}2=c_0$. Therefore $$\boxed{\ c(x,t)=c_s-(c_s-c_0)\,\operatorname{erf}\!\Bigl(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\Bigr)\ }$$ The error function rises from $\operatorname{erf}0=0$ to $\operatorname{erf}\infty=1$; useful values are $\operatorname{erf}0.5=0.520$, $\operatorname{erf}1=0.843$, $\operatorname{erf}1.5=0.966$, $\operatorname{erf}2=0.995$. A contour of fixed concentration sits at fixed $\eta$, so its depth is $x=2\eta\sqrt{Dt}$: penetration depth grows as $\sqrt{Dt}$. Doubling the depth costs four times the time (Problem 3).

Worked example 3.2: thin-film source and diffusion couple

(a) A thin layer of $M$ atoms per unit area is deposited on a surface and diffuses into a solid. (b) Two semi-infinite solids with concentrations $c_1$ and $c_2$ are welded at $x=0$ (a diffusion couple).

(a) Reflecting at the surface, the thin-film solution is the diffusion kernel of M8 doubled: $c(x,t)=\dfrac{M}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\,e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}$ for $x\ge0$ (the total $\int_0^\infty c\,\mathrm dx=M$ is conserved). Plotting $\ln c$ against $x^2$ gives a straight line of slope $-1/(4Dt)$, the standard way to measure $D$ from a tracer profile (Problem 4). (b) By linearity, $c(x,t)=\dfrac{c_1+c_2}{2}+\dfrac{c_1-c_2}{2}\operatorname{erf}\!\Bigl(\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\Bigr)$ with $x$ measured from the weld: the interface stays at the mean concentration for ever, and the width of the interdiffusion zone grows as $\sqrt{Dt}$. When $D$ depends on concentration, as in most real alloys, the Boltzmann–Matano analysis extracts $D(c)$ from the measured profile, using the same similarity variable.

4. Separation of variables on a finite domain#

For a bounded domain the profile has a length scale $L$ and similarity no longer works. The method of separation of variables looks for solutions $u(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$, which turns a linear PDE into two ODEs linked by a separation constant, and then superposes them.

Worked example 4.1: a slab with fixed surfaces

Solve $c_t=Dc_{xx}$ for $0<x<L$, $c(0,t)=c(L,t)=0$, $c(x,0)=c_0$ (a plate with initially uniform hydrogen, degassing at both faces).

Step 1: separate. $c=X(x)T(t)$ gives $\dfrac{T'}{DT}=\dfrac{X''}{X}=-\lambda$, a constant (both sides depend on different variables). So $X''=-\lambda X$ and $T'=-D\lambda T$.

Step 2: boundary conditions fix the spatial modes. $X(0)=X(L)=0$ allows nontrivial solutions only for $\lambda=\bigl(\tfrac{n\pi}{L}\bigr)^2$, $X_n=\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}{L}$, $n=1,2,\ldots$ (an eigenvalue problem: these are the eigenfunctions of $-\mathrm d^2/\mathrm dx^2$, the sine modes of M8). The time part is $T_n=e^{-D(n\pi/L)^2t}$.

Step 3: superpose and fit the initial condition. $c=\sum_nb_n\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}Le^{-Dn^2\pi^2t/L^2}$ with $b_n$ the sine-series coefficients of $c_0$ on $(0,L)$: $b_n=\dfrac2L\displaystyle\int_0^Lc_0\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}Ldx=\dfrac{2c_0}{n\pi}\bigl(1-(-1)^n\bigr)$, i.e. $\dfrac{4c_0}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$ and zero for even $n$: $$c(x,t)=\frac{4c_0}{\pi}\sum_{n\ \mathrm{odd}}\frac1n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\;e^{-n^2\pi^2Dt/L^2}.$$ Mode $n$ decays with time constant $\tau_n=L^2/(n^2\pi^2D)$: the fundamental has $\tau_1=L^2/(\pi^2D)$ and higher modes decay $9,25,\ldots$ times faster, so after a short time the profile is simply the lowest sine. The fraction remaining in the slab is $\dfrac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{n\,\mathrm{odd}}\dfrac1{n^2}e^{-n^2t/\tau_1}$, and for $\mathrm{Fo}=Dt/L^2\gtrsim0.1$ the first term alone is accurate to better than $1\%$ (Problem 5).

Other boundary conditions only change the mode functions. Insulated faces ($c_x=0$) give cosines, $\cos(n\pi x/L)$, with $n=0$ the conserved mean; mixed Dirichlet/Neumann conditions give $\sin\bigl((n+\tfrac12)\pi x/L\bigr)$; convective (Robin) conditions give modes whose wavenumbers solve a transcendental equation such as $\tan(\alpha L)=\dots$ (found numerically). In every case the method works because the modes are an orthogonal basis (M5, applied to the operator $-\partial_x^2$): the PDE becomes independent ODEs, one per mode. Inhomogeneous boundary data are handled by subtracting a steady-state solution first.

5. Waves#

The one-dimensional wave equation $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$ (a string, or a longitudinal elastic wave in a rod, with $u$ the displacement) is solved by d’Alembert’s formula: any function of the form $$u(x,t)=f(x-ct)+g(x+ct)$$ solves it, because substituting $\xi=x-ct$ shows $u_{tt}=c^2f''=c^2u_{xx}$. The first term is a profile moving right at speed $c$ without changing shape, the second a profile moving left. Initial data $u(x,0)=\phi$, $u_t(x,0)=\psi$ fix $f$ and $g$. A pulse arriving at a fixed end reflects with a sign change; at a free end it reflects without. For a plane wave $u=e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ the equation gives the linear dispersion $\omega=ck$, the long-wavelength limit of the phonon dispersion derived in Problem 5.9 of M5.

On a finite bar of length $L$ with conditions at the ends, separation of variables gives standing waves. With both ends fixed, $u_n=\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}{L}\cos\dfrac{n\pi ct}{L}$, with frequencies $f_n=\dfrac{nc}{2L}$: the fundamental has half a wavelength in the bar. The same $f_n$ holds for a free–free bar (cosine modes), which is the case in resonant ultrasound spectroscopy. The wave speed of a thin rod is $c=\sqrt{E/\rho}$ (Young’s modulus over density; $5.0$ km/s in steel), and for a bulk solid the longitudinal and transverse speeds are $c_L=\sqrt{(K+\tfrac43G)/\rho}$ and $c_T=\sqrt{G/\rho}$. Measuring resonant frequencies therefore yields the elastic constants of M6, and the energy of a mode follows the oscillator laws of M7 (Problem 7).

Hyperbolic versus parabolic in numerical work

The wave equation conserves energy and transmits sharp features; the diffusion equation destroys them. Numerical schemes for waves are limited by the CFL condition $c\,\Delta t\le\Delta x$ (information must not travel more than one cell per step); schemes for diffusion are limited by $D\,\Delta t\le\tfrac12\Delta x^2$ (section 7). Using a diffusion scheme on a wave problem, or the reverse, gives spurious damping or instability.

6. Laplace and Poisson equations#

Steady states with no time dependence satisfy the Laplace equation $\nabla^2u=0$ (no sources) or the Poisson equation $\nabla^2u=-s$ (with a source density $s$): $\nabla^2T=-q/\kappa$ for heat generation $q$, $\nabla^2\phi=-\rho/\varepsilon$ for electrostatics. Solutions of Laplace’s equation (harmonic functions) obey the maximum principle: they take their extreme values on the boundary and nowhere inside (M3, Problem 3.8), and the solution with given Dirichlet data is unique. The solutions with spherical or cylindrical symmetry were found in M3: $A+B/r$ and $A+B\ln r$.

Worked example 6.1: Laplace’s equation in a rectangle

Solve $\nabla^2\phi=0$ in $0<x<a$, $0<y<b$ with $\phi=0$ on $x=0$, $x=a$, $y=0$ and $\phi(x,b)=V_0\sin(\pi x/a)$.

Separate $\phi=X(x)Y(y)$: $X''/X=-Y''/Y=-k^2$. The conditions on $x=0,a$ give $X=\sin(n\pi x/a)$ with $k=n\pi/a$. Then $Y''=k^2Y$ gives $Y=\sinh(ky)$ (to satisfy $Y(0)=0$). The top boundary data is a single sine mode with $n=1$, so no series is needed: $$\phi(x,y)=V_0\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{a}\;\frac{\sinh(\pi y/a)}{\sinh(\pi b/a)}.$$ Check: $\nabla^2\phi=\bigl[-(\pi/a)^2+(\pi/a)^2\bigr]\phi=0$ and all four boundary conditions hold. The disturbance decays into the interior as $e^{-\pi(b-y)/a}$: the field at a distance $y$ from a periodic boundary perturbation of wavelength $2a$ decays on the scale $a/\pi$, so fine surface structure is felt only near the surface. The same structure describes the potential or stress field near a rough surface (Problem 9).

In materials science: screening and the Yukawa potential

In an electrolyte or a metal, mobile charges rearrange to screen a point charge, and the linearised Poisson–Boltzmann equation reads $\nabla^2\phi=\kappa^2\phi$. For a radial field set $\phi=u(r)/r$: the spherical Laplacian gives $u''=\kappa^2u$, so $u=e^{-\kappa r}$ and $\phi=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon r}e^{-\kappa r}$, the screened (Debye–Hückel, or Yukawa) potential. The screening length $1/\kappa$ is $0.96$ nm in a $0.1$ M monovalent electrolyte at room temperature. The same equation with $\kappa=1/\ell$ is the Thomas–Fermi screening of impurities in metals and the reaction–diffusion profile of M7, Example 4.2 (Problem 10).

7. Finite differences and stability#

Replace $c(x_j,t_n)$ by $c_j^n$ on a grid with spacing $\Delta x$ and time step $\Delta t$, and the derivatives by the differences of M1. For diffusion, forward Euler in time and the central three-point stencil in space (the FTCS scheme) give $$c_j^{n+1}=c_j^n+r\,\bigl(c_{j+1}^n-2c_j^n+c_{j-1}^n\bigr),\qquad r=\frac{D\,\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}.$$ It is cheap and simple, with error $O(\Delta t)+O(\Delta x^2)$, but it does not work for every $\Delta t$.

Worked example 7.1: von Neumann stability analysis

Find the condition on $r$ for FTCS to be stable.

Insert one Fourier mode, $c_j^n=g^n\,e^{ik j\Delta x}$, where $g$ is the amplification factor per step. Then $g=1+r\bigl(e^{i\theta}-2+e^{-i\theta}\bigr)=1-2r(1-\cos\theta)=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2)$ with $\theta=k\Delta x\in[0,\pi]$. Stability needs $|g|\le1$ for every $\theta$. The worst case is $\theta=\pi$ (the sawtooth mode), where $g=1-4r$, so $-1\le1-4r$, i.e. $$r=\frac{D\,\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\le\frac12 .$$ For $r>\tfrac12$ the highest-wavenumber mode is amplified by $|1-4r|>1$ every step, with alternating sign: the familiar “checkerboard” blow-up. This is the stability limit of M7, $h<2/\lambda$, with $\lambda=4D/\Delta x^2$ the largest eigenvalue of the discrete Laplacian (the same $k_\mathrm{eff}^2$ as in M8). In two and three dimensions the limits are $r\le\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac16$.

The restriction $\Delta t\le\Delta x^2/(2D)$ is severe: refining the grid by $10$ costs $100$ times more steps in time and $10$ times more points per dimension. Implicit schemes avoid it. In the backward-Euler scheme, $c_j^{n+1}-r(c_{j+1}^{n+1}-2c_j^{n+1}+c_{j-1}^{n+1})=c_j^n$, one solves a tridiagonal linear system at each step (cost $O(N)$ with the Thomas algorithm; M4), and $g=1/(1+4r\sin^2(\theta/2))$ satisfies $|g|\le1$ for all $r$. The Crank–Nicolson scheme averages the two and is second order in time. Spectral (Fourier) and finite element discretisations are discussed in the corresponding topics.

8. A nonlinear example: spinodal decomposition#

The Cahn–Hilliard equation governs phase separation in a binary alloy with composition $c(\mathbf r,t)$: $$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\bigl(M\nabla\mu\bigr),\qquad\mu=f'(c)-\kappa\nabla^2c,$$ where $f(c)$ is the homogeneous free-energy density, $\kappa$ the gradient-energy coefficient (penalising sharp interfaces), $M$ the mobility, and $\mu$ the chemical potential. This is the continuity equation of M3 with a flux driven by $\nabla\mu$. In the miscibility gap $f''(c)<0$ (the spinodal region), uniform composition is unstable. Linear stability analysis shows how: it is the PDE version of the fixed-point analysis of M7.

Worked example 8.1: the spinodal wavelength

Linearise the Cahn–Hilliard equation about a uniform composition $c_0$ and find the growth rate of a perturbation of wavenumber $k$.

Write $c=c_0+\delta(\mathbf r,t)$ with $\delta$ small; then $f'(c)\approx f'(c_0)+f''(c_0)\delta$ and, for constant $M$, $\partial_t\delta=M\nabla^2\bigl(f''\delta-\kappa\nabla^2\delta\bigr)$. A Fourier mode $\delta\propto e^{ikx}e^{\sigma t}$ has $\nabla^2\to-k^2$, so $$\sigma(k)=-M\,k^2\bigl(f''+\kappa k^2\bigr).$$ If $f''>0$ every mode decays (stable: a single-phase region). If $f''<0$, modes with $k^2<|f''|/\kappa$ grow ($\sigma>0$): long wavelengths are unstable, but the gradient term stabilises very short ones. The growth rate is largest where $\partial\sigma/\partial(k^2)=0$: $$k_m^2=\frac{|f''|}{2\kappa},\qquad\sigma_m=\frac{M\,f''^2}{4\kappa},\qquad\lambda_m=\frac{2\pi}{k_m}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{|f''|}}.$$ The microstructure that appears is therefore a pattern with a characteristic length $\lambda_m$, set by the competition between the driving force ($|f''|$) and the interfacial energy ($\kappa$): the interconnected, fine-scale structure seen in spinodally decomposed alloys and glasses. It also shows uphill diffusion: the effective diffusivity $M f''$ is negative. Problem 11 puts in numbers.

Beyond the linear regime nonlinear terms saturate the growth, and the pattern coarsens; that is the domain of the numerical phase-field method (Phase-field modelling), whose time step, as above, must resolve $\sigma_m$ and satisfy a stability limit that, for a fourth-order operator, scales as $\Delta x^4$ for explicit schemes (so semi-implicit spectral schemes are the norm).

9. How the pieces fit together#

PDEs are classified by their character, and the type dictates both the physics and the numerics. Diffusion in unbounded media is a similarity problem with $\sqrt{Dt}$ scaling; in finite bodies it is a Fourier problem whose slowest mode sets the time constant. Waves propagate along characteristics or ring as standing modes; steady states are harmonic and extremal on the boundary. Von Neumann analysis in Fourier space predicts which numerical schemes are stable, and the same linear analysis predicts when a nonlinear system, such as an alloy in its miscibility gap, will spontaneously form patterns. The next two modules provide the other half of simulation mathematics: probability and statistics for sampling and error bars, and variational methods, which lead to the weak forms of finite elements.

Key takeaways
  • Second-order linear PDEs are elliptic (Laplace, Poisson: steady states, no time), parabolic (diffusion: smoothing, irreversible) or hyperbolic (wave: finite-speed propagation); the type decides which conditions are needed and which numerical method is stable.
  • A well-posed problem needs boundary conditions of the right kind: Dirichlet (fixed value), Neumann (fixed flux, e.g. insulated), Robin (convective exchange, with the Biot number $hL/\kappa$ saying which resistance dominates).
  • Diffusion into a semi-infinite solid has the similarity solution $c=c_s-(c_s-c_0)\,\mathrm{erf}\!\big(x/2\sqrt{Dt}\big)$, so depths scale as $\sqrt{Dt}$; a finite slab is solved by separation of variables, a Fourier series whose slowest mode has time constant $L^2/\pi^2D$.
  • The wave equation has solutions $f(x\mp ct)$ and standing modes at frequencies $nc/2L$, the basis of ultrasonic measurement of elastic constants; the Laplace equation has no interior maxima, so steady-state fields are smooth and extremes sit on boundaries.
  • Explicit finite differences for diffusion are stable only if $D\,\Delta t/\Delta x^2\le\tfrac12$ (von Neumann analysis); implicit schemes remove the limit at the cost of solving a tridiagonal system.
  • Linear stability analysis in Fourier space of the Cahn-Hilliard equation, $\sigma(k)=-Mk^2(f''+\kappa k^2)$, predicts spinodal decomposition and its characteristic wavelength $2\pi\sqrt{2\kappa/|f''|}$.

Problem 9.1Basic

Classify each equation as elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic: (a) $u_{xx}+2u_{xy}+5u_{yy}=0$; (b) $u_{xx}-4u_{xy}+u_{yy}=0$; (c) $u_t=3u_{xx}$; (d) $u_{tt}=9u_{xx}$; (e) $4u_{xx}+4u_{xy}+u_{yy}=0$. Name a physical example of each type.

Hint

Compute $B^2-4AC$ for each (for (c) and (d) treat $t$ as the second variable and note the missing derivatives).

Full solution

(a) $A=1$, $B=2$, $C=5$: $4-20=-16<0$, elliptic. (b) $B^2-4AC=16-4=12>0$, hyperbolic. (c) Only $u_{xx}$ with no second derivative in $t$: $A=3$, $B=C=0$ gives $B^2-4AC=0$, parabolic (heat/diffusion). (d) Rewriting $9u_{xx}-u_{tt}=0$: $A=9$, $C=-1$, so $B^2-4AC=36>0$, hyperbolic (waves, speed $3$). (e) $B^2-4AC=16-16=0$, parabolic.

Examples: elliptic: steady heat flow or electrostatic potential (Laplace/Poisson); parabolic: diffusion of carbon in steel; hyperbolic: sound waves in a metal bar.

Problem 9.2Basic

Verify by direct substitution that (a) $c=e^{-Dk^2t}\cos kx$ solves $c_t=Dc_{xx}$; (b) $u=\sin(x-ct)+e^{(x+ct)}$ solves $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$; (c) $\phi=\ln\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ solves $\nabla^2\phi=0$ for $(x,y)\ne0$; (d) $\phi=e^{-\kappa r}/r$ solves $\nabla^2\phi=\kappa^2\phi$ for $r\ne0$.

Hint

For (d) use the radial Laplacian $\dfrac1{r^2}(r^2\phi')'$, or $\phi=u/r$ and $\nabla^2\phi=u''/r$.

Full solution

(a) $c_t=-Dk^2c$ and $c_{xx}=-k^2c$, so $c_t=Dc_{xx}$. ✓

(b) $u_{tt}=-c^2\sin(x-ct)+c^2e^{x+ct}$ and $u_{xx}=-\sin(x-ct)+e^{x+ct}$, so $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$. ✓ (Any $f(x-ct)+g(x+ct)$ works.)

(c) $\phi=\tfrac12\ln(x^2+y^2)$: $\phi_x=x/r^2$, $\phi_{xx}=(r^2-2x^2)/r^4=(y^2-x^2)/r^4$; by symmetry $\phi_{yy}=(x^2-y^2)/r^4$. Sum $=0$. ✓ (Compare with the cylindrical solution $A+B\ln r$.)

(d) With $\phi=u/r$ and $u=e^{-\kappa r}$: $\nabla^2\phi=\dfrac1r\dfrac{\mathrm d^2(r\phi)}{\mathrm dr^2}=\dfrac{u''}r=\dfrac{\kappa^2e^{-\kappa r}}{r}=\kappa^2\phi$. ✓ Direct check via $\dfrac1{r^2}\bigl(r^2\phi'\bigr)'$ gives the same.

Problem 9.3Core

A steel with initial carbon content $0.20$ wt% is carburised at $950^\circ$C with a surface held at $1.00$ wt%. The diffusion coefficient of carbon in austenite at this temperature is $D=1.1\times10^{-11}$ m$^2$/s. (a) How long does it take for the carbon content to reach $0.50$ wt% at a depth of $1.0$ mm? (b) What is the concentration at $1.0$ mm after $8$ h? (c) If the same depth is required at $0.50$ wt% for a part twice as deep, how does the time change?

Hint

$\dfrac{c_s-c}{c_s-c_0}=\operatorname{erf}\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}$. Here $(1.0-0.5)/(1.0-0.2)=0.625$ and $\operatorname{erf}z=0.625$ for $z\approx0.627$.

Full solution

(a) $\operatorname{erf}z=0.625\Rightarrow z=0.627$ (from tables or inverse erf). Then $\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}=0.627$ gives $t=\dfrac{x^2}{4Dz^2}=\dfrac{(10^{-3})^2}{4(1.1\times10^{-11})(0.3935)}=\dfrac{10^{-6}}{1.731\times10^{-11}}=5.78\times10^4$ s $=16$ h.

(b) $t=8$ h $=28\,800$ s: $2\sqrt{Dt}=2\sqrt{1.1\times10^{-11}\times28\,800}=2\sqrt{3.17\times10^{-7}}=1.126\times10^{-3}$ m. $z=1.0/1.126=0.888$, $\operatorname{erf}0.888=0.791$. Then $c=1.0-0.8(0.791)=0.367$ wt%.

(c) The contour at fixed concentration sits at fixed $z$, so $x\propto\sqrt t$, i.e. $t\propto x^2$: twice the depth needs four times as long ($64$ h). Case depth in carburising is therefore very expensive to increase; the usual case depths are $0.5$–$2$ mm in $4$–$16$ h.

16 h; 0.37 wt%; time $\times4$

(b) What is the concentration at the surface in terms of the deposited amount $M$ per unit area? (c) Find the depth to which the tracer has spread (where $c$ drops to $1\%$ of the surface value).

Hint

$\ln[c(0)/c(x)]=x^2/(4Dt)$. For (b) use $c(0,t)=M/\sqrt{\pi Dt}$ for a surface source.

Full solution

(a) $\ln[c(0)/c(30\,\mu\mathrm m)]=2=\dfrac{x^2}{4Dt}$, so $D=\dfrac{x^2}{8t}=\dfrac{(30\times10^{-6})^2}{8(7200)}=\dfrac{9\times10^{-10}}{5.76\times10^4}=1.56\times10^{-14}$ m$^2$/s.

(b) $c(0,t)=\dfrac{M}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}=\dfrac{M}{\sqrt{\pi\times1.56\times10^{-14}\times7200}}=\dfrac{M}{1.88\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m}}$, i.e. $M/(18.8\ \mu\mathrm m)$.

(c) $\ln100=4.605=x^2/(4Dt)$, so $x=\sqrt{4.605\times4Dt}=\sqrt{4.605\times4.50\times10^{-10}}=\sqrt{2.07\times10^{-9}}=45\ \mu$m. This plot of $\ln c$ against $x^2$ being linear is the check that the mechanism is simple volume diffusion; grain-boundary or dislocation-pipe diffusion would show an additional steep tail with a different slope.

$D=1.56\times10^{-14}$ m²/s; $c(0)=M/(18.8\ \mu\mathrm m)$; 45 µm

Problem 9.5Core

A steel plate of thickness $L=2.0$ mm initially contains uniform hydrogen at concentration $c_0$ and both faces are held at zero concentration (bake-out). $D=1.0\times10^{-10}$ m$^2$/s. (a) Find the time constant of the slowest mode. (b) Write the fraction of hydrogen remaining, $M(t)/M_0=\dfrac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{n\,\mathrm{odd}}\dfrac{e^{-n^2t/\tau_1}}{n^2}$, and find the time to reduce the hydrogen content to $1\%$ using only the first term; (c) check that the third term is negligible at that time. (d) How does the time scale with thickness?

Hint

$\tau_1=L^2/(\pi^2D)$. First term: $0.8106\,e^{-t/\tau_1}$.

Full solution

(a) $\tau_1=\dfrac{L^2}{\pi^2D}=\dfrac{(2\times10^{-3})^2}{9.870\times10^{-10}}=4.05\times10^3$ s $=68$ min.

(b) $0.8106\,e^{-t/\tau_1}=0.01\Rightarrow t=\tau_1\ln81.06=4053\times4.395=1.78\times10^4$ s $=4.9$ h.

(c) The $n=3$ term is $\tfrac{0.8106}{9}e^{-9t/\tau_1}=0.0901e^{-39.6}\approx5\times10^{-19}$: utterly negligible; the first mode alone is accurate once $t\gtrsim\tau_1/4$. At early times, though, the series converges slowly and the erf solution of Example 3.1 is better, because the plate interior still “sees” no boundary.

(d) $t\propto L^2$: a plate of $4$ mm takes $20$ h, and one of $1$ mm just $1.2$ h; baking out thick parts is slow, and the decay is a standard exponential (hence the “time constant” language). Real hydrogen in steel is slowed by trapping, which lowers the effective $D$ by orders of magnitude.

$\tau_1=4.05$ ks; 1% left after 4.9 h; $t\propto L^2$

Problem 9.6Core

A steel plate of half-thickness $L=1.0$ cm ($\kappa=40$ W m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, $\rho=7850$ kg/m$^3$, $c_p=470$ J kg$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$) cools from $800^\circ$C in ambient air at $25^\circ$C with $h=25$ W m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$. (a) Compute the Biot number and say whether a lumped model is justified. (b) Find the time constant and the time to cool to $100^\circ$C. (c) Repeat (a) for a water quench ($h=2000$ W m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$): what changes?

Hint

For a slab of half-thickness $L$ cooling from both faces $V/A=L$, so $\tau=\rho c_pL/h$ and $T-T_\infty=(T_0-T_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}$.

Full solution

(a) $\mathrm{Bi}=hL/\kappa=25\times0.01/40=0.0063\ll0.1$: the interior stays nearly isothermal, so the lumped model is accurate (internal temperature differences are below about $1\%$ of the surface-to-air difference).

(b) $\tau=\dfrac{\rho c_pL}{h}=\dfrac{7850\times470\times0.01}{25}=1476$ s $=24.6$ min. $T-25=775\,e^{-t/\tau}$; for $T=100$: $e^{-t/\tau}=75/775=0.0968$, $t=\tau\ln(10.33)=1476\times2.335=3447$ s $=57$ min.

(c) $\mathrm{Bi}=2000\times0.01/40=0.5$: not small. Now the surface is cooled much faster than the interior can supply heat, so strong gradients develop: the surface temperature approaches the quench temperature quickly while the core lags (quenching stresses, cracking and martensite forming at the surface first). A transient conduction (PDE) solution, Heisler charts or a finite element simulation is needed: the lumped model gives only $\tau=7850\times470\times0.01/2000=18$ s as an optimistic estimate.

$\mathrm{Bi}=0.006$; $\tau=1476$ s; 57 min to 100 °C

Problem 9.7Core

(a) Compute the longitudinal wave speed in a thin steel rod ($E=200$ GPa, $\rho=7850$ kg/m$^3$) and the first three resonance frequencies of a free–free steel bar of length $0.30$ m. (b) An aluminium rod of length $0.250$ m has its fundamental free–free longitudinal resonance at $10.10$ kHz and $\rho=2700$ kg/m$^3$. Find $E$. (c) Why is the technique sensitive to defects?

Hint

$c=\sqrt{E/\rho}$, $f_n=nc/(2L)$, so $E=\rho(2Lf_1)^2$.

Full solution

(a) $c=\sqrt{200\times10^9/7850}=5.05$ km/s. $f_n=nc/(2L)$ with $L=0.30$: $f_1=5048/0.6=8.41$ kHz, $f_2=16.8$ kHz, $f_3=25.2$ kHz.

(b) $2Lf_1=0.5\times10\,100=5050$ m/s $=c$, so $E=\rho c^2=2700\times5050^2=6.89\times10^{10}$ Pa $=68.9$ GPa, within $2\%$ of the textbook $70$ GPa. (A free–free bar has the same $f_n=nc/2L$ as a fixed–fixed string: mode shapes $\cos(n\pi x/L)$ with antinodes at the ends.)

(c) The frequency depends on $\sqrt{E/\rho}$ and on dimensions, so any reduction in effective stiffness (cracks, porosity, texture, softening by damage) lowers the frequencies, while damping lowers $Q$ (broadens the line, M7). Precision frequency measurements ($10^{-5}$ relative) therefore detect tiny stiffness changes, and with many modes of a small sample (resonant ultrasound spectroscopy) one fits all the independent elastic constants $C_{ij}$ of a single crystal.

$c=5.05$ km/s; $f=8.4,\ 16.8,\ 25.2$ kHz; $E=68.9$ GPa

Problem 9.8Core

(a) Derive the amplification factor of FTCS for the 1D diffusion equation and the stability condition. (b) For $D=1\times10^{-9}$ m$^2$/s, find the largest stable $\Delta t$ for $\Delta x=1\ \mu$m and for $\Delta x=0.1\ \mu$m, and the number of steps to simulate $100$ s. (c) For the backward-Euler scheme find $g$ and show it is unconditionally stable. (d) Which scheme would you use for a long, fine-grid simulation?

Hint

Insert $c_j^n=g^ne^{ikj\Delta x}$. Backward Euler: $g(1+4r\sin^2(\theta/2))=1$.

Full solution

(a) $g=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2)$, with $r=D\Delta t/\Delta x^2$; worst case $\theta=\pi$: $g=1-4r\ge-1\Rightarrow r\le\tfrac12$ (Example 7.1).

(b) $\Delta t_\mathrm{max}=\Delta x^2/(2D)$. For $\Delta x=10^{-6}$ m: $\dfrac{10^{-12}}{2\times10^{-9}}=5\times10^{-4}$ s, so $100/5\times10^{-4}=2\times10^5$ steps. For $\Delta x=10^{-7}$ m: $5\times10^{-6}$ s, so $2\times10^7$ steps: refining the grid ten-fold costs $100\times$ more steps (and $10\times$ more points per step in 1D), a $1000\times$ increase in work in 1D, and $10^6$ in 3D.

(c) $c_j^{n+1}-r\,\delta^2c_j^{n+1}=c_j^n$ gives $g=\dfrac1{1+4r\sin^2(\theta/2)}\in(0,1]$ for every $r>0$ and every $\theta$: unconditionally stable and non-oscillatory.

(d) The implicit scheme (or Crank–Nicolson, or a spectral/semi-implicit scheme) allows $\Delta t$ chosen for accuracy rather than stability; each step costs a tridiagonal solve ($O(N)$ in 1D; a sparse solve or FFT in higher dimensions), which is far cheaper than $100\times$ more steps. The explicit scheme is only competitive for coarse grids and short runs, or on GPUs where its simplicity pays off.

Problem 9.9Challenge

Use the solution of Example 6.1 with $a=b$ to answer: (a) What is the value of $\phi$ at the centre of the square? (b) Show that the maximum principle holds. (c) If the top boundary has the pattern $V_0\sin(n\pi x/a)$ instead, how does the field decay into the interior, and how deep does it penetrate (distance for a factor $e^{-1}$)? (d) For a surface with a periodic roughness of wavelength $\lambda$ (so $k=2\pi/\lambda$), what is the penetration depth, and what does it imply about fine surface features?

Hint

For mode $n$: $\phi\propto\sinh(n\pi y/a)/\sinh(n\pi b/a)$, which near the top boundary behaves as $e^{-n\pi(b-y)/a}$ when $n\pi b/a\gg1$.

Full solution

(a) $\phi(a/2,a/2)=V_0\sin(\pi/2)\dfrac{\sinh(\pi/2)}{\sinh\pi}=V_0\dfrac{2.301}{11.549}=0.199\,V_0$: about a fifth of the maximum boundary value, even though the centre is only half a side from the hot edge.

(b) $\phi=V_0\sin(\pi x/a)\sinh(\pi y/a)/\sinh(\pi b/a)$ is zero on three sides and has maximum $V_0$ along the top edge (at $x=a/2$); for $y<b$ the factor $\sinh(\pi y/a)/\sinh(\pi b/a)<1$, so $\phi<V_0$ in the interior: the extremum lies on the boundary. Also $\phi\ge0$ everywhere.

(c) For mode $n$: $\phi_n\propto\sinh(n\pi y/a)/\sinh(n\pi b/a)\approx e^{-n\pi(b-y)/a}$ when $n\pi b/a$ is large. The decay length is $a/(n\pi)$.

(d) With wavenumber $k=n\pi/a=2\pi/\lambda$, the field of a surface perturbation of wavelength $\lambda$ decays as $e^{-ky}$ with depth $y$, with penetration depth $1/k=\lambda/2\pi$. Fine roughness ($\lambda=100$ nm) is felt only within about $16$ nm of the surface; long-wavelength variations reach deep. Laplace-type fields (electrostatic, steady-state temperature, elastic fields from surface loads) act as low-pass filters of boundary data, which is why surface roughness matters for thin films only if it is comparable with the film thickness, and why inverse problems (inferring surface data from interior measurements) are ill-conditioned.

Problem 9.10Challenge

(a) Solve the radial screening problem $\nabla^2\phi=\kappa^2\phi$ with $\phi\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ and $\phi\approx q/(4\pi\varepsilon r)$ near the charge. (b) The Debye length is $\lambda_D=1/\kappa=\sqrt{\varepsilon k_\mathrm{B}T/(2N_Ae^2I)}$ for a $z{:}z$ electrolyte of ionic strength $I$; show that at $298$ K in water this equals $0.304/\sqrt{I}$ nm ($I$ in mol/L) and evaluate it for $0.10$ M and for $0.001$ M. (c) What fraction of the unscreened potential remains at $r=3\lambda_D$? (d) Compare the dimensionality: in 1D, solve $\phi''=\kappa^2\phi$ with $\phi(0)=\phi_0$.

Hint

(a) Set $\phi=u/r$. (b) Scale: $\lambda_D\propto I^{-1/2}$, so $\lambda_D(0.001)=\lambda_D(0.1)\sqrt{100}$.

Full solution

(a) With $\phi=u/r$, $\nabla^2\phi=u''/r$, so $u''=\kappa^2u$ and $u=Ae^{-\kappa r}+Be^{\kappa r}$. Decay at infinity needs $B=0$, and matching the Coulomb behaviour as $r\to0$ gives $A=q/(4\pi\varepsilon)$: $\phi=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon r}e^{-\kappa r}$, the screened (Yukawa) potential.

(b) The standard constant: at $298$ K in water ($\varepsilon_r=78.4$), $\lambda_D=0.304\,\mathrm{nm}/\sqrt{I[\mathrm{M}]}$. For $I=0.10$ M: $0.304/0.316=0.96$ nm. For $0.001$ M: $0.304/0.0316=9.6$ nm (ten times larger: $\sqrt{100}$). So in dilute solutions electrostatic interactions extend over tens of nanometres; in concentrated ones, screening reduces the range to a few atomic spacings, which is why electrolyte concentration controls colloidal stability and double-layer thickness at electrodes.

(c) The screened potential relative to the bare Coulomb value is $e^{-\kappa r}$; at $r=3\lambda_D$ it is $e^{-3}=0.050$: $5\%$ remains.

(d) In 1D, $\phi''=\kappa^2\phi$ gives $\phi=\phi_0e^{-\kappa x}$: pure exponential decay on the length $1/\kappa$ (the Gouy–Chapman diffuse layer next to a charged surface). The extra $1/r$ in 3D is just the geometric dilution of a point source. The same mathematics appears in the reaction–diffusion profile of M7, Example 4.2, with $\kappa=\sqrt{k/D}$.

Problem 9.11Challenge

An alloy has $f''(c_0)=-5\times10^{8}$ J/m$^3$ in its spinodal region and gradient coefficient $\kappa=1.0\times10^{-9}$ J/m. The mobility satisfies $M|f''|=D_\mathrm{eff}=1.0\times10^{-18}$ m$^2$/s. (a) Find the range of unstable wavenumbers. (b) Find the fastest-growing wavenumber, wavelength and growth rate. (c) Give the time constant for the fastest mode and the wavelength of the resulting pattern. (d) What happens to a mode with $k$ above the cut-off, and what would a positive $f''$ do?

Hint

$\sigma(k)=-Mk^2(f''+\kappa k^2)$. Unstable when $k^2<|f''|/\kappa$; maximum at $k_m^2=|f''|/(2\kappa)$ with $\sigma_m=Mf''^2/(4\kappa)=D_\mathrm{eff}k_m^2/2$.

Full solution

(a) Unstable for $k^2<|f''|/\kappa=5\times10^{8}/10^{-9}=5\times10^{17}$ m$^{-2}$, i.e. $k<7.1\times10^{8}$ m$^{-1}$ ($\lambda>8.9$ nm).

(b) $k_m^2=|f''|/(2\kappa)=2.5\times10^{17}$ m$^{-2}$, so $k_m=5.0\times10^{8}$ m$^{-1}$ and $\lambda_m=2\pi/k_m=12.6$ nm. $\sigma_m=D_\mathrm{eff}k_m^2/2=\tfrac12(10^{-18})(2.5\times10^{17})=0.125$ s$^{-1}$. (Check: $Mf''^2/(4\kappa)$ with $M=D_\mathrm{eff}/|f''|$ gives $D_\mathrm{eff}|f''|/(4\kappa)=10^{-18}\times5\times10^{8}/(4\times10^{-9})=0.125$. ✓)

(c) $\tau_m=1/\sigma_m=8$ s, and the pattern has the characteristic wavelength $\approx12.6$ nm, set only by $\kappa/|f''|$, not by the mobility. (Initially the amplitude grows as $e^{\sigma_mt}$ from thermal fluctuations of size $\sim0.01$, so that composition reaches order one after $\ln(100)\times8\approx37$ s. Real spinodal decomposition in solids takes minutes to hours because $D_\mathrm{eff}$ is much smaller.)

(d) For $k>7.1\times10^{8}$ m$^{-1}$, $f''+\kappa k^2>0$ and $\sigma<0$: short-wavelength fluctuations decay, as the gradient energy cost of sharp structure outweighs the driving force. Without the $\kappa$ term ($\kappa=0$) all wavelengths would grow, the fastest being the shortest, which is mathematically ill-posed (an infinite growth rate at $k\to\infty$): the gradient term is what makes the model well-posed. For $f''>0$ (outside the spinodal), $\sigma<0$ at all $k$: fluctuations decay by ordinary diffusion with $D=Mf''$ and the alloy remains homogeneous.

$k<7.1\times10^8$ m⁻¹; $\lambda_m=12.6$ nm; $\sigma_m=0.125$ s⁻¹

Problem 9.12Challenge

(a) Uniqueness. Suppose $c_1$ and $c_2$ both solve $c_t=Dc_{xx}$ on $(0,L)$ with the same initial data and with $c=0$ at both ends. Let $w=c_1-c_2$ and $E(t)=\tfrac12\int_0^Lw^2\mathrm dx$. Show $\mathrm dE/\mathrm dt=-D\int_0^Lw_x^2\mathrm dx\le0$ and conclude $w\equiv0$. (b) Backward diffusion. If you try to run the diffusion equation backwards ($t\to-t$, i.e. $c_t=-Dc_{xx}$), what happens to a Fourier mode $e^{ikx}$? A measured profile contains noise of relative amplitude $10^{-6}$ at a wavelength of $0.1\ \mu$m; with $D=10^{-14}$ m$^2$/s, by what factor is this noise amplified when reconstructing the profile $1$ hour earlier? (c) Why can diffusion not be “undone” in practice?

Hint

(a) Integrate $\int ww_t=D\int ww_{xx}$ by parts. (b) The mode amplifies as $e^{+Dk^2t}$.

Full solution

(a) $w$ satisfies the same equation with $w(x,0)=0$, $w(0,t)=w(L,t)=0$. $\dfrac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dt}=\int_0^Lww_t\,\mathrm dx=D\int_0^Lww_{xx}\,\mathrm dx=D\bigl[ww_x\bigr]_0^L-D\int_0^Lw_x^2\,\mathrm dx=-D\int_0^Lw_x^2\,\mathrm dx\le0$, because the boundary term vanishes ($w=0$ at the ends). So $E$ never increases, and $E(0)=0$, $E\ge0$ force $E(t)=0$ for all $t$, i.e. $w\equiv0$. The solution is unique (integration by parts is the “energy method”, the same step that produces the weak form of FEM in M12).

(b) Reversing time flips the sign: $\hat c(k,-t)=\hat c(k,0)\,e^{+Dk^2t}$, so each mode grows rather than decays, and the fastest for the shortest wavelengths. Here $k=2\pi/(10^{-7})=6.28\times10^7$ m$^{-1}$, $Dk^2t=10^{-14}\times3.95\times10^{15}\times3600=1.42\times10^{5}$. The amplification factor is $e^{1.4\times10^5}$: astronomically large (about $10^{62\,000}$), so any noise at that wavelength swamps the answer. Even at $\lambda=1\ \mu$m: $k^2$ is $100$ times smaller, $Dk^2t=1.4\times10^3$, still $e^{1400}$.

(c) Diffusion irreversibly destroys the fine-scale information: the forward map multiplies short-wavelength content by $e^{-Dk^2t}$, which is effectively zero beyond a cutoff, so it cannot be recovered. The backward problem is ill-posed (the solution does not depend continuously on the data). In practice deconvolution of blurred data needs regularisation (truncating or damping the high-$k$ modes, as in the SVD truncation of M5 and the ridge regression of M11), and this is also the mathematical face of the second law: diffusion increases entropy.
