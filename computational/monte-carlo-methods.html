Monte Carlo and kinetic Monte Carlo

Many questions about a material ask for an average over the possible arrangements of its atoms: the equilibrium composition of coexisting phases, the degree of order of an alloy, the adsorbed amount on a surface, the pressure of a liquid, the free energy of a defect. Statistical mechanics writes each as a sum or integral over an astronomically large configuration space; Monte Carlo (MC) methods evaluate it by random sampling, steering the samples towards the configurations that matter. A second family, kinetic Monte Carlo, follows the evolution of a system in real time by picking events at random with the right probabilities, and reaches times that molecular dynamics cannot. Both methods are simple to program, and both are easy to get subtly wrong; this course teaches the principles (detailed balance, ergodicity, correlation), applies them to the standard models of materials science, and always checks them against an exact result or an independent method. The labs LAB 504 (the Ising model with numba) and the program of COMP 102 are companions; the programs of this course are in labs/cms-503.

Lecture 1. Importance sampling and the Metropolis algorithm#

Why random sampling

The average of a function $A$ over the Boltzmann distribution of a system of $N$ particles is $\langle A\rangle=\int A(\mathbf r^N)\,\mathrm e^{-U(\mathbf r^N)/k_BT}\mathrm d\mathbf r^N/Z$, an integral in $3N$ dimensions. Grid quadrature fails because of the curse of dimensionality: a rule of order $p$ with $m$ points per axis has an error $\sim m^{-p}=n^{-p/d}$ for a total of $n=m^d$ evaluations: with $d=100$ and a trapezoidal rule ($p=2$) one gets $n^{-0.02}$, a useless accuracy. Random sampling has an error $\sigma/\sqrt N$ independent of $d$. The experiment below integrates $\mathrm e^{-|\mathbf x|^2}$ over the unit hypercube with $10^5$ function evaluations by a midpoint grid and by plain Monte Carlo:

In one to three dimensions the grid is vastly better (relative error $4\times10^{-12}$, $8\times10^{-7}$, $6\times10^{-5}$) and Monte Carlo gives $10^{-3}$–$10^{-5}$ with the random scatter expected from $1/\sqrt N$. Beyond $d\approx4$ the grid has only $17$, $6$, $4$, $3$, $2$ points per axis and its error rises ($6\times10^{-4}$, $7\times10^{-3}$, $2\times10^{-2}$, $5\times10^{-2}$, $0.13$) while Monte Carlo stays at its $10^{-3}$ level: the two cross at four to six dimensions. A problem with $3N$ coordinates has $d$ of order $10^2$–$10^6$: random sampling is the only option.

Uniform random points are still wasteful: for the Boltzmann integral almost all points of configuration space have an astronomically small weight (two atoms overlapping gives $\mathrm e^{-U/k_BT}\approx\mathrm e^{-100}$). Importance sampling draws configurations with a probability proportional to the weight itself, $P(\mathbf r^N)\propto\mathrm e^{-U/k_BT}$; then $\langle A\rangle$ is simply the arithmetic mean of $A$ over the samples. The question is how to generate samples from a distribution that one can evaluate only up to a normalising constant $Z$, which one does not know. Metropolis, Rosenbluth, Rosenbluth, Teller and Teller (1953) gave the answer: a Markov chain, a random walk in configuration space with transition probabilities $W(a\to b)$ that depend only on the current state and are designed so that the chain converges to the target $P$.

Detailed balance and the Metropolis rule

A Markov chain has the stationary distribution $P$ if the transition probabilities satisfy detailed balance, $P(a)\,W(a\to b)=P(b)\,W(b\to a)$ for every pair of states (a sufficient, not necessary, condition: sum over $a$ to see that $P$ is invariant), and it converges to $P$ from any start if the chain is ergodic (every state can be reached from every other in a finite number of steps) and aperiodic. The Metropolis construction: propose a move $a\to b$ with a symmetric probability $T(a\to b)=T(b\to a)$ (a random displacement of one atom), and accept it with probability $\mathrm{acc}(a\to b)=\min\{1,P(b)/P(a)\}=\min\{1,\mathrm e^{-\Delta E/k_BT}\}$. Check: for $E_b>E_a$, $P(a)\cdot T\cdot\mathrm e^{-(E_b-E_a)/k_BT}=P(b)\cdot T\cdot1$, because $P(b)/P(a)=\mathrm e^{-(E_b-E_a)/k_BT}$; for $E_b\le E_a$ the roles swap. The rule needs only the energy difference of the move, never $Z$; moves that lower the energy are always accepted, moves that raise it are accepted with the Boltzmann factor. A rejected move is counted again (the old configuration is added to the average a second time): omitting this is a classic mistake that biases the result.

The conditions have practical consequences. Ergodicity fails for single-atom displacements in a system with a rigid constraint (a polymer with fixed bonds needs pivot moves) and in glassy or two-phase systems where the barrier between regions is high (the chain stays in one basin: it looks converged but is not). Detailed balance fails if the proposal is not symmetric (a move that is biased in one direction needs the correction factor $T(b\to a)/T(a\to b)$ in the acceptance), if the random-number generator is poor, if the sequence of the moves is chosen depending on the state, or if the acceptance uses a different weight than the one being sampled. The statistical error comes from the correlation between successive samples (Lecture 6): the number of independent samples is $N/(2\tau_\mathrm{int})$.

Lecture 2. Lattice models: the alloy as an Ising model#

Consider a binary alloy A–B on a lattice in which each site is occupied by A or B, with nearest-neighbour bond energies $\epsilon_\mathrm{AA}$, $\epsilon_\mathrm{BB}$ and $\epsilon_\mathrm{AB}$. Define a spin $s_i=+1$ for A and $-1$ for B. The energy of a bond is a function of the two spins that can always be written $\epsilon(s_i,s_j)=a+b\,(s_i+s_j)-J\,s_is_j$ with $\epsilon_\mathrm{AA}=a+2b-J$, $\epsilon_\mathrm{BB}=a-2b-J$ and $\epsilon_\mathrm{AB}=a+J$ (bond energies, negative for attraction). Solving for the coupling, $$J=\tfrac12\left[\epsilon_\mathrm{AB}-\tfrac12(\epsilon_\mathrm{AA}+\epsilon_\mathrm{BB})\right],$$ and the sum over bonds gives $E=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j+E_0$, where $E_0$ depends only on the composition (it is constant in a canonical simulation). If unlike pairs are less favourable than the average of the like pairs ($J>0$) the alloy has a tendency to cluster; if they are more favourable ($J<0$) it orders. The regular-solution parameter of MSE 301 is $\Omega=zN_A[\epsilon_\mathrm{AB}-\tfrac12(\epsilon_\mathrm{AA}+\epsilon_\mathrm{BB})]=2zJN_A$ for coordination number $z$. The Ising model of PHYS 301 is therefore the lattice model of an alloy (or a lattice gas, with occupied/empty sites), and $J>0$ gives phase separation into an A-rich and a B-rich phase below a critical temperature, exactly the miscibility gap of MSE 301. The simulation of a real alloy conserves the composition (atoms swap places, Kawasaki dynamics) rather than flipping spins, but the equilibrium coexistence curve is the same as that of the spin-flip (Glauber) dynamics of the Ising magnet. The regular-solution theory of Lecture 3 of MSE 301 is the mean-field (Bragg–Williams) approximation of this model, with $T_c=\Omega/2R=zJ/k_B$ ($z=4$ for the square lattice). How good is the approximation? We compute the exact coexistence of the square-lattice model by Metropolis Monte Carlo, with $32\times32$ sites, $20\,000$ sweeps per temperature, and compare with the Onsager exact solution $m(T)=[1-\sinh^{-4}(2J/k_BT)]^{1/8}$ and the mean-field self-consistent equation $m=\tanh(zJm/k_BT)$; the composition of the coexisting phases is $x_B=(1\mp m)/2$:

Monte Carlo reproduces the exact solution: at $T=1.8$, $2.0$, $2.1$ and $2.2$ ($T/T_c=0.79$–$0.97$) the simulated order parameters are $0.9571\pm0.0003$, $0.9113\pm0.0007$, $0.8703\pm0.0017$ and $0.7813\pm0.0052$ against $0.9569$, $0.9113$, $0.8687$ and $0.7848$ (Onsager): agreement within one or two standard errors, close to the critical point included. This is the verification that the code (the Metropolis rule, the periodic boundaries, the energy bookkeeping) is correct, and it is the standard practice before any new calculation: reproduce a case with a known answer. Mean-field theory is wrong in two ways. (i) The critical temperature: $T_c^\mathrm{MF}=4.0$ against the exact $2.2692$, too high by $76\%$ (in three dimensions the overestimate is smaller: $6.0$ against $4.51$, $33\%$, for the simple cubic lattice, $22\%$ for fcc, and the mean field becomes exact only in infinite dimension). (ii) The shape of the gap: at $T/T_c=0.925$ the solubility (the composition of the minority component in the coexisting phase) is $x_B=0.0648$ in the simulation ($0.0657$ from the exact Onsager value) and only $0.0264$ in the mean-field theory, a factor of $2.5$ too small; at $T/T_c=0.793$ it is $0.0214$ against $0.0130$. The mean-field approximation ignores the short-range clustering and fluctuations that increase the entropy of the mixed state and so the solubility. The consequence for alloy thermodynamics is large: a regular-solution model whose interaction parameter was fitted to the experimental critical temperature underestimates the solubility limit, and CALPHAD parameters absorb the difference in effective temperature-dependent interaction parameters (the entropy-like terms $b_k$ of Lecture 6 of MSE 301). Above $T_c$ the simulated $|m|$ is not zero ($0.2009$ at $T=2.5$, $0.0831$ at $3.0$): a finite-size effect of the order $1/\sqrt{N}=0.03$ plus short-range order, which vanishes only in the infinite system.

What lattice Monte Carlo is used for in practice

(i) Cluster expansions: the energy of a lattice configuration is fitted to DFT energies as a sum over clusters of sites, $E=\sum_\alpha J_\alpha\Pi_\alpha(\sigma)$; Monte Carlo on the cluster expansion gives the order–disorder transitions, solubility limits and short-range order of real alloys with first-principles accuracy (the ATAT and CASM codes). (ii) Surface adsorption and catalysis: lattice gases with adsorbate interactions, grand canonical (the chemical potential is set by the gas pressure). (iii) Magnetism and ferroelectrics with spin Hamiltonians (Heisenberg, XY). (iv) Microstructure: the Potts model for grain growth, with spin states as grain orientations. The Hamiltonian is a model that has to be justified, but the sampling of its statistical mechanics is exact up to the statistical error.

Lecture 3. Off-lattice Monte Carlo and the comparison with molecular dynamics#

For atoms in continuous space the Metropolis algorithm is the same: pick an atom at random, propose a displacement $\mathbf r_i\to\mathbf r_i+\Delta\,(\boldsymbol\xi-\tfrac12)$ with $\boldsymbol\xi$ uniform in the unit cube, compute the energy change $\Delta E$ (only the pairs involving atom $i$: $O(N)$ operations), accept with probability $\min(1,\mathrm e^{-\Delta E/k_BT})$. $N$ attempted moves are one sweep, the unit of MC time. No forces and no velocities are needed, and the momentum distribution is trivial (Maxwell), so MC gives the same configurational averages as MD in the canonical ensemble, but not the dynamics. The maximum displacement $\Delta$ is a parameter that controls the acceptance rate: too small and almost every move is accepted but the configuration moves slowly; too large and almost every move is rejected. The comparison with MD is a strong test of both methods: the same state point, the same potential (truncated at $2.5\sigma$, with the long-range tail corrections to the energy and the pressure $U_\mathrm{tail}/N=\tfrac83\pi\rho[\tfrac13r_c^{-9}-r_c^{-3}]=-0.4520$ and $P_\mathrm{tail}=\tfrac{16}3\pi\rho^2[\tfrac23r_c^{-9}-r_c^{-3}]=-0.7621$ added), $256$ atoms. The pressure in MC is obtained from the virial of the forces, $P=\rho k_BT+\langle W\rangle/3V$, evaluated on the sampled configurations (forces are not used for the sampling but they are a function of the positions):

"""Metropolis Monte Carlo of the Lennard-Jones fluid (256 atoms, T* = 0.85, rho* = 0.8442), against molecular dynamics (LAMMPS, NVT) at the same state point."""
            import numpy as np
            from numba import njit

            @njit
            def energy_virial(x, L, rc2):
                n = x.shape[0]; e = 0.0; w = 0.0
                for i in range(n - 1):
                    for j in range(i + 1, n):
                        d = x[i] - x[j]; d -= L * np.round(d / L); r2 = d @ d
                        if r2 < rc2:
                            ir6 = (1.0 / r2)**3; e += 4 * (ir6 * ir6 - ir6); w += 24 * (2 * ir6 * ir6 - ir6)
                return e, w
            @njit
            def one_atom_energy(x, i, xi, L, rc2):
                e = 0.0
                for j in range(x.shape[0]):
                    if j != i:
                        d = xi - x[j]; d -= L * np.round(d / L); r2 = d @ d
                        if r2 < rc2:
                            ir6 = (1.0 / r2)**3; e += 4 * (ir6 * ir6 - ir6)
                return e
            @njit
            def mc(x, L, T, delta, nsweeps, nsample, rc2, seed):
                np.random.seed(seed); n = x.shape[0]; acc = 0; tot = 0
                es = np.empty(nsweeps // nsample); ps = np.empty(nsweeps // nsample); k = 0
                for sw in range(nsweeps):
                    for _ in range(n):
                        i = np.random.randint(n); xn = x[i] + delta * (np.random.random(3) - 0.5)
                        dE = one_atom_energy(x, i, xn, L, rc2) - one_atom_energy(x, i, x[i], L, rc2)
                        tot += 1
                        if dE <= 0 or np.random.random() < np.exp(-dE / T): x[i] = xn; acc += 1
                    if sw % nsample == 0 and sw >= 0:
                        e, w = energy_virial(x, L, rc2); es[k] = e; ps[k] = w; k += 1
                return es, ps, acc / tot

Other ensembles and moves

The advantage of MC is that any move can be used as long as detailed balance holds in the chosen ensemble, including moves that are physically unrealistic but efficient:

  • NPT: add a volume move $V\to V^\prime$ with scaling of all coordinates, accepted with $\min\{1,\exp[-(\Delta U+P\Delta V)/k_BT+N\ln(V^\prime/V)]\}$ (the volume is sampled uniformly in $\ln V$ or $V$, with the corresponding Jacobian).
  • Grand canonical (GCMC): insert or delete a particle, with acceptances $\min\{1,\ \frac{V}{\Lambda^3(N+1)}\mathrm e^{(\mu-\Delta U)/k_BT}\}$ and $\min\{1,\ \frac{\Lambda^3N}{V}\mathrm e^{(-\mu-\Delta U)/k_BT}\}$ ($\Lambda$ the thermal wavelength): the standard method for adsorption in porous materials (zeolites, metal–organic frameworks) and for vapour–liquid equilibria; insertions into a dense liquid are rarely accepted and need biased insertion schemes.
  • Swap moves in alloys: exchange the identities of an A and a B atom (even at long distance), which equilibrates the chemical order of a solid in a few thousand sweeps where MD would need seconds of real time because of the slow diffusion. Hybrid MD/MC with swaps is the standard to equilibrate the chemical order of high-entropy alloys.
  • Cluster moves near a critical point (Swendsen–Wang, Wolff) and replica exchange (parallel tempering) for rough landscapes (Lecture 4).

Lecture 4. The density of states: Wang–Landau sampling#

The canonical simulations above give averages at one temperature at a time. If the density of states $g(E)$ (the number of configurations with energy $E$) is known, the whole thermodynamics follows at every temperature: $Z(T)=\sum_Eg(E)\mathrm e^{-E/k_BT}$, $U=\sum Eg\,\mathrm e^{-E/kT}/Z$, $C=(\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2)/k_BT^2$, and also the free energy $F=-k_BT\ln Z$ and entropy, which ordinary Metropolis cannot give (they need the normalisation $Z$). The Wang–Landau algorithm (2001) estimates $\ln g(E)$ in a random walk in energy space: a move $E\to E^\prime$ is accepted with probability $\min\{1,g(E)/g(E^\prime)\}$ using the current estimate of $g$, and after each step $\ln g(E_\mathrm{current})$ is increased by a modification factor $\ln f$; the histogram of visited energies becomes flat when $g$ is right, then $\ln f$ is halved (typically from $1$ to $10^{-6}$ or lower), and the walk continues. A walk that visits all energies with equal probability crosses free-energy barriers (such as the one between the two phases at a first-order transition) easily. We test it on a model whose density of states can be found by exact enumeration, the $4\times4$ Ising lattice ($2^{16}=65\,536$ states):

Lecture 5. Kinetic Monte Carlo#

Metropolis Monte Carlo has no real time: a “sweep” is not a physical unit. For processes made of discrete, rare events (a vacancy hops, an adatom jumps, a molecule desorbs, a precipitate loses an atom) there is an algorithm that does give time. Each event $k$ has a rate $r_k$ (s$^{-1}$), usually from transition-state theory, $r_k=\nu_k\,\mathrm e^{-E_{a,k}/k_BT}$, with the attempt frequency $\nu_k\approx10^{12}$–$10^{13}$ s$^{-1}$ and the barrier $E_{a,k}$ from DFT with nudged elastic band calculations or from empirical potentials. If the events are independent and Poissonian, the system sitting in a state waits an exponentially distributed time before the next event, and the event that occurs is chosen with probability proportional to its rate. The residence-time (BKL, Gillespie) algorithm exploits this exactly:

  1. List all possible events in the current state and their rates $r_k$; $R=\sum_kr_k$.
  2. Choose event $k$ with probability $r_k/R$ (draw $u_1\in[0,1)$ and find the $k$ for which the cumulative sum of rates crosses $u_1R$).
  3. Execute the event, update the state and the list of events.
  4. Advance the clock by $\Delta t=-\ln(u_2)/R$, with $u_2\in(0,1]$ uniform. Go to 1.

There is no rejection: every step makes an event, however rare the events are, so a kinetic MC run spans microseconds to hours of physical time when MD would need $10^{12}$–$10^{20}$ steps. It is the method of choice for diffusion-controlled kinetics in solids (vacancy-mediated diffusion in alloys, ordering, precipitation), epitaxial growth, catalysis (surface reaction networks), radiation damage evolution. The check below uses a single vacancy hopping on a simple cubic lattice, for which the diffusion coefficient is known exactly: $D=\Gamma a^2/6$, where $\Gamma=6r$ is the total jump rate:

What kinetic Monte Carlo needs, and where it fails
  • The event catalogue must be complete and the rates correct. Missing a mechanism (a concerted move, a long jump, an unexpected relaxation) gives wrong kinetics without any warning. For lattice-based KMC all the barriers (which depend on the local environment: the neighbours of the jumping atom) have to be computed or fitted, often by a cluster expansion of the barriers, and for off-lattice KMC the transition states are searched on the fly (adaptive kinetic MC, with the dimer method).
  • Separation of time scales: if some events are much faster than others (a fast back-and-forth hop between two sites), the algorithm spends all its steps on them (“flickers”), and one has to treat the fast subsystem analytically or merge the states.
  • Harmonic transition-state theory applies when the barrier is much larger than $k_BT$ and the dynamics after crossing is not recrossing; for barriers below $\approx5k_BT$ use MD.
  • Cost: the rate list must be updated efficiently (binary-tree or composition-rejection selection give $O(\log N)$ or $O(1)$ per event); for $10^6$ sites and $10^6$ events per second the time of an event is a few microseconds.

Lecture 6. Error analysis and efficient sampling#

Successive configurations of a Markov chain are correlated, so a sample of length $N$ contains only $N/(2\tau_\mathrm{int})$ independent measurements, where the integrated autocorrelation time $\tau_\mathrm{int}=\tfrac12+\sum_{t\ge1}A(t)$ is the sum of the normalised autocorrelation function of the observable (COMP 102, LAB 504). The error of the mean is $\sigma\sqrt{2\tau_\mathrm{int}/N}$. Near a critical point the correlation length diverges and the local-update Metropolis algorithm suffers critical slowing down: $\tau\sim\xi^z$ with $z\approx2.2$. For the $32\times32$ Ising model:

"""Autocorrelation times of the Metropolis and Wolff (cluster) algorithms for the 2D Ising model (L = 32) at several temperatures."""

            def metropolis_sweep(s, beta, L):
                for _ in range(L * L):

Good practice

  • Equilibration: discard the initial part until observables stop drifting; start from two different states (ordered and disordered) and check that they give the same result (history dependence is the signature of a metastable state or of non-ergodicity).
  • Error bars from autocorrelation analysis or from blocks longer than $\tau$, not from the naive formula; use several independent runs with different seeds.
  • Finite-size effects: repeat for several system sizes; near a critical point use finite-size scaling (the Binder cumulant of LAB 504).
  • Random numbers: a good generator with a documented seed (COMP 202, Lecture 8); independent streams for independent runs.
  • Verification: reproduce a known result (the exact Onsager magnetisation, the exact density of states of a small lattice, the exact $D$ of a random walker) every time a code is changed.

Key takeaways
  • Monte Carlo evaluates averages over a probability distribution by sampling it: its error falls as N^(-1/2) whatever the dimension, so it beats grid quadrature above about 4 to 6 dimensions (relative error 1.3e-1 for a 12-dimensional grid with 2 points per axis against 7e-4 for Monte Carlo at the same cost of 1e5 evaluations), and the Metropolis rule min(1, exp(-dE/kT)) samples the Boltzmann distribution because it satisfies detailed balance.
  • On a lattice the Ising model is the binary alloy: for the square lattice the Monte Carlo order parameter agrees with Onsager's exact result within the statistical error (0.8703 +- 0.0017 against 0.8687 at T = 2.10 J/k, 0.7813 +- 0.0052 against 0.7848 at 2.20), whereas the mean-field (regular-solution) theory places the critical temperature at 4.0 instead of 2.269 (76 percent too high) and has the wrong shape of the miscibility gap (x = 0.0264 against the true 0.0648 at T/Tc = 0.925).
  • Off-lattice Metropolis Monte Carlo of a Lennard-Jones liquid reproduces the molecular-dynamics averages (see the table for U/N and P at T* = 0.85, rho* = 0.8442); the step size must be tuned (acceptance 0.73, 0.49, 0.32, 0.16 and 0.06 for steps of 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.45 and 0.7) and the efficiency of exploration is largest at acceptance of order 0.2 to 0.5.
  • Wang-Landau sampling estimates the density of states g(E) with a flat-histogram random walk: on the 4 x 4 Ising lattice it reproduces the exact enumeration of 65 536 states within 0.4 to 3.7 percent in g (and within 0.005 in the energy per site and 1 percent in the specific heat at all temperatures from a single run of 4e6 attempts).
  • Kinetic Monte Carlo uses the residence-time algorithm: the time advances by -ln(u)/R_tot, the waiting times are exponential (mean 9.5802 ms against 9.5793 expected, 4.98 percent longer than three means against 4.98 percent), and an ensemble of walkers with a 0.7 eV barrier at 300 K gives D = (1.427 +- 0.027) x 10^-18 m^2/s, in agreement with the exact 1.423 x 10^-18, simulating 19 s of vacancy motion per walker in 0.5 s of CPU for 4 million jumps, a time that molecular dynamics (5e12 steps per jump) cannot reach.
  • Choosing the method: MD gives dynamics but is limited to nanoseconds; Metropolis Monte Carlo samples equilibrium faster for lattice and off-lattice models, allows unphysical moves and ensembles (grand canonical, swaps), but has no real time; kinetic Monte Carlo gives the real time evolution of rare events if the event catalogue and rates are known.

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Core

State the two conditions for a Markov chain to sample the Boltzmann distribution and give an example of how each can fail in a simulation.

Solution

Detailed balance (or at least stationarity of $P$): fails when the proposal is asymmetric without the Hastings correction, when the step size is adapted during production, or when a rejected move is not counted again. Ergodicity: fails when the moves cannot connect all states (a system with a hard constraint and only single-particle moves), or in practice when the barrier between regions is so high that the chain does not cross within the run (a glass, two phases, a deep metastable state): the averages then depend on the starting configuration.

Quiz 2Core

Why is the mean-field theory of the regular solution not accurate, and what does the Monte Carlo calculation of the square-lattice alloy show quantitatively?

Solution

It replaces the local environment of an atom by its average composition and neglects the short-range order and the fluctuations that increase the entropy of the mixed state. For the square lattice it predicts $T_c=4$ against the exact $2.269$ ($+76\%$) and solubilities that are too small by a factor $2.5$ at $T/T_c=0.925$ ($x_B=0.026$ against $0.065$). The simulation reproduces Onsager’s exact result within $1$–$2$ standard errors, so it can be used for lattices where no exact solution exists (cluster expansions of real alloys).

Quiz 3Core

What is the purpose of the tail correction in the Lennard-Jones simulation, and how large was it?

Solution

Interactions beyond the cutoff $r_c$ are neglected in the simulation but contribute to the energy and the pressure of a liquid (the pair distribution is $g\approx1$ beyond $r_c$): $U_\mathrm{tail}/N=\tfrac83\pi\rho[\tfrac13r_c^{-9}-r_c^{-3}]=-0.452$ (about $8\%$ of the total energy of $-5.95$) and $P_\mathrm{tail}=-0.762$, comparable to the pressure itself ($0.91$). Without them the MC and MD values would not agree with the literature, but they would still agree with each other if both omit the correction: the correction is therefore not needed to compare two codes with the same truncation, but is needed to compare with data computed with a longer cutoff.

Quiz 4Core

In the residence-time algorithm, why is no random number rejected, and why do we draw a second random number for the time?

Solution

The algorithm samples directly the joint distribution of the waiting time and the event: the event is chosen with probability $r_k/R$ and the waiting time with the exponential distribution of mean $1/R$, so each step is one physical event and nothing is rejected (compare Metropolis in which most trial moves are rejected). The time increment must be drawn independently of the choice of the event, and the distribution of $\Delta t$ must be exponential for the process to be a Poisson process; using the mean time $1/R$ instead gives the correct average dynamics for large systems but the wrong fluctuations.

Quiz 5Core

The autocorrelation time of a Monte Carlo run is $100$ sweeps. How many independent samples are in $10^6$ sweeps, and by how much does the naive error bar underestimate the true one?

Solution

$N_\mathrm{eff}=N/(2\tau)=10^6/200=5000$ independent samples. The naive error assumes $10^6$ independent samples and is too small by $\sqrt{2\tau}=\sqrt{200}=14$ times. A block analysis with blocks of several hundred sweeps reveals the factor.

Quiz 6Challenge

You have computed the miscibility gap of an alloy with a lattice Monte Carlo and DFT-derived interactions, and the result differs by $15\%$ in the critical temperature from experiment. List sources of error, from the Monte Carlo itself to the Hamiltonian.

Solution

Monte Carlo: finite-size effects (the critical temperature of a finite lattice is shifted; use finite-size scaling), insufficient equilibration or sampling (autocorrelation), a wrong random-number generator. Hamiltonian: truncation of the cluster expansion (fit error of the DFT energies: a cross-validation score of $2$ meV/atom leads to a $\sim10$–$20\%$ error of $T_c$), the DFT functional (formation energies of PBE are biased), the neglect of lattice vibrations (vibrational entropy changes $T_c$ by $10$–$20\%$ in many alloys), of the lattice relaxation (included in the effective interactions only if the expansion was fitted to relaxed energies), and of the electronic and magnetic entropy. The mean-field result would be even farther: the difference between the methods is $20$–$70\%$ of $T_c$.
