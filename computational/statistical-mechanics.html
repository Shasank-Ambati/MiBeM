Statistical mechanics for simulation

A piece of copper contains $10^{23}$ atoms, each moving according to Newton’s laws, yet we describe it with just a few numbers: temperature, pressure, heat capacity, thermal expansion. Statistical mechanics is the bridge between the two descriptions. It is also the theory behind every simulation method in this curriculum: molecular dynamics samples the Boltzmann distribution by following trajectories, Monte Carlo samples it by random moves, free-energy methods compute the partition function itself, and the error bars of all of them come from the statistics of correlated samples. This course builds the theory from the counting of states to the Ising model, with each result checked by a number.

Lecture 1. Microstates, entropy and temperature#

A macrostate is described by a few thermodynamic variables (energy $U$, volume $V$, number of particles $N$). A microstate is a complete specification of the system at the atomic level (in quantum mechanics, one quantum state of the whole system). Many microstates correspond to the same macrostate; their number is the multiplicity $\Omega(U,V,N)$. The one postulate of statistical mechanics is:

Fundamental postulate

An isolated system in equilibrium is equally likely to be found in any of its accessible microstates. The probability of a macrostate is therefore proportional to its multiplicity, and the equilibrium macrostate is the one with the largest $\Omega$.

The entropy is defined as $S=k_\mathrm B\ln\Omega$ (PHYS 102), where $k_\mathrm B=1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K. The logarithm makes entropy additive: two independent systems have $\Omega=\Omega_1\Omega_2$ and $S=S_1+S_2$. Temperature is then defined by how the entropy responds to energy: $$\frac1T=\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\Bigr)_{V,N}.$$ Why this is temperature: put two systems A and B in thermal contact; they exchange energy, the total $U_A+U_B$ is fixed, and the multiplicity of the combined system is $\Omega_A(U_A)\Omega_B(U-U_A)$. The most probable $U_A$ maximises $\ln\Omega_A+\ln\Omega_B$, so $\partial S_A/\partial U_A=\partial S_B/\partial U_B$: at equilibrium $1/T$ is the same in both. This is the zeroth law, now derived.

A system you can count: the Einstein solid

Take $N$ identical quantum oscillators (a crude model of the atoms of a solid, PHYS 302), each with energies $0$, $\hbar\omega$, $2\hbar\omega$, $\ldots$, sharing $q$ quanta of energy. The number of ways to distribute $q$ indistinguishable quanta among $N$ oscillators is the “stars and bars” count $$\Omega(N,q)=\binom{q+N-1}{q}=\frac{(q+N-1)!}{q!\,(N-1)!}.$$

Worked example 1.1: two small solids share energy

Solid A and solid B each have $N=3$ oscillators, and $q=6$ quanta are shared between them. List the multiplicity for each possible number $q_A$ of quanta in A, and find the probability that the energy is shared equally.

Use $\Omega(3,q)=\binom{q+2}{q}$: $\Omega=1$, $3$, $6$, $10$, $15$, $21$, $28$ for $q=0, \ldots, 6$. The multiplicity of the combined system with $q_A$ quanta in A is $\Omega(3,q_A)\Omega(3,6-q_A)$: $28$, $63$, $90$, $100$, $90$, $63$, $28$ for $q_A=0, \ldots, 6$. The total is $462=\binom{11}{6}=\Omega(6,6)$, as it must be (the sum over $q_A$ counts every microstate of the combined $6$-oscillator solid). The equal division $q_A=3$ has $100$ microstates, probability $100/462=21.6\%$, the most likely single outcome; but a division with all the energy in A has only $28/462=6\%$. For three oscillators the preference for equal sharing is mild.

With more oscillators the preference becomes overwhelming. The script of the lab computes it exactly; for two solids with $N=300$ oscillators each and $q=100$ quanta, the most probable division is $q_A=50$ (as the symmetry requires) and the probability distribution has a mean of $50.0$ and a standard deviation of $5.4$ quanta: the relative width is only $11\%$, and it shrinks as $1/\sqrt N$ when the system grows. For $N\sim10^{23}$ the relative fluctuations are $\sim10^{-12}$ and the most probable state is, for all purposes, the only state. This is why thermodynamics works.

Large numbers and Stirling’s approximation

For large $n$, $\ln n!\approx n\ln n-n$ (Stirling; the next correction is $\tfrac12\ln2\pi n$). Check: $\ln 100!=363.74$, and $100\ln100-100=360.52$ differs by $0.9\%$. With this, the entropy of the Einstein solid for $q,N\gg1$ is $S/k_\mathrm B=(q+N)\ln(q+N)-q\ln q-N\ln N$. Differentiating, $1/T=\partial S/\partial U=(k_\mathrm B/\hbar\omega)\ln[(q+N)/q]$, so with $U=q\hbar\omega$ the energy per oscillator is $\hbar\omega/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_\mathrm BT}-1)$ (the zero-point energy omitted): the Einstein result of Lecture 3 is already contained in the counting.

The two-state system and negative temperature

The simplest model is $N$ independent spins (or any two-level systems), each with energy $0$ (ground) or $\varepsilon$ (excited). With $n$ excited spins the energy is $U=n\varepsilon$ and $\Omega=\binom Nn$. By Stirling, $$\frac S{k_\mathrm B}=N\ln N-n\ln n-(N-n)\ln(N-n),\qquad\frac1T=\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}=\frac{k_\mathrm B}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{N-n}{n},$$ so $n/N=1/(e^{\varepsilon/k_\mathrm BT}+1)$: at low $T$ almost all spins are in the ground state, at $T\to\infty$ the populations equalise ($n/N\to\tfrac12$, the maximum entropy $S=Nk_\mathrm B\ln2$). If more than half of the spins are excited ($n>N/2$) the entropy decreases with energy, $\partial S/\partial U<0$, and the definition gives a negative temperature: not “colder than zero” but hotter than infinity, since such a system gives energy to any normal system it touches. Negative temperatures are realised in nuclear spin systems and cold atoms; a system with unbounded kinetic energy (any atom) cannot have them.

Worked example 1.2: how rare is a fluctuation?

A box of gas contains $N$ molecules, each equally likely to be in the left or right half. What is the probability that all are found in the left half, for $N=10$, $100$ and one mole?

Each molecule is in the left half with probability $\tfrac12$, so the probability of the particular configuration with all in the left half is $2^{-N}$. $N=10$: $2^{-10}=9.8\times10^{-4}$, about one in a thousand: easily seen. $N=100$: $2^{-100}=7.9\times10^{-31}$. $N=N_\mathrm A=6.02\times10^{23}$: $2^{-N}=10^{-0.301N}=10^{-1.8\times10^{23}}$. Even if the gas redistributed itself a billion times per second since the beginning of the universe ($4\times10^{17}$ s), the chance that it was ever observed once would be $\sim10^{-1.8\times10^{23}}\times10^{26}$. Entropy increase is not a law that forbids the rare fluctuation, it is the overwhelming statistical dominance of the typical states.

Lecture 2. The Boltzmann distribution and the partition function#

An isolated system is a rare thing; real systems exchange energy with their surroundings. Consider a small system S (one atom, one molecule, or a macroscopic sample) in thermal contact with a very large heat bath R at temperature $T$, the combination being isolated with total energy $E_\mathrm{tot}$. If S is in a particular microstate $i$ of energy $E_i$, the bath has energy $E_\mathrm{tot}-E_i$ and $\Omega_R(E_\mathrm{tot}-E_i)$ microstates. By the fundamental postulate, $$P_i\propto\Omega_R(E_\mathrm{tot}-E_i)=\exp\!\Bigl[\frac{S_R(E_\mathrm{tot}-E_i)}{k_\mathrm B}\Bigr]\approx\exp\!\Bigl[\frac{S_R(E_\mathrm{tot})}{k_\mathrm B}-\frac{E_i}{k_\mathrm BT}\Bigr],$$ because $E_i\ll E_\mathrm{tot}$ allows the expansion $S_R(E_\mathrm{tot}-E_i)\approx S_R(E_\mathrm{tot})-E_i\,\partial S_R/\partial E=S_R(E_\mathrm{tot})-E_i/T$. The first term is a constant, so $$\boxed{P_i=\frac{e^{-E_i/k_\mathrm BT}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_ie^{-E_i/k_\mathrm BT}}$$ (the canonical ensemble, with $\beta=1/k_\mathrm BT$). The sum runs over microstates: if a level of energy $E$ has degeneracy $g$, it appears $g$ times. $Z$, the partition function (German Zustandssumme, sum over states), is not just a normalisation constant: it contains the whole thermodynamics.

Everything from $Z$

Mean energy: $\langle E\rangle=\sum_iE_iP_i=-\dfrac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}$. Helmholtz free energy: $F=-k_\mathrm BT\ln Z$. Entropy: $S=-\partial F/\partial T=k_\mathrm B\ln Z+\langle E\rangle/T=-k_\mathrm B\sum_iP_i\ln P_i$ (the Gibbs entropy). Heat capacity: $C_V=\partial\langle E\rangle/\partial T$. Pressure: $p=-\partial F/\partial V=k_\mathrm BT\,\partial\ln Z/\partial V$.

Proof of the first. $-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta=-\frac1Z\sum_i(-E_i)e^{-\beta E_i}=\sum_iE_iP_i$. The Gibbs entropy: with $\ln P_i=-\beta E_i-\ln Z$ one finds $-k_\mathrm B\sum P_i\ln P_i=k_\mathrm B\beta\langle E\rangle+k_\mathrm B\ln Z=\langle E\rangle/T-F/T$, which is $S$ because $F=\langle E\rangle-TS$.

When the energy levels are continuous (a classical gas), the sum is an integral over phase space, $Z=\frac1{h^{3N}N!}\int e^{-H(\mathbf p,\mathbf q)/k_\mathrm BT}\,\mathrm d^{3N}p\,\mathrm d^{3N}q$, where $h$ is Planck’s constant (the volume of a quantum state in phase space) and $N!$ accounts for indistinguishable particles. A product of independent systems has a product of partition functions, $Z=Z_1Z_2$, so that $\ln Z$ and all extensive quantities add.

A simulation samples this distribution

Molecular dynamics with a thermostat and Metropolis Monte Carlo are algorithms whose long-time averages converge to $\sum_iA_iP_i$ with exactly this $P_i$. The Metropolis acceptance rule $\min(1,e^{-\Delta E/k_\mathrm BT})$ is chosen so that the ratio of the probabilities of two states is $e^{-\Delta E/k_\mathrm BT}$ (detailed balance, CMS 503). In LAB 504 the energy per spin of a $32\times32$ Ising lattice at $T=1.8$ is $-1.8603$ from the simulation and $-1.8593$ from the exact partition function of Onsager.

The two-level system, and the Schottky heat capacity

For $N$ independent two-level systems with energies $0$ and $\varepsilon$, the one-particle partition function is $z=1+e^{-\beta\varepsilon}$ and $Z=z^N$. Then $$\langle E\rangle=-N\frac{\partial\ln z}{\partial\beta}=\frac{N\varepsilon}{e^{\beta\varepsilon}+1},\qquad C_V=Nk_\mathrm B\Bigl(\frac\varepsilon{k_\mathrm BT}\Bigr)^2\frac{e^{\varepsilon/k_\mathrm BT}}{(e^{\varepsilon/k_\mathrm BT}+1)^2}.$$ The heat capacity goes to zero at both low temperature (the upper level is frozen out) and high temperature (the populations are saturated at $\tfrac12$) and has a maximum, the Schottky anomaly, of $0.4392\,Nk_\mathrm B$ at $k_\mathrm BT=0.4167\,\varepsilon$ (found by setting the derivative to zero numerically). A bump of this kind in the heat capacity of a solid at low temperature is the signature of a two-level degree of freedom, such as a magnetic ion in a crystal field or a tunnelling defect: a spectroscopy done with a calorimeter. The result also shows that heat capacity measures the number of levels that are thermally accessible, which is the unifying idea of Lecture 3.

Worked example 2.1: a magnetic defect

An impurity in a crystal has a ground state and an excited level $\varepsilon=0.10$ meV higher. What fraction is in the excited state at $T=1$ K, at $T=0.1$ K, and at $T=100$ K? At what temperature is the heat capacity largest?

$\varepsilon/k_\mathrm B=0.10\times10^{-3}\times11\,604.5/1\ \mathrm{K}=1.1605$ K (using $1\ \mathrm{eV}/k_\mathrm B=11\,604.5$ K). At $T=1$ K: $n/N=1/(e^{1.1605}+1)=1/(3.192+1)=0.2385$. At $0.1$ K: $e^{11.605}=1.1\times10^5$, so $n/N=9\times10^{-6}$. At $100$ K: $e^{0.0116}=1.0117$, so $n/N=0.4971$, almost exactly one half. The heat capacity peaks at $k_\mathrm BT=0.4167\varepsilon$, i.e. at $T=0.4167\times1.1605=0.484$ K. Heat capacity measurements below a kelvin probe levels of this size.

Lecture 3. Partition functions of simple systems#

With the canonical recipe, the thermodynamics of a system follows from its energy levels. Three examples provide most of the intuition of solid-state and gas physics.

The harmonic oscillator and the Einstein solid

A quantum oscillator has $E_n=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar\omega$, so $z=\sum_n e^{-\beta\hbar\omega(n+1/2)}=\dfrac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}=\dfrac1{2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}$ (a geometric series). The mean energy and heat capacity are $$\langle E\rangle=\hbar\omega\Bigl[\frac12+\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega/k_\mathrm BT}-1}\Bigr],\qquad C=k_\mathrm B\Bigl(\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_\mathrm BT}\Bigr)^2\frac{e^{\hbar\omega/k_\mathrm BT}}{(e^{\hbar\omega/k_\mathrm BT}-1)^2}.$$ At high temperature $\langle E\rangle\to k_\mathrm BT$ (equipartition: two quadratic terms) and $C\to k_\mathrm B$; at low temperature $C\propto e^{-\hbar\omega/k_\mathrm BT}$ goes to zero exponentially, because the oscillator cannot absorb less than one quantum. A solid of $N$ atoms is $3N$ oscillators of this kind. With the Einstein temperature $\theta_E=\hbar\omega/k_\mathrm B$, the molar heat capacity is $C=3R\,f_E(\theta_E/T)$, and Dulong and Petit’s $3R$ is the high-temperature limit. Einstein (1907) explained why diamond, with its high frequency, has a heat capacity far below $3R$ at room temperature; the exponential low-$T$ decay is however too fast compared with experiment, which follows $T^3$ (Debye, Lecture 5), because real solids have vibrations at all frequencies down to zero.

Zero-point energy matters for light atoms

At $T=0$ the oscillator has $E_0=\tfrac12\hbar\omega$. For the lattice vibrations of copper ($\nu_\mathrm{max}\approx8$ THz, LAB 301) the zero-point energy per atom is $\tfrac98k_\mathrm B\theta_D\approx0.033$ eV, small against the cohesive energy of $3.5$ eV but not negligible in the lattice constant of light elements (helium does not solidify at atmospheric pressure at any temperature because its zero-point energy is larger than the energy barrier to melting).

The ideal gas and the Sackur–Tetrode equation

For one particle of mass $m$ in a box of volume $V$, the classical partition function is $z=\dfrac{V}{h^3}\int e^{-p^2/2mk_\mathrm BT}\mathrm d^3p=\dfrac V{\lambda^3}$, with the thermal de Broglie wavelength $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_\mathrm BT}$. For $N$ identical, indistinguishable, non-interacting particles, $Z=z^N/N!$ (the factor $1/N!$ corrects for counting permutations of identical particles as different states; without it the entropy would not be extensive, which is the Gibbs paradox). With Stirling’s formula, $$F=-k_\mathrm BT\ln Z=-Nk_\mathrm BT\Bigl[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3}+1\Bigr].$$ Differentiating, $p=-\partial F/\partial V=Nk_\mathrm BT/V$ (the ideal gas law, derived), $U=\tfrac32Nk_\mathrm BT$ (equipartition, derived) and the Sackur–Tetrode entropy $$S=Nk_\mathrm B\Bigl[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3}+\frac52\Bigr].$$ It is a purely quantum result: $h$ appears in it. The classical gas is a valid description if $V/N\gg\lambda^3$, i.e. the thermal wavelength is much smaller than the spacing between molecules.

Worked example 3.1: the entropy of argon

Compute the molar entropy of argon gas ($m=39.948$ u) at $298.15$ K and $1$ bar (the standard state) and compare with the value $154.84$ J/(mol K) obtained from calorimetry (heat capacity measurements integrated from near $0$ K).

$\lambda=h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_\mathrm BT}$ with $h=6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s, $m=6.634\times10^{-26}$ kg, $k_\mathrm B=1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K and $T=298.15$ K gives $\lambda=1.600\times10^{-11}$ m (about $0.016$ nm). The volume per molecule is $V/N=k_\mathrm BT/p=4.116\times10^{-26}$ m$^3$, so $V/N\lambda^3=4.116\times10^{-26}/4.094\times10^{-33}=1.006\times10^{7}$ and $S/Nk_\mathrm B=\ln(1.006\times10^7)+2.5=16.12+2.5=18.62$; times $R$: $S=154.85$ J/(mol K). This agrees with the calorimetric $154.84$ to $0.01\%$: a calculation with only Planck’s constant and the mass, no fitted parameter, reproduces a measurement of the heat absorbed by the real gas, which was one of the early triumphs of statistical mechanics. At $100$ K the same formula (at $1$ atm) gives $132.0$ and at $1000$ K $179.9$ J/(mol K), an increase of $47.9$ J/(mol K) $=\tfrac52R\ln10$: at constant pressure the entropy of a monatomic ideal gas grows as $\tfrac52R\ln T$.

Equipartition and the classical limit

Any energy term that is quadratic in a coordinate or momentum, $E=\tfrac12ax^2$, gives a classical partition function $\int e^{-\beta ax^2/2}\mathrm dx=\sqrt{2\pi k_\mathrm BT/a}\propto T^{1/2}$ and so $\langle E\rangle=-\partial\ln z/\partial\beta=\tfrac12k_\mathrm BT$. This is the equipartition theorem. It explains the Dulong–Petit law ($3N$ oscillators each with two quadratic terms, $\langle E\rangle=3Nk_\mathrm BT$, $C=3Nk_\mathrm B$), the heat capacity of gases ($\tfrac32k_\mathrm B$ per atom, $\tfrac52k_\mathrm B$ for a diatomic molecule at moderate temperatures: translation plus two rotations; the vibration is frozen out), and the kinetic temperature used in molecular dynamics: $\langle\tfrac12mv_x^2\rangle=\tfrac12k_\mathrm BT$ is how a simulation thermometer works. It fails when the spacing of the energy levels exceeds $k_\mathrm BT$ (frozen degrees of freedom, as in the exponential decay of the oscillator heat capacity), which is why classical molecular dynamics overestimates the heat capacity of solids below the Debye temperature (LAB 301: $C_v/3R=0.937$ at $300$ K for copper, $0.215$ at $50$ K from the quantum formula).

Lecture 4. Free energy, fluctuations and defects#

The canonical ensemble describes a system with fixed $N$, $V$ and temperature. The thermodynamic potential that goes with it is the Helmholtz free energy $F=-k_\mathrm BT\ln Z=U-TS$ (PHYS 102): a system at fixed $T$ and $V$ evolves to the minimum of $F$, which balances the tendency to low energy against the tendency to high entropy. Two other ensembles complete the set. The isothermal–isobaric ensemble ($N,p,T$ fixed) has Gibbs free energy $G=F+pV$ as potential and is the ensemble of the NPT molecular dynamics runs of LAB 503; the grand canonical ensemble ($\mu,V,T$ fixed, particles exchanged with a reservoir) has probability $P_{i,N}=e^{-\beta(E_i-\mu N)}/\Xi$ with the grand partition function $\Xi=\sum_{N}\sum_ie^{-\beta(E_i-\mu N)}$. The chemical potential $\mu=(\partial F/\partial N)_{T,V}$ is the free energy needed to add one particle; for the ideal gas, $\mu=k_\mathrm BT\ln(N\lambda^3/V)$, so $\mu$ rises with density and particles flow from high to low $\mu$.

Fluctuations

In the canonical ensemble the energy is not fixed but fluctuates around its mean. Differentiating $\langle E\rangle=-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$ once more, $$\boxed{\langle(\delta E)^2\rangle=\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2=\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2}=k_\mathrm BT^2C_V}$$ (the notation $\delta E=E-\langle E\rangle$). The heat capacity, a response of the average to a change of temperature, equals a measure of the spontaneous fluctuations: this is a case of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem. Since $C_V$ and $\langle E\rangle$ are both extensive, the relative fluctuation $\sigma_E/\langle E\rangle=\sqrt{k_\mathrm BT^2C_V}/U\propto1/\sqrt N$. For a monatomic ideal gas ($U=\tfrac32Nk_\mathrm BT$, $C_V=\tfrac32Nk_\mathrm B$) it is $\sqrt{2/3N}$: $1.05\times10^{-12}$ for a mole, but $2.8\%$ for a simulation box of $N=864$ atoms and $7.9\%$ for $N=108$. In an NpT ensemble the volume fluctuates with $\langle(\delta V)^2\rangle=k_\mathrm BT\,V\kappa_T$ ($\kappa_T$ is the isothermal compressibility), and in the grand canonical ensemble $\langle(\delta N)^2\rangle=k_\mathrm BT(\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu)$.

Using fluctuations in simulations

These relations are practical tools. In a canonical Monte Carlo or thermostatted MD run, $C_V=\langle(\delta E)^2\rangle/k_\mathrm BT^2$ is obtained from the variance of the recorded energies, with no extra simulation; in LAB 504 the specific heat and the susceptibility of the Ising model are computed exactly in this way, and show a peak growing with the lattice size near the critical temperature, where the fluctuations become large (a divergence of fluctuations is how a continuous phase transition shows up). The caveat: the ensemble matters. The fluctuations of the energy in a microcanonical (NVE) run are not the canonical ones, and a thermostat that is too strong or too weak distorts them: only a few thermostats (Nosé–Hoover chains, stochastic velocity rescaling, Langevin with small damping) generate the correct canonical fluctuations.

Worked example 4.1: how large are the fluctuations in a simulation box?

A molecular-dynamics cell of $N=864$ argon-like atoms is held at $T=85$ K in the canonical ensemble. Treating the system as a monatomic ideal gas, estimate the rms fluctuation of the kinetic energy and the corresponding fluctuation of the instantaneous kinetic temperature.

The kinetic energy is $K=\tfrac32Nk_\mathrm BT_\mathrm{inst}$; its mean is $\tfrac32Nk_\mathrm BT=\tfrac32(864)(1.381\times10^{-23})(85)=1.52\times10^{-18}$ J ($9.5$ eV). The canonical variance of the kinetic energy of $3N$ quadratic degrees of freedom is $\langle(\delta K)^2\rangle=\tfrac32N(k_\mathrm BT)^2$, so $\sigma_K/\langle K\rangle=\sqrt{2/3N}=2.8\%$, i.e. $\sigma_K=0.27$ eV. The instantaneous temperature, defined by $K=\tfrac32Nk_\mathrm BT_\mathrm{inst}$, then fluctuates by $2.8\%$ of $85$ K, $\sigma_T=2.4$ K: a thermostat that holds $T$ to $\pm0.1$ K would be wrong. A simulation reports averages of $T$ with a scatter of this size, which is the reason for the “temperature fluctuates around its target” check in LAB 502.

Equilibrium defects

The most important application of free-energy minimisation for a materials scientist is the equilibrium concentration of point defects. Take a crystal of $N$ lattice sites and create $n$ vacancies, each costing a formation energy $E_f$ (and, in general, a formation entropy $S_f$ from the changed vibrations around it). The $n$ vacancies can be placed on the $N$ sites in $\binom Nn$ ways, which gives the configurational entropy $S_\mathrm{conf}=k_\mathrm B\ln\binom Nn\approx k_\mathrm B[N\ln N-n\ln n-(N-n)\ln(N-n)]$. The free energy change is $\Delta F=n(E_f-TS_f)-TS_\mathrm{conf}$. Setting $\partial\Delta F/\partial n=0$ gives, for $n\ll N$, $$\boxed{\frac nN=\exp\Bigl(\frac{S_f}{k_\mathrm B}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{E_f}{k_\mathrm BT}\Bigr)}.$$ The factor $e^{S_f/k_\mathrm B}$ is usually between $1$ and $e^3\approx20$. The exponential dependence on $E_f/k_\mathrm BT$ is the dominant effect; the concentration is never zero at $T>0$, because the entropy of mixing a few vacancies into the crystal is infinitely steep at $n=0$ ($\partial S_\mathrm{conf}/\partial n\to\infty$).

Worked example 4.2: vacancies in copper

The vacancy formation energy of copper is $E_f=1.28$ eV (LAB 503). Compute the equilibrium vacancy fraction at $300$, $600$, $900$ and $1300$ K without formation entropy, and at $1300$ K with $S_f=2k_\mathrm B$. How many vacancies are there in $1$ cm$^3$ of copper at $1300$ K? The melting point is $1358$ K.

$k_\mathrm BT=0.02585$, $0.05170$, $0.07755$ and $0.1120$ eV, so $E_f/k_\mathrm BT=49.5$, $24.8$, $16.5$ and $11.4$, giving $n/N=3.1\times10^{-22}$, $1.8\times10^{-11}$, $6.8\times10^{-8}$ and $1.1\times10^{-5}$. With $S_f=2k_\mathrm B$ at $1300$ K the result is multiplied by $e^2=7.4$: $8.1\times10^{-5}$. Copper has $N/V=8.49\times10^{22}$ atoms/cm$^3$, so at $1300$ K there are $9\times10^{17}$ (without $S_f$) to $7\times10^{18}$ vacancies per cm$^3$. At room temperature, one vacancy per $3\times10^{21}$ sites would mean about $0.03$ vacancies per cm$^3$: practically none. Measured vacancy fractions near the melting point are of order $10^{-4}$, consistent with this calculation including a formation entropy. (A quenched sample can retain the high-temperature vacancies, which then dominate the diffusion at room temperature.)

The same argument for any reaction

Minimising a free energy that contains an energy cost per defect and an entropy of mixing is the pattern behind the law of mass action, the equilibrium concentration of dopants and interstitials, the solubility limit of a second element (LAB 505: the solubility of B in A at the eutectic temperature, $0.046$), and the Arrhenius form of diffusion in CMS 504, where the diffusion coefficient is the product of the equilibrium vacancy fraction and the rate at which a vacancy jumps.

Lecture 5. Quantum statistics: electrons, phonons and photons#

The classical Boltzmann treatment counts the particles as distinguishable and ignores that identical particles of different kinds obey different rules. Quantum mechanics distinguishes fermions (electrons, protons, neutrons: half-integer spin; at most one particle per single-particle state, the Pauli principle) and bosons (photons, phonons, helium-4: integer spin; any number per state). In the grand canonical ensemble the average occupation of a single-particle state of energy $\varepsilon$ is $$\bar n_\mathrm{FD}=\frac1{e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_\mathrm BT}+1},\qquad\bar n_\mathrm{BE}=\frac1{e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_\mathrm BT}-1}$$ for the Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions, and both reduce to the Boltzmann factor $e^{-(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_\mathrm BT}$ when $\bar n\ll1$. Derivation for one state: the state has occupation $n$ and energy $n\varepsilon$; for fermions $n=0,1$ so $\Xi=1+e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$ and $\bar n=e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}/\Xi$, which is the FD form; for bosons $n=0,1,2,\ldots$ and the geometric series $\Xi=1/(1-e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)})$ gives $\bar n=1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}-1)$. For photons and phonons the number of quanta is not conserved, so $\mu=0$ and $\bar n=1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_\mathrm BT}-1)$, the Planck distribution (the same average as in the quantum oscillator of Lecture 3).

Phonons: the Debye model

The lattice vibrations of a solid with $N$ atoms are $3N$ normal modes (LAB 301), each a quantum oscillator. Einstein gave all the modes the same frequency; Debye (1912) took the density of modes of a continuous elastic medium, $g(\omega)=\dfrac{3V\omega^2}{2\pi^2c^3}$ ($c$ is an average sound velocity), up to a cutoff $\omega_D$ chosen so that the total number of modes is $3N$: $\omega_D=c(6\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}$. The Debye temperature is $\theta_D=\hbar\omega_D/k_\mathrm B$. The energy and heat capacity follow by summing the oscillator results over $g(\omega)$: $$C_V=9Nk_\mathrm B\Bigl(\frac T{\theta_D}\Bigr)^3\int_0^{\theta_D/T}\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,\mathrm dx\ \longrightarrow\ \begin{cases}3Nk_\mathrm B&T\gg\theta_D\\[2pt]\dfrac{12\pi^4}5Nk_\mathrm B\Bigl(\dfrac T{\theta_D}\Bigr)^3&T\ll\theta_D.\end{cases}$$ The $T^3$ law at low temperature is an exact consequence of the linear dispersion $\omega=ck$ of long-wavelength sound waves, which is why it is observed in all insulators. For copper ($\theta_D=343$ K, LAB 301 found $332$–$345$ K) the coefficient is $12\pi^4R/(5\theta_D^3)=4.82\times10^{-5}$ J/(mol K$^4$): $C=0.048$ J/(mol K) at $10$ K and $0.385$ at $20$ K. The full Debye formula gives $4.99$ J/(mol K) at $50$ K, $14.8$ at $100$ K, $21.6$ at $200$ K and $23.4$ at $300$ K, to be compared with the value $23.4$ obtained from the real phonon spectrum in the lab: a model with one parameter reproduces a heat capacity to $1\%$ at room temperature.

Photons

For electromagnetic radiation in a cavity, $g(\omega)=V\omega^2/\pi^2c^3$ (two polarisations) and $\mu=0$ give the Planck spectrum and a total energy density $u=aT^4$ with $a=\pi^2k_\mathrm B^4/15\hbar^3c^3=7.57\times10^{-16}$ J m$^{-3}$ K$^{-4}$. The radiated power is $\sigma T^4$ with the Stefan–Boltzmann constant $\sigma=ac/4=5.67\times10^{-8}$ W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-4}$, which is how a furnace loses heat at high temperature (PHYS 102).

Electrons: the Fermi gas

The conduction electrons of a metal form a gas of fermions at a density so high that the Pauli principle dominates. At $T=0$ the single-particle states are filled up to the Fermi energy $E_F$, found by counting states in a box ($2\times\dfrac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\dfrac{4\pi}3k_F^3=N$): $$E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3},\qquad T_F=\frac{E_F}{k_\mathrm B}.$$ For copper with one conduction electron per atom, $n=8.47\times10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$ gives $E_F=7.03$ eV and $T_F=81\,600$ K. Room temperature ($k_\mathrm BT=0.026$ eV) is therefore only $0.37\%$ of $E_F$: the electron gas is degenerate. The Fermi–Dirac distribution is a step function smeared over a layer of width $\sim k_\mathrm BT$ at $E_F$; only the electrons in that layer, a fraction $\sim T/T_F$, can absorb heat. The result (Sommerfeld expansion) is a heat capacity linear in $T$: $$C_\mathrm{el}=\frac{\pi^2}2Nk_\mathrm B\frac T{T_F}=\gamma T,\qquad\gamma_\mathrm{free}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{R}{T_F}=0.50\ \mathrm{mJ/(mol\,K^2)}\ \text{for Cu}.$$ The measured $\gamma$ of copper is $0.695$ mJ/(mol K$^2$), $38\%$ larger, because the electrons interact with the lattice and with each other (a “thermal effective mass” $m^*=1.38\,m_e$). The total low-temperature heat capacity of a metal is $C=\gamma T+\beta T^3$ with $\beta=4.82\times10^{-5}$ J/(mol K$^4$) for copper. The two terms are equal at $T=\sqrt{\gamma/\beta}=3.8$ K: above this temperature the lattice dominates, which is why the electronic contribution is unimportant at room temperature ($\gamma T=0.21$ J/(mol K), under $1\%$ of $3R$), and why a plot of $C/T$ against $T^2$ gives a straight line with intercept $\gamma$ and slope $\beta$, the standard way of separating them.

Lecture 6. Interacting systems: the Ising model, mean-field theory and the regular solution#

Non-interacting systems have a partition function that factorises, and exact solutions. A phase transition requires interaction between the particles. The simplest model of a system with interactions and a transition is the Ising model: spins $s_i=\pm1$ on a lattice with energy $H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j-h\sum_is_i$, with $J>0$ favouring parallel neighbours and $h$ a magnetic field. It describes a ferromagnet, but the same mathematics describes a binary alloy (spin up = atom A, spin down = atom B; lattice gas for occupied/empty sites), which is why it is the model system for order–disorder transitions in materials (CMS 505). In one dimension it can be solved exactly and has no transition at $T>0$; in two dimensions Onsager solved it (1944) and found a transition at $k_\mathrm BT_c/J=2/\ln(1+\sqrt2)=2.269$; in three dimensions no exact solution is known and $T_c\approx4.51J/k_\mathrm B$ comes from simulations (LAB 504).

Mean-field theory

The difficulty is the correlation between neighbouring spins. Mean-field theory replaces the interaction of a spin with its $z$ neighbours (the coordination number) by an interaction with their average orientation $m=\langle s\rangle$: each spin sees an effective field $h_\mathrm{eff}=h+zJm$, so the one-spin partition function is $2\cosh(\beta h_\mathrm{eff})$ and $m=\tanh(\beta(h+zJm))$. For $h=0$ this is a self-consistency equation, $$\boxed{m=\tanh\!\Bigl(\frac{zJ}{k_\mathrm BT}\,m\Bigr)}.$$ Graphically, the line $y=m$ and the curve $y=\tanh(m/\tau)$ with $\tau=k_\mathrm BT/zJ$ always intersect at $m=0$; if the slope of the tanh at the origin, $1/\tau$, exceeds $1$ there are two further solutions $\pm m_0$. The slope reaches one at the mean-field critical temperature $$k_\mathrm BT_c^\mathrm{MF}=zJ.$$ Below $T_c$ the symmetric solution $m=0$ becomes unstable and the system chooses $m=+m_0$ or $-m_0$ (spontaneous symmetry breaking). Near $T_c$, expanding the tanh, $m\approx\sqrt{3(1-T/T_c)}$: the order parameter vanishes with an exponent $\beta=\tfrac12$.

Landau theory in one equation

Mean-field theory can be recast as an expansion of the free energy in the order parameter, $F(m)=F_0+a(T-T_c)m^2+bm^4$ with $a,b>0$ (the symmetry $m\to-m$ forbids odd powers). For $T>T_c$ the minimum is at $m=0$; for $T<T_c$ it is at $m_0^2=a(T_c-T)/2b$: $m_0\propto(T_c-T)^{1/2}$ again. This is the starting point of the phase-field models of CMS 505, where $F(m)$ is combined with a gradient term to describe interfaces, and of LAB 506.

The regular solution is the Ising model of alloys

A binary solution of A and B atoms on a lattice with $z$ neighbours, with pair energies $\varepsilon_{AA}$, $\varepsilon_{BB}$ and $\varepsilon_{AB}$, has in the mean-field approximation the Gibbs energy of mixing per mole $$\Delta G_\mathrm{mix}=\Omega x(1-x)+RT[x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)],\qquad\Omega=zN_\mathrm A\Bigl[\varepsilon_{AB}-\tfrac12(\varepsilon_{AA}+\varepsilon_{BB})\Bigr].$$ This is exactly the expression used in LAB 505 with $\Omega=L$ the interaction parameter, and in the Cahn–Hilliard model of LAB 506 as a double well. With the mapping $\varepsilon_{AA}=\varepsilon_{BB}=-J$, $\varepsilon_{AB}=+J$ one finds $\Omega=2zN_\mathrm AJ$, and the critical temperature of demixing, from $\partial^2\Delta G/\partial x^2=\partial^3\Delta G/\partial x^3=0$ at $x=\tfrac12$, is $T_c=\Omega/2R$, equal to $zJ/k_\mathrm B$: the same as the mean-field Ising result. Below $T_c$ the common tangent (binodal) is found from $\ln\frac{x}{1-x}=-\frac{\Omega}{RT}(1-2x)$ and the spinodal, where $\partial^2\Delta G/\partial x^2=0$, from $x(1-x)=RT/2\Omega$.

Lecture 7. Time correlations, transport and the error of a simulation#

So far we have computed averages over the ensemble. A molecular-dynamics run produces instead a time series $A(t)$, and the connection is the ergodic hypothesis: for a system that explores all its accessible microstates, the long-time average equals the ensemble average, $\bar A=\lim\frac1\tau\int_0^\tau A(t)\,\mathrm dt=\langle A\rangle$. The hypothesis fails when a system is trapped: a glass cannot reach the crystal on any practical timescale; an Ising model below $T_c$ stays in $m>0$ or $m<0$ for a time that grows as $e^{N}$; in LAB 504 a quench of a $64\times64$ lattice at $T=1.8$ trapped the system in a striped state that the ensemble average would never contain. Whether a simulation is ergodic on the time of the run is something that must be checked (two different initial states, several seeds), not assumed.

Correlation functions

The time autocorrelation function of a fluctuating quantity $A$ is $$C_{AA}(t)=\frac{\langle\delta A(0)\,\delta A(t)\rangle}{\langle\delta A^2\rangle},\qquad\delta A=A-\langle A\rangle,$$ equal to $1$ at $t=0$ and decaying to $0$ over a correlation time. Many transport coefficients are integrals of such functions (Green–Kubo relations); for the self-diffusion coefficient $$D=\frac13\int_0^\infty\langle\mathbf v(0)\cdot\mathbf v(t)\rangle\,\mathrm dt,$$ and the equivalent Einstein relation in terms of the mean-square displacement is $D=\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle|\mathbf r(t)-\mathbf r(0)|^2\rangle/6t$ (the one used in LAB 502). The viscosity and the thermal conductivity have analogous forms with the stress and the heat current. Check the formulae with the random walk of LAB 101: the velocity (the step $\pm1$ per unit time) is uncorrelated from one step to the next, so $\langle v(0)v(t)\rangle=\delta_{t,0}$ and the integral (here a sum) gives $D=\tfrac12\langle v^2\rangle=0.5$ in one dimension, in agreement with the measured slope $\langle x^2\rangle=2Dt$ ($D=0.48$ from $2000$ walkers). The static correlation functions describe the structure: the radial distribution function $g(r)$ of the argon liquid in LAB 502 is the probability of finding an atom at distance $r$ from another, and its zero-wavevector Fourier transform is related to the compressibility, $S(0)=\rho k_\mathrm BT\kappa_T$: structure and thermodynamics are connected by the fluctuations of Lecture 4.

How long do samples stay correlated?

Consecutive samples of a Monte Carlo or molecular-dynamics run are not independent. If a quantity has an autocorrelation function $C(t)$, the integrated autocorrelation time is $\tau_\mathrm{int}=\tfrac12+\sum_{t\ge1}C(t)$ (in units of the sampling interval; for a pure exponential decay $C=e^{-t/\tau}$ it equals $\tau$). A run of $N$ samples then contains only $N_\mathrm{eff}=N/2\tau_\mathrm{int}$ independent ones, and the statistical error of the mean is $$\boxed{\varepsilon=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\tau_\mathrm{int}}{N}}}$$ where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the quantity. A naive estimate $\sigma/\sqrt N$ is too small by the factor $\sqrt{2\tau_\mathrm{int}}$. In LAB 504, $\tau_\mathrm{int}$ of the magnetisation of a $32\times32$ lattice is $3.1$ sweeps at $T=1.8$ and $161$ sweeps at $T_c$ (critical slowing down): at $T_c$, $40\,000$ sweeps contain $124$ independent samples, and the error bar is $\sqrt{2\times161}=18$ times larger than the naive one.

Block averaging

When the autocorrelation function is not available, divide the series into $n_b$ blocks of length $L_b$ longer than $\tau_\mathrm{int}$, average inside each block, and compute the standard error of the block means, $\sigma_b/\sqrt{n_b}$. If $L_b$ is larger than the correlation time the block means are independent, and the error estimate is reliable; plotting it against $L_b$ shows a plateau when $L_b\gg\tau$. The method is more robust than estimating $\tau$ directly and is the standard way to quote an error bar in simulation papers.

Worked example 7.1: the error bar of a diffusion coefficient

A molecular-dynamics run of $N=10^5$ samples of an observable with $\sigma=2.0$ (arbitrary units) has an integrated autocorrelation time of $50$ sampling intervals. What is the statistical error of the mean, and how many independent samples are there? How long would the run have to be to halve the error?

$N_\mathrm{eff}=10^5/(2\times50)=1000$ independent samples. The error is $\varepsilon=2.0\sqrt{2\times50/10^5}=0.063$, compared with the naive $\sigma/\sqrt N=0.0063$, ten times smaller and wrong. To halve the error one needs four times as many samples, $4\times10^5$: the cost of precision grows as the inverse square of the error, and the cost of a correlated observable is multiplied by $2\tau_\mathrm{int}$. Running ten independent trajectories of $10^5$ samples would give the error directly from their scatter (the standard error of ten means), which has the same statistical power and protects against a trajectory that is trapped.

Key takeaways
  • Statistical mechanics connects the microscopic world to thermodynamics: the entropy S = k_B ln Omega counts the microstates compatible with a macrostate, temperature is defined by 1/T = dS/dU, and the equilibrium state is the overwhelmingly most probable one (two Einstein solids with 300 oscillators each share energy within about 5 quanta of the mean 50).
  • A system in contact with a heat bath has probability P_i = exp(-E_i/k_B T)/Z; the partition function Z contains all thermodynamics: F = -k_B T ln Z, U = -d ln Z/d beta, S = -dF/dT, and the heat capacity C_V = (U^2 variance)/(k_B T^2).
  • Simple partition functions give the classic results: the two-level system has a Schottky heat-capacity peak of 0.439 N k_B at k_B T = 0.417 epsilon, the quantum oscillator gives the Einstein heat capacity, and the ideal gas gives the Sackur-Tetrode entropy (154.85 J/mol/K for argon at 298.15 K and 1 bar, against the calorimetric 154.84).
  • Equilibrium concentrations of defects follow from minimising F: the vacancy fraction is exp(-E_f/k_B T) (for E_f = 1.28 eV it is 3e-22 at 300 K and 1.1e-5 at 1300 K), and the relation C_V = Var(E)/(k_B T^2) means that thermodynamic derivatives can be measured from fluctuations in a simulation.
  • Quantum statistics explain the heat capacity of solids (Debye T^3 law, with 4.8e-5 J/mol/K^4 T^3 for copper) and of electrons (the Fermi energy of copper is 7.0 eV, so electrons are degenerate up to 81 000 K and contribute only a linear term); mean-field theory of the Ising model gives T_c = zJ/k_B = 4 J/k_B for the square lattice, too high by 76 per cent compared with the exact 2.269, and Monte Carlo corrects it.

(a) At $933$ K, $E_f/k_\mathrm BT=0.67/0.08040=8.33$, $e^{-8.33}=2.40\times10^{-4}$, times $e^{0.6}=1.82$: $n/N=4.4\times10^{-4}$. At $300$ K, $E_f/k_\mathrm BT=25.9$ and $n/N=1.0\times10^{-11}$. (b) $4.4\times10^{-4}\times6.02\times10^{22}=2.6\times10^{19}$ cm$^{-3}$. (c) The diffusion coefficient is proportional to the product of the vacancy fraction and the rate of vacancy jumps, $D\propto\exp[-(E_f+E_m)/k_\mathrm BT]$, where $E_m$ is the migration energy; with $E_f=0.67$ eV the migration energy is about $1.3-0.67=0.6$ eV. At $300$ K the number of vacancies is negligible and diffusion is extremely slow, which is why aluminium alloys are annealed at high temperature.

(a) $4.4\times10^{-4}$ at $933$ K; $1\times10^{-11}$ at $300$ K (b) $2.6\times10^{19}$ cm$^{-3}$ (c) $Q=E_f+E_m$, $E_m\approx0.6$ eV

Problem 7Core

Aluminium has three conduction electrons per atom and $n=18.1\times10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$. (a) Find $E_F$ and $T_F$. (b) What fraction of the electrons can be thermally excited at $300$ K? (c) Estimate the electronic heat capacity coefficient $\gamma=\tfrac{\pi^2}2R/T_F$ per mole of electrons; the measured value for aluminium is $1.35$ mJ/(mol K$^2$) per mole of atoms.

Hint

$E_F=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}$; per mole of atoms there are $3$ moles of electrons.

Full solution

(a) $E_F=11.67$ eV ($=1.87\times10^{-18}$ J), $T_F=1.35\times10^5$ K. (b) The excitable fraction is about $\tfrac32k_\mathrm BT/E_F=1.5\times0.02585/11.67=0.33\%$ (the order of $T/T_F$). (c) For one mole of electrons $\gamma=\tfrac{\pi^2}2(8.314)/(1.354\times10^5)=0.303$ mJ/(mol K$^2$); there are three moles of electrons per mole of Al atoms, so $\gamma=0.91$ mJ/(mol K$^2$) per mole of atoms, compared with $1.35$ measured: the measured value is $1.5$ times larger, again from electron–phonon and electron–electron interactions (thermal mass $m^*\approx1.5\,m_e$).

(a) $11.7$ eV, $1.35\times10^5$ K (b) $\approx0.3\%$ (c) $0.91$ mJ/(mol K$^2$) (measured $1.35$)

Problem 8Core

At low temperature the molar heat capacity of a metal is $C=\gamma T+\beta T^3$. A measurement gives $C/T=0.888$ mJ/(mol K$^2$) at $T^2=4$ K$^2$ and $1.466$ mJ/(mol K$^2$) at $T^2=16$ K$^2$. (a) Find $\gamma$ and $\beta$. (b) Find the Debye temperature from $\beta=12\pi^4R/(5\theta_D^3)$. (c) At what temperature are the two contributions equal?

Hint

$C/T=\gamma+\beta T^2$ is a straight line in $T^2$.

Full solution

(a) The slope is $\beta=(1.466-0.888)/(16-4)=0.0482$ mJ/(mol K$^4$) $=4.82\times10^{-5}$ J/(mol K$^4$) and the intercept $\gamma=0.888-4(0.0482)=0.695$ mJ/(mol K$^2$). (b) $\theta_D=[12\pi^4R/(5\beta)]^{1/3}=[12(97.41)(8.314)/(5\times4.82\times10^{-5})]^{1/3}=(4.03\times10^7)^{1/3}=343$ K: the Debye temperature of copper. (c) $\gamma T=\beta T^3$ at $T=\sqrt{\gamma/\beta}=\sqrt{0.695/0.0482}=3.8$ K.

(a) $\gamma=0.695$ mJ/(mol K$^2$), $\beta=4.82\times10^{-5}$ J/(mol K$^4$) (b) $343$ K (c) $3.8$ K

Problem 9Challenge

(a) Derive the mean-field critical temperature $T_c=zJ/k_\mathrm B$ of the Ising model from $m=\tanh(zJm/k_\mathrm BT)$. (b) For the square lattice, solve for $m$ at $T=2.0$, $3.0$ and $3.8$ in units of $J/k_\mathrm B$ and compare at $T=2.0$ with the exact value $0.9113$. (c) Using the expansion $\tanh y\approx y-y^3/3$, show that $m\approx\sqrt{3(1-T/T_c)}$ near $T_c$ and evaluate it at $T=3.8$.

Hint

A nonzero solution exists when the slope of $\tanh(m/\tau)$ at the origin exceeds $1$, $\tau=k_\mathrm BT/zJ$.

Full solution

(a) Near $m=0$, $\tanh(m/\tau)\approx m/\tau$, so the equation $m=m/\tau$ has the slope $1/\tau$ at the origin; for $\tau<1$ the curve starts above the line $y=m$ and intersects it again at finite $m$ (while $\tanh$ saturates at $1$). At $\tau=1$, $k_\mathrm BT_c=zJ$. (b) Numerically, with $z=4$: $T=2.0$: $m=\tanh(2m)\Rightarrow m=0.9575$; $T=3.0$: $m=0.7755$; $T=3.8$: $m=0.3795$. At $T=2.0$ the exact value is $0.9113$ (and the Monte Carlo of LAB 504 gives $0.910$): the mean-field value is $5\%$ too high, and at $T=3.0$ the exact lattice is already nearly disordered ($m\approx0.1$ in a finite lattice, where mean field predicts $0.78$). (c) $m=\tanh(m/\tau)\approx m/\tau-m^3/3\tau^3$ gives $1=1/\tau-m^2/3\tau^3$, hence $m^2=3\tau^2(1-\tau)\approx3(1-\tau)$ near $\tau=1$ (here $\tau=T/T_c$). At $T=3.8$: $\tau=0.95$ and $m\approx\sqrt{3(0.05)}=0.387$, close to the exact mean-field solution $0.3795$.

(a) $T_c=zJ/k_\mathrm B$ (b) $0.9575$, $0.7755$, $0.3795$ (exact at $T=2$: $0.911$) (c) $m\approx\sqrt{3(1-T/T_c)}=0.387$

Problem 10Challenge

For a regular solution with $\Omega=14\,000$ J/mol (Worked example 6.1), (a) show that the critical temperature is $T_c=\Omega/2R$ and (b) compute the spinodal compositions at $T=700$ K. (c) A solution of composition $x=0.30$ is quenched from high temperature to $700$ K. Is it inside the spinodal? What about $x=0.20$?

Hint

The spinodal is where $\partial^2\Delta G/\partial x^2=RT/[x(1-x)]-2\Omega=0$.

Full solution

(a) $\partial^2\Delta G/\partial x^2=RT/[x(1-x)]-2\Omega$ has the minimum value $4RT-2\Omega$ at $x=\tfrac12$, and it is negative for some $x$ only if $4RT<2\Omega$, i.e. below $T_c=\Omega/2R=842$ K. (b) At $700$ K, $x(1-x)=RT/2\Omega=8.314\times700/28\,000=0.2079$; $x=\tfrac12[1-\sqrt{1-0.8316}]=\tfrac12(1-0.4103)=0.295$, and $0.705$ by symmetry. (c) $x=0.30$ is just inside the spinodal ($0.295<0.30<0.705$): unstable to composition fluctuations, it decomposes by spinodal decomposition. $x=0.20$ is outside the spinodal but inside the miscibility gap (the binodal at $700$ K is at $0.17$, from $\ln\frac x{1-x}=-\frac{\Omega}{RT}(1-2x)$), so it is metastable and separates only by nucleation: slower and with a different microstructure.

(a) $T_c=\Omega/2R=842$ K (b) $0.295$ and $0.705$ (c) $0.30$ spinodal; $0.20$ metastable

Problem 11Challenge

A simulation yields $10^5$ samples of an energy with $\sigma=2.0$ eV and autocorrelation function $C(t)=e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau=50$ sampling intervals. (a) Compute $\tau_\mathrm{int}$, the number of independent samples and the statistical error of the mean energy. (b) What block length makes block averaging reliable? (c) If the autocorrelation time at a second temperature is $5$ intervals, how much shorter can the run be for the same error?

Hint

For $C(t)=e^{-t/\tau}$ the sum is $\tau_\mathrm{int}=\tfrac12+\sum_{t\ge1}e^{-t/\tau}\approx\tau$.

Full solution

(a) $\tau_\mathrm{int}=\tfrac12+1/(e^{1/50}-1)=50.5$ intervals ($\approx\tau$). $N_\mathrm{eff}=10^5/(2\times50.5)=990$. Error $=2.0\sqrt{2\times50.5/10^5}=0.064$ eV. (b) The blocks must be long compared with $\tau$: at least $5$–$10\tau$, i.e. $250$–$500$ intervals, which gives $200$–$400$ blocks from the run (enough to estimate the standard error of the block means to $5\%$). (c) The error at fixed $\sigma$ depends on $N/\tau_\mathrm{int}$: with $\tau$ ten times smaller the same error is obtained with $N=10^4$, a run ten times shorter: the cost of a simulation is dominated by the slowest relaxation.

(a) $\tau_\mathrm{int}=50.5$, $N_\mathrm{eff}=990$, error $0.064$ eV (b) $\ge250$–$500$ intervals (c) ten times shorter

Problem 12Challenge

Show that the self-diffusion coefficient from the Green–Kubo formula agrees with the Einstein relation for a one-dimensional walker whose velocity correlation is $\langle v(0)v(t)\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle e^{-|t|/\tau}$. (a) Compute $D$ from $D=\int_0^\infty\langle v(0)v(t)\rangle\mathrm dt$. (b) Compute the mean-square displacement $\langle x^2(t)\rangle=2\int_0^t(t-s)\langle v(0)v(s)\rangle\,\mathrm ds$ and its long-time limit. (c) What is the short-time behaviour and what does it mean physically?

Hint

$\int_0^te^{-s/\tau}\mathrm ds=\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ and $\int_0^tse^{-s/\tau}\mathrm ds=\tau^2-\tau(t+\tau)e^{-t/\tau}$.

Full solution

(a) In one dimension $D=\int_0^\infty\langle v(0)v(t)\rangle\mathrm dt=\langle v^2\rangle\tau$ (with $\langle v^2\rangle=k_\mathrm BT/m$ this is the Einstein relation $D=k_\mathrm BT\tau/m=k_\mathrm BT/\gamma_f$ with a friction coefficient $\gamma_f=m/\tau$). (b) $\int_0^t(t-s)e^{-s/\tau}\mathrm ds=t\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})-[\tau^2-\tau(t+\tau)e^{-t/\tau}]=t\tau-\tau^2+\tau^2e^{-t/\tau}$. So $\langle x^2\rangle=2\langle v^2\rangle\tau[t-\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})]\to2Dt-2D\tau$ at long times: a straight line of slope $2D$, which confirms that $D$ from the slope of the mean-square displacement and from the Green–Kubo integral are the same. (c) For $t\ll\tau$, $e^{-t/\tau}\approx1-t/\tau+t^2/2\tau^2$ gives $\langle x^2\rangle\approx\langle v^2\rangle t^2$: ballistic motion (the particle has not yet forgotten its velocity), crossing over to diffusive motion ($\propto t$) at $t\gg\tau$. This is why the mean-square displacement in MD (LAB 502) must be fitted after the ballistic regime, in the linear part.

(a) $D=\langle v^2\rangle\tau$ (b) $\langle x^2\rangle=2D[t-\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})]\to2Dt-2D\tau$ (c) ballistic $\propto t^2$ then diffusive

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Basic

State the fundamental postulate of statistical mechanics and use it to explain why heat flows from hot to cold.

Solution

An isolated system in equilibrium is equally likely to be in any accessible microstate, so macrostates occur with probability proportional to their multiplicity. When two systems exchange energy, the total multiplicity $\Omega_A(U_A)\Omega_B(U-U_A)$ is overwhelmingly largest where $\partial S_A/\partial U_A=\partial S_B/\partial U_B$, i.e. at equal temperature. Starting from unequal temperatures the energy moves to the state of larger total multiplicity: the hotter system (smaller $\partial S/\partial U$) loses energy to the colder one, because that increases the total entropy.

Quiz 2Basic

A system has energy levels $0$, $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon$ (a doubly degenerate excited level). Write $Z$ and the probability of the ground level, and give the limits for $T\to0$ and $T\to\infty$.

Solution

$Z=1+2e^{-\varepsilon/k_\mathrm BT}$, so $P_0=1/Z$. For $T\to0$, $P_0\to1$ (all in the ground state); for $T\to\infty$, $Z\to3$ and $P_0\to\tfrac13$: all three states equally likely (the degeneracy counts states, not levels). The entropy tends to $k_\mathrm B\ln3$.

Quiz 3Core

Explain how $C_V$ can be obtained from the fluctuations of the energy in a canonical simulation, and why a microcanonical run cannot give the same variance.

Solution

$C_V=\langle(\delta E)^2\rangle/k_\mathrm BT^2$: it follows from differentiating $\langle E\rangle=-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$ twice. In the canonical ensemble $E$ fluctuates because of the contact with the bath; in an NVE run $E$ is constant (apart from integration drift), so its variance is zero and $C_V$ must be obtained from the fluctuations of the kinetic energy (Lebowitz–Percus–Verlet formula) or by differentiating the mean energy of several runs. Thermostats must be correct canonical ones for the formula to apply.

Quiz 4Core

Why does the Dulong–Petit law fail for diamond at room temperature, and why does the Einstein model fail at very low temperature while the Debye model works?

Solution

The quanta of the vibrations of diamond ($k_\mathrm B\theta_E\approx0.11$ eV) are larger than $k_\mathrm BT=0.026$ eV at $300$ K, so most modes are frozen and $C=6.1$ J/(mol K)$\ll3R$. The Einstein model has all modes at one frequency, so at low $T$ the heat capacity decays exponentially, $\propto e^{-\theta_E/T}$; real solids have long-wavelength acoustic modes with frequencies going to zero ($\omega=ck$), which can always be excited and give the $T^3$ law of the Debye model (the density of modes grows as $\omega^2$).

Quiz 5Core

The electrons of copper are described by $E_F=7.03$ eV. Explain why their heat capacity at $300$ K is only about $1\%$ of the classical value $\tfrac32k_\mathrm B$ per electron, and how the temperature dependence differs from that of the lattice.

Solution

The electron gas is degenerate: only electrons within about $k_\mathrm BT$ of the Fermi level can change state, a fraction $\sim k_\mathrm BT/E_F=0.0037$ of them. Classical equipartition would give each electron $\tfrac32k_\mathrm B$; the quantum result is $\tfrac{\pi^2}{2}k_\mathrm B(T/T_F)$ per electron, i.e. a factor $\sim T/T_F=0.4\%$ ($0.0037\times\pi^2/3\approx1.2\%$ of $\tfrac32k_\mathrm B$). It is linear in $T$ while the lattice contribution goes as $T^3$ at low $T$ and saturates at $3R$ above $\theta_D$.

Quiz 6Challenge

You run a Monte Carlo simulation of a binary alloy near its critical temperature. The energy autocorrelation time is $2000$ steps and you have $2\times10^6$ steps. How many independent samples do you have, and what would you do to improve the estimate of the error bar and the confidence in the run?

Solution

$N_\mathrm{eff}=N/2\tau=2\times10^6/4000=500$. The error bar of the mean is $\sigma\sqrt{2\tau/N}=\sigma\times0.045$, not $\sigma\times7\times10^{-4}$. Improve: (i) run several independent chains from different initial states and seeds (this also tests ergodicity: a trapped chain would give a result different from the others), (ii) use block averaging with blocks of at least $10\tau=20\,000$ steps to get an error bar that does not depend on estimating $\tau$, (iii) consider a cluster algorithm or parallel tempering to reduce critical slowing down, and (iv) move away from $T_c$ for quantities that do not require it.
