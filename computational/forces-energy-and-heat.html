Forces, energy, heat and motion

This page covers the physics that molecular simulations are built on: how forces change motion, how energy is stored and exchanged, and why temperature is really a measure of how much atoms are jiggling. Along the way you will see why atoms behave like little masses on springs.

1. Describing motion#

To describe how something moves we use three linked quantities. Position is where it is. Velocity is how fast its position is changing (a speed together with a direction), in metres per second. Acceleration is how fast its velocity is changing, in metres per second per second (m/s2). Each one is the rate of change of the one before, so they are linked by the derivatives of the calculus page. If an object starts with velocity v0 and accelerates steadily at a, its velocity after time t is v = v0 + a t. Near the Earth’s surface, falling objects accelerate downward at g = 9.8 m/s2.

2. Forces and Newton’s laws#

A force is a push or a pull. It is measured in newtons (N), where one newton is the force that gives a one-kilogram mass an acceleration of one metre per second per second. Forces have a size and a direction, so they are vectors (arrows). Isaac Newton summarised how forces change motion in three laws:

  1. Inertia. An object with no net force on it keeps moving at constant velocity (or stays still). It takes a force to change motion.
  2. F = ma. A net force F on a mass m produces an acceleration a = F/m. The same force accelerates a light object more than a heavy one.
  3. Action and reaction. When one object pushes on another, the second pushes back with an equal and opposite force.

Worked example. A force of 10 N acts on a 2 kg block. Its acceleration is 10/2 = 5 m/s2. If it starts from rest, after 3 s its speed is 5 × 3 = 15 m/s.

The second law is the heart of molecular dynamics: if you know the force on every atom you can calculate its acceleration, update its velocity and position a tiny time later, and repeat. Everything depends on knowing the forces, and where the forces come from is the next section.

3. Energy#

Energy is the capacity to cause change. It comes in many forms, and the key fact about it is that it can be converted from one form to another but is never created or destroyed: it is conserved. The unit is the joule (J). Two forms matter most here:

  • Kinetic energy is energy of motion: K = ½ m v2. Doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy.
  • Potential energy is energy stored because of position or arrangement. Lift a mass m a height h and it stores m g h. Stretch a spring and it stores ½ k x2. The chemical bond between two atoms stores energy depending on their separation.

Energy conservation means kinetic plus potential energy stays constant when there is no friction: K + U = constant. Worked example. A ball is dropped from a height of 5 m. At the top it has potential energy m g(5) and no kinetic energy. At the bottom all of it has become kinetic: ½ m v2 = m g(5), so v = √(2 × 9.8 × 5) ≈ 9.9 m/s, whatever the mass.

The same idea in mechanical form: when a force moves an object, it does work equal to force times distance, and that work changes the energy. Power is the rate of doing work, in watts (1 W = 1 J/s).

4. Energy landscapes#

Plot the potential energy against position and you get a curve like a landscape of hills and valleys. A ball released on it rolls downhill. This picture is so useful that it is used for everything from rolling balls to chemical reactions to atoms.

  • A valley bottom is a stable equilibrium: nudge the ball and it rolls back.
  • A hilltop is an unstable equilibrium: the slightest push makes it roll away.
  • A pass between two valleys is a barrier: to get from one valley to the next the ball needs enough energy to climb it. The height of the barrier above the valley decides how likely the crossing is, which is why chemical reactions and atomic jumps need energy to start.
  • The force on the ball is the downhill slope: F = −dU/dx. Steep slope, big force; flat bottom, no force.

For atoms the same applies, with many dimensions instead of one: the energy depends on where all the atoms are, and they move toward valleys of that energy landscape. This is the idea behind the bond curve on the atoms page.

5. Springs and vibrations#

Stretch a spring and it pulls back; compress it and it pushes out. For many springs, and for atoms displaced a small distance from equilibrium, the force is proportional to the displacement: Hooke’s law, F = −k x. The constant k is the stiffness, in newtons per metre. Its potential energy is ½ k x2, a parabola, matching the bottom of any smooth energy valley.

A mass on a spring, once displaced, oscillates back and forth. How fast depends on the stiffness and the mass: a stiffer spring is quicker, a heavier mass is slower. The time for one full cycle, the period, is

T = 2π √(m / k),    frequency f = 1/T

Worked example. For k = 10 N/m and m = 1 kg, T = 2π√(1/10) ≈ 2.0 s, so the frequency is 0.5 hertz (cycles per second).

Now the key observation: atoms bonded together behave like masses joined by springs, because near the bond length the energy curve is a parabola. A typical bond has stiffness about 20 N/m, and a copper atom has a mass of 63.5 atomic mass units (1.05 × 10−25 kg). The same formula then gives a frequency of about 2 trillion vibrations per second (2 THz), a period under a picosecond. Atoms really do shake this fast, which is why simulations of atoms must take time steps of a femtosecond.

6. Heat and temperature#

What is temperature, really? Atoms in a solid, liquid or gas are always moving. In a solid they vibrate about fixed positions; in a gas they fly about. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of that random atomic motion: the hotter something is, the faster its atoms jiggle. Heat is the transfer of that energy from a hotter body to a colder one, as the hot jiggling atoms nudge their cooler neighbours.

The link is precise. The average kinetic energy per atom is (3/2) kB T, where T is the temperature in kelvin and kB = 8.617 × 10−5 eV/K is the Boltzmann constant, the conversion between temperature and energy. At room temperature (300 K) the thermal energy kBT is only 0.0259 eV, much smaller than a bond energy of a few eV, which is why solids stay together at room temperature. At absolute zero (0 K, or −273.15 °C) the motion is at its minimum.

This explains many everyday facts. Solids expand when heated because bigger vibrations push the average spacing out (a bond curve is steeper on the squeezed side than on the stretched side). They melt when the jiggling becomes so large that atoms break out of their places; a useful rule of thumb is that this happens when atoms move by about a tenth of the atomic spacing. 7. States of matter#

At low temperature atoms are held in place (solid); with more thermal energy they slide past each other but stay in contact (liquid); with still more they fly apart (gas). Changing from one state to another takes energy, called latent heat, even though the temperature does not change while it happens: melting ice at 0 °C absorbs 334 kJ per kilogram before the temperature rises again.

Materials scientists use the word phase more broadly for any region with uniform structure and composition. Solid iron, for example, has different crystal structures (phases) at different temperatures: body-centred cubic at room temperature and face-centred cubic above 912 °C. Which phases are present, and how fast they change, controls the properties of steel, and predicting this is a major use of simulation.

8. Stress and strain#

To describe how a solid object responds to a load, engineers use quantities that do not depend on its size. Stress is the force per unit area, σ = F/A, in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m2). Strain is the stretch as a fraction of the original length, ε = ΔL/L, with no unit. For small loads most solids follow a linear law, the material version of Hooke’s law:

σ = E ε

The constant E is Young’s modulus, the stiffness of the material: about 210 GPa for steel, 70 GPa for aluminium, 3 GPa for nylon. It comes directly from the bond stiffness. If the load goes beyond the yield stress, the deformation becomes permanent (dislocations have started to move); eventually the material fractures.

Worked example. A steel rod 1 m long with a cross-section of 100 mm2 (1.0 × 10−4 m2) carries a load of 10 kN. Stress = 10 000 N/1.0 × 10−4 m2 = 1.0 × 108 Pa = 100 MPa. Strain = σ/E = 108/(210 × 109) = 4.8 × 10−4. The rod stretches by 4.8 × 10−4 × 1 m = 0.48 mm, which is invisible to the eye but very real.

Key takeaways
  • A force changes motion according to F = ma, and forces on atoms come from the slope of an energy curve.
  • Energy is conserved: it moves between kinetic (motion) and potential (position) forms.
  • Atoms joined by bonds behave like masses on springs, and they vibrate at around ten trillion times a second.
  • Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of atoms; heating a solid makes the atoms jiggle more until it melts.
  • Stress is force per area, strain is relative stretch, and their ratio for a given material is its stiffness.

9. Check your understanding#

A force of 10 N acts on a 2 kg mass. What is its acceleration?

a = F/m = 10/2 = 5 m/s2.

A ball is dropped from 5 m. How fast is it going just before it lands (ignoring air resistance)?

Energy conservation: ½v2 = g h, so v = √(2 × 9.8 × 5) = 9.9 m/s.

What is the period of a 1 kg mass on a spring with stiffness 10 N/m?

T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√0.1 ≈ 1.99 s.

How does the average thermal energy per atom at 600 K compare with that at 300 K?

It is proportional to temperature (3/2 kBT), so at 600 K it is exactly twice as large.

A steel rod (E = 210 GPa) carries a stress of 100 MPa. What is the strain?

ε = σ/E = 108/(2.1 × 1011) = 4.8 × 10−4, or 0.048%.

Why do solids expand when they are heated?

Heating makes atoms vibrate with larger amplitude. Because the bond energy curve is steeper on the squeezed side than on the stretched side, larger vibrations push the average spacing to larger values, so the whole solid grows.
