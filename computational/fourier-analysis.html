Fourier series and transforms

Crystals are periodic, vibrations are waves, diffraction patterns are the Fourier transform of a structure, and the diffusion equation turns into independent decay problems once you split a profile into sinusoids. Fourier analysis, the decomposition of functions into waves, is therefore one of the most widely used tools in computational materials science: plane-wave DFT, spectral phase-field solvers, Ewald summation, spectral analysis of molecular dynamics, and the interpretation of X-ray, neutron and electron diffraction. This course develops the theory carefully, from complex numbers to the fast Fourier transform and Bloch’s theorem.

Lecture 1. Complex numbers and exponentials#

A complex number is $z=x+iy$ with $i^2=-1$; $x=\operatorname{Re}z$, $y=\operatorname{Im}z$. Its conjugate is $\bar z=x-iy$ and its modulus is $|z|=\sqrt{z\bar z}=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. In the complex plane it has a polar form $z=re^{i\theta}$, with $r=|z|$ and $\theta=\arg z$. The key link is $$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\qquad\text{(Euler’s formula),}$$ which can be checked by comparing the Taylor series of $e^{i\theta}$, $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ (MATH 101). Consequences:

  • $|e^{i\theta}|=1$: the point moves around the unit circle; $e^{i\pi}=-1$ and $e^{2\pi i}=1$.
  • Multiplication adds angles and multiplies moduli, $re^{i\theta}\cdot se^{i\phi}=rs\,\allowbreak e^{i(\theta+\phi)}$: multiplying by $e^{i\phi}$ is a rotation by $\phi$. De Moivre: $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n=\cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta$.
  • $\cos\theta=\dfrac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2$, $\sin\theta=\dfrac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$. Product and power identities for trigonometric functions follow by multiplying exponentials (Problem 1).
  • The $N$-th roots of unity $\omega_m=e^{2\pi im/N}$ ($m=0,\ldots,N-1$) are equally spaced on the unit circle. Their key property is $\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi imn/N}=\begin{cases}N&n\text{ a multiple of }N\\0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$ a geometric series that underlies the discrete Fourier transform.

The function $e^{ikx}=\cos kx+i\sin kx$ is a plane wave: with time dependence, $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ travels to the right at speed $\omega/k$. A real oscillation $A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)$ is the real part of the phasor $\hat Ae^{i\omega t}$ with complex amplitude $\hat A=Ae^{i\varphi}$ carrying both size and phase; the driven oscillator of MATH 203 is solved in two lines this way.

Why the roots of unity sum to zero

For $n$ not a multiple of $N$, put $r=e^{2\pi in/N}\ne1$. The sum is a geometric series, $\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}r^m=\dfrac{1-r^N}{1-r}$, and $r^N=e^{2\pi in}=1$, so the numerator vanishes and the sum is $0$. If $n$ is a multiple of $N$, every term equals $1$ and the sum is $N$. This single fact makes the DFT invertible (Lecture 4).

Worked example 1.1: powers, roots and trigonometry

(a) Compute $(1+i)^8$. (b) Find the cube roots of $-8$. (c) Express $\cos^3\theta$ in terms of $\cos\theta$ and $\cos3\theta$.

(a) $1+i=\sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$, so $(1+i)^8=(\sqrt2)^8e^{2\pi i}=16$. (b) $-8=8e^{i\pi}$, so the roots are $2e^{i(\pi+2\pi m)/3}$ for $m=0,1,2$: $2e^{i\pi/3}=1+i\sqrt3$, $2e^{i\pi}=-2$ and $2e^{-i\pi/3}=1-i\sqrt3$, equally spaced on a circle of radius $2$. (c) $\cos^3\theta=\tfrac18\bigl(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}\bigr)^3=\tfrac18\bigl(e^{3i\theta}+3e^{i\theta}+3e^{-i\theta}+e^{-3i\theta}\bigr)=\tfrac14\cos3\theta+\tfrac34\cos\theta$. Check at $\theta=0$: $1=\tfrac14+\tfrac34$ ✓. This is how a cubic nonlinearity creates a third harmonic.

Lecture 2. Fourier series#

Orthogonality#

Let $f(x)$ have period $L$ and define $k_n=2\pi n/L$. The functions $e^{ik_nx}$ are orthogonal over one period: $$\frac1L\int_0^Le^{i(k_n-k_m)x}\,\mathrm dx=\delta_{nm},$$ because for $n\ne m$ the integral of a full number of oscillations vanishes. This is the continuous analogue of the orthonormal basis vectors of MATH 103, with the integral of $f\bar g$ playing the role of the dot product.

Fourier series

A function of period $L$ that is piecewise smooth can be written $$f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n\,e^{ik_nx},\qquad c_n=\frac1L\int_0^Lf(x)\,e^{-ik_nx}\,\mathrm dx .$$ $c_0$ is the mean of $f$. If $f$ is real, $c_{-n}=\bar c_n$, and the equivalent real form is $f=\tfrac12a_0+\sum_{n\ge1}\bigl(a_n\cos k_nx+b_n\sin k_nx\bigr)$ with $a_n=\tfrac2L\int f\cos k_nx\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$ and $b_n=\tfrac2L\int f\sin k_nx\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$.

The coefficient formula follows by multiplying the series by $e^{-ik_mx}$ and integrating: orthogonality kills every term except $n=m$. Parseval’s theorem says the mean square of $f$ equals the sum of squared coefficients: $$\frac1L\int_0^L|f|^2\,\mathrm dx=\sum_n|c_n|^2,$$ so the “power” of a signal is the sum of the powers of its harmonics. For even functions only cosines appear, for odd functions only sines (saving half the work).

Worked example 2.1: the sawtooth and the Basel sum

Find the Fourier series of $f(x)=x$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$, extended periodically ($L=2\pi$), and use Parseval’s theorem to sum $\sum1/n^2$.

$f$ is odd, so only sines: $b_n=\dfrac1\pi\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\sin nx\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\dfrac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin nx\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx$. By parts, $\int_0^\pi x\sin nx\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\Bigl[-\dfrac{x\cos nx}{n}\Bigr]_0^\pi+\int_0^\pi\dfrac{\cos nx}{n}\mathrm dx=-\dfrac{\pi(-1)^n}{n}+0$. Hence $b_n=\dfrac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$ and $$x=2\Bigl(\sin x-\frac{\sin2x}{2}+\frac{\sin3x}{3}-\cdots\Bigr).$$ Parseval (real form): mean square $\dfrac1{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^\pi x^2\mathrm dx=\dfrac{\pi^2}{3}$ equals $\tfrac12\sum b_n^2=\tfrac12\sum\dfrac4{n^2}$. Therefore $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6$. The coefficients decay only like $1/n$ because the periodic extension has jumps at $x=\pm\pi$.

Convergence, smoothness and the Gibbs phenomenon#

How many harmonics do you need? The answer depends on smoothness, and it matters every time a spectral method is used:

Function (periodic)Coefficients $|c_n|$ decay asExample
Jump discontinuity$1/n$square wave, sawtooth
Continuous, derivative has jumps$1/n^2$triangle wave
$p-1$ continuous derivatives, $p$-th jumps$1/n^{p+1}$
Infinitely smooth (analytic)faster than any power, typically $e^{-\alpha n}$$e^{\cos x}$, Gaussians, smooth densities

Each derivative multiplies $c_n$ by $ik_n$, which explains the pattern: differentiating a function $p$ times until it has a jump gives $n^p|c_n|\sim1/n$. Near a jump, partial sums overshoot by about $9\%$ of the jump height no matter how many terms are kept: the Gibbs phenomenon (the peak narrows but does not shrink). It appears whenever a sharp interface is represented in a truncated Fourier basis, for instance a sharp composition profile in a spectral phase-field simulation or a truncated density in plane-wave DFT, and is controlled by smoothing the interface over several grid points.

Worked example 2.2: the square wave

Expand $f(x)=+1$ on $(0,\pi)$, $-1$ on $(-\pi,0)$ (period $2\pi$).

$f$ is odd, so $b_n=\dfrac2\pi\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\sin nx\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\dfrac2{\pi n}\bigl(1-(-1)^n\bigr)$, which is $\dfrac4{\pi n}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$: $$f(x)=\frac4\pi\Bigl(\sin x+\frac{\sin3x}{3}+\frac{\sin5x}{5}+\cdots\Bigr).$$ At $x=\pi/2$, $f=1$ and $\sin(n\pi/2)$ alternates $1,-1,\ldots$ for odd $n$, giving the Leibniz series $1-\tfrac13+\tfrac15-\cdots=\tfrac\pi4$. The partial sums peak at $1.179$ near the jump however many terms are used ($1.182$ with $5$ terms, $1.179$ with $100$): the Gibbs overshoot of $0.179$, or $8.95\%$ of the jump of $2$.

Worked example 2.3: the triangle wave and how fast the coefficients decay

Expand $f(x)=|x|$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$ (period $2\pi$) and compare the decay with the square wave.

$f$ is even, so only cosines: $a_0/2=\tfrac1\pi\int_0^\pi x\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\tfrac\pi2$ and $a_n=\tfrac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\cos nx\,\allowbreak \mathrm dx=\tfrac2{\pi n^2}\bigl[(-1)^n-1\bigr]$, which is $-\dfrac4{\pi n^2}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$: $$|x|=\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\Bigl(\cos x+\frac{\cos3x}{9}+\frac{\cos5x}{25}+\cdots\Bigr).$$ Setting $x=0$ gives $\sum_{m\ge0}\dfrac1{(2m+1)^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2}8$. The coefficients fall as $1/n^2$ (the periodic extension is continuous with only a kink), against $1/n$ for the square wave (a jump). With only the $n=1$ term, the approximation at $x=0$ is $\tfrac\pi2-\tfrac4\pi=0.30$ against the true value $0$, and with $n=1,3$: $0.30-0.14=0.16$ — fast convergence and no Gibbs overshoot, because the function is continuous.

Lecture 3. The Fourier transform#

Let the period $L\to\infty$: the spacing of the wavenumbers $k_n$ goes to zero, the sum becomes an integral, and a non-periodic function can be written as a continuous superposition of plane waves. With the convention used here, $$F(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,\mathrm dx,\qquad f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(k)\,e^{ikx}\,\mathrm dk .$$ $F(k)$ is the amplitude of the plane wave of wavenumber $k$ in $f$. (Other books move the $2\pi$ or use $e^{2\pi i\nu x}$; always check which convention a code or paper uses.) The properties that make the transform so useful:

The scaling rule has a famous consequence. Let $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_k$ be the root-mean-square widths of $|f|^2$ and $|F|^2$; then $$\sigma_x\,\sigma_k\ge\tfrac12,$$ with equality only for Gaussians. A function cannot be sharply localised in both position and wavenumber; this is the mathematics behind the Heisenberg uncertainty principle ($p=\hbar k$), and the reason that a short pulse has a broad spectrum, a small crystal broad diffraction peaks, and a localised wave packet a spread of momenta.

Solving diffusion with Fourier transforms#

The diffusion equation $\partial_tc=D\partial_x^2c$ becomes, after transforming in $x$ (the derivative rule gives $\partial_x^2\to-k^2$), $$\frac{\partial\hat c(k,t)}{\partial t}=-Dk^2\,\hat c(k,t)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat c(k,t)=\hat c(k,0)\,e^{-Dk^2t}.$$ A partial differential equation has become an independent decay law for every wavenumber. Short wavelengths (large $k$) decay fastest, rate $Dk^2$, so diffusion smooths: it removes fine structure first. To get back to real space, note that multiplying by $e^{-Dk^2t}$ corresponds, by the convolution theorem, to convolving with the inverse transform of $e^{-Dk^2t}$. Comparing with the Gaussian pair (with $\sigma^2/2=Dt$) that inverse transform is the diffusion kernel $$G(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\,e^{-x^2/(4Dt)},\qquad c(x,t)=\int G(x-x',t)\,c(x',0)\,\mathrm dx' .$$ The profile from an initial delta is a Gaussian of variance $\sigma^2=2Dt$ (verified directly in MATH 201), and a general initial profile is smeared by this Gaussian. Because variances add under convolution of Gaussians ($\hat G$ multiplies), diffusing for $t_1$ and then $t_2$ equals diffusing for $t_1+t_2$ (Problem 6).

In materials science: homogenisation of a segregated profile

A cast alloy has composition varying sinusoidally with the dendrite spacing $\lambda$, $c=\bar c+A\cos(2\pi x/\lambda)$. In Fourier space only one mode is present, $k=2\pi/\lambda$, so $c=\bar c+Ae^{-t/\tau}\cos kx$ with $\tau=1/(Dk^2)=\lambda^2/(4\pi^2D)$: the time for a homogenising heat treatment grows as the square of the segregation wavelength (Problem 7). Real microsegregation contains many wavelengths, each decaying at its own rate: the longest persists longest.

Two proofs from the definition

Derivative rule. Integrate by parts, $\int f'(x)e^{-ikx}\mathrm dx=\bigl[f e^{-ikx}\bigr]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+ik\int fe^{-ikx}\mathrm dx=ik\,\allowbreak F(k)$, since $f\to0$ at infinity. Applied twice, $\nabla^2\to-k^2$.

Convolution theorem. $\int\!\!\int f(x')g(x-x')e^{-ikx}\mathrm dx'\mathrm dx$: substitute $u=x-x'$ so that $e^{-ikx}=e^{-ikx'}e^{-iku}$. The double integral factorises into $\int f(x')e^{-ikx'}\mathrm dx'\times\int g(u)e^{-iku}\mathrm du=F(k)G(k)$. Convolution in real space is therefore multiplication in Fourier space; this is why a diffusing profile is a Gaussian-filtered copy of the initial one, and why a measured diffraction peak is the true peak convolved with the instrument response.

Worked example 3.1: a finite crystal and a correlation length

(a) A slab of scatterers fills $|x|<a/2$ with $a=10$ nm. Where is the first zero of its transform? (b) A structure has correlations $e^{-|x|/\xi}$ with $\xi=5$ nm. Find the width of its line in $k$.

(a) $F(k)=2\sin(ka/2)/k$ vanishes first when $ka/2=\pi$, i.e. $k=2\pi/a=0.628\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. The central peak therefore has a full width $\approx4\pi/a$ (between the first zeros) and the width of a Bragg peak in $k$ scales as $1/(\text{crystal size})$: this is the Scherrer broadening. (b) The transform is Lorentzian, $F=2\xi/(1+\xi^2k^2)$. It falls to half its peak at $\xi k=1$, so the half-width is $1/\xi=0.2\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$ and the full width $0.4\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. A shorter correlation length means a broader line.

Lecture 4. The discrete Fourier transform and the FFT#

Computers handle a function only as $N$ samples $f_n=f(n\Delta)$, $n=0,\ldots,N-1$, at spacing $\Delta$ over a length $L=N\Delta$ (treated as periodic). The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is $$X_k=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f_n\,e^{-2\pi ikn/N},\qquad f_n=\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X_k\,e^{2\pi ikn/N},$$ the exact finite analogue of the Fourier series, and the inversion works because of the roots-of-unity sum of section 1. Parseval becomes $\sum|f_n|^2=\tfrac1N\sum|X_k|^2$. The index $k$ corresponds to the wavenumber $2\pi k/L$ (frequencies above $N/2$ are the negative ones, $k\to k-N$). The direct sum costs $O(N^2)$ operations; the fast Fourier transform (FFT) of Cooley and Tukey exploits the symmetry of the roots of unity (splitting even and odd samples recursively) to compute it in $O(N\log N)$, which turns a $10^6$-point transform from $10^{12}$ operations into $2\times10^7$. In three dimensions a grid of $n^3$ points is transformed in $O(n^3\log n)$ by one-dimensional FFTs along each axis.

Sampling, resolution and aliasing#

  • Resolution: the spacing of wavenumbers is $\Delta k=2\pi/L$. To resolve closely spaced peaks you need a long record or a large cell, which is why long molecular dynamics runs give sharper vibrational spectra.
  • Range and the Nyquist limit: the highest representable wavenumber is $k_\mathrm{Nyq}=\pi/\Delta$ (two samples per wavelength). For a time series sampled every $\Delta t$, the highest representable frequency is $1/(2\Delta t)$.
  • Aliasing: a wave faster than Nyquist is not lost; it is misread as a slower one. Samples of $\cos(2\pi\nu t)$ at rate $\nu_s$ are identical to those of a wave at the aliased frequency $|\nu-m\nu_s|$ for any integer $m$. A $9$ Hz signal sampled at $10$ Hz looks like $1$ Hz.
Aliasing in simulation data

If you save an MD trajectory every $20$ fs (sampling rate $50$ THz, Nyquist $25$ THz, $\approx833$ cm$^{-1}$) and compute a vibrational spectrum, a C–H stretch at $3000$ cm$^{-1}$ ($89.9$ THz) is aliased into a spurious peak at $|89.9-100|=10.1$ THz, about $336$ cm$^{-1}$, in the middle of the low-frequency lattice region (Problem 9). Save velocities at least twice as often as the fastest vibration, or low-pass filter first. The same logic limits the real-space grid of a plane-wave or spectral calculation: features smaller than twice the grid spacing are invisible or aliased.

Spectral differentiation#

Derivatives are exact and trivial in Fourier space, so a periodic derivative is computed as: transform, multiply by $ik$, transform back. For smooth periodic functions the error decreases faster than any power of $\Delta$ (“spectral accuracy”), whereas a finite difference has $O(\Delta^2)$ error. The effect of finite differences is visible in Fourier space: the three-point stencil $(f_{j+1}-2f_j+f_{j-1})/\Delta^2$ acting on $e^{ikx}$ returns $-\tfrac{4}{\Delta^2}\sin^2\tfrac{k\Delta}2\,\allowbreak e^{ikx}$ instead of $-k^2e^{ikx}$: the effective wavenumber is $k_\mathrm{eff}^2=\tfrac4{\Delta^2}\sin^2(k\Delta/2)$, correct only for $k\Delta\ll1$ (Problem 10). Spectral phase-field codes exploit this exactness and the speed of the FFT.

Worked example 4.1: a four-point DFT, and aliasing

(a) Compute the DFT of $x=(1,2,3,4)$. (b) A $70$ Hz signal is sampled at $100$ Hz. What frequency appears?

(a) $X_k=\sum_nx_ne^{-2\pi ikn/4}=\sum_nx_n(-i)^{kn}$. $X_0=10$; $X_1=1-2i-3+4i=-2+2i$; $X_2=1-2+3-4=-2$; $X_3=-2-2i=\overline{X_1}$ (as it must be for real input). The mean is $X_0/N=2.5$, and Parseval holds: $\sum|x_n|^2=30=\tfrac14\sum|X_k|^2=\tfrac14(100+8+4+8)=30$ ✓. (b) The Nyquist frequency is $f_s/2=50$ Hz. A $70$ Hz tone has $\cos(2\pi\cdot70\,n/100)=\cos(2\pi\cdot30\,n/100)$ at the sample times, so it appears as a $30$ Hz peak: an alias. The cure is to sample faster or low-pass filter first. The spectral resolution is $\Delta f=f_s/N$: with $N=2000$ samples the lines are $0.05$ Hz apart.

Lecture 5. Reciprocal space, diffraction and Bloch waves#

Diffraction is a Fourier transform#

When radiation of wavevector $\mathbf k_0$ scatters weakly from a distribution of scatterers with density $\rho(\mathbf r)$, the scattered amplitude into wavevector $\mathbf k$ is proportional to the Fourier transform of $\rho$ at the scattering vector $\mathbf q=\mathbf k-\mathbf k_0$: $$A(\mathbf q)\propto\int\rho(\mathbf r)\,e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,\mathrm d^3r,\qquad I(\mathbf q)=|A(\mathbf q)|^2 .$$ Now use the table of pairs. A crystal is a lattice (a comb) convolved with a unit-cell motif, so its transform is the reciprocal lattice (a comb) multiplied by the transform of the motif: sharp peaks only at reciprocal lattice vectors $\mathbf G$ (the Laue condition $\mathbf q=\mathbf G$, equivalent to Bragg’s law of MATH 103), with intensity set by the structure factor $$F_{hkl}=\sum_jf_j\,e^{-2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)},$$ the sum over the atoms $j$ of the motif at fractional positions $(x_j,y_j,z_j)$, weighted by atomic scattering factors $f_j$. When the sum vanishes for symmetry reasons, the reflection is absent: this is the origin of the selection rules distinguishing FCC, BCC and diamond structures. A finite crystal of length $L$ is the infinite one times a box of width $L$, so each peak is convolved with a sinc of width $\sim2\pi/L$: peak width is inverse to crystallite size. The Scherrer equation is the result, $\Delta(2\theta)=K\lambda/(L\cos\theta)$ with $K\approx0.9$ (Problem 5). Disordered materials (liquids, glasses) have no sharp peaks, but the transform of the pair-correlation function gives the structure factor $S(q)$, the main experimental link to simulation.

Bloch’s theorem and plane waves#

In a crystal the potential is periodic, $V(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=V(\mathbf r)$, so its Fourier series contains only reciprocal lattice vectors: $V(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf G}V_{\mathbf G}e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}$. Bloch’s theorem states that the electron wavefunctions can be chosen in the form $$\psi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),\qquad u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),$$ a plane wave times a function with the periodicity of the lattice. Expanding $u_{\mathbf k}$ in its own Fourier series gives $\psi_{\mathbf k}=\sum_{\mathbf G}c_{\mathbf k+\mathbf G}\,\allowbreak e^{i(\mathbf k+\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}$: a plane-wave basis, the basis of most DFT codes for solids. It is truncated at a cutoff energy $E_\mathrm{cut}$, $\hbar^2|\mathbf k+\mathbf G|^2/2m\le E_\mathrm{cut}$, and the number of plane waves grows as $E_\mathrm{cut}^{3/2}V$. The wavevector $\mathbf k$ is only defined modulo $\mathbf G$ (aliasing again: $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k+\mathbf G$ label the same Bloch state), so it can be restricted to one cell of reciprocal space, the first Brillouin zone; integrals over it are done by sampling a grid of $\mathbf k$-points. Where the free-electron energies of $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k-\mathbf G$ coincide ($\mathbf k=\mathbf G/2$, the zone boundary), the potential component $V_{\mathbf G}$ couples them and opens an energy gap of $2|V_{\mathbf G}|$ (Problem 11): the origin of band gaps.

Worked example 5.1: reading a powder pattern

Cu K$\alpha$ radiation ($\lambda=0.15406$ nm) hits silicon (diamond cubic, $a=0.5431$ nm). Where is the (111) peak, and what are the reciprocal-lattice vector and the first Brillouin-zone edge for a 1D chain with $a=0.4$ nm?

For cubic crystals $d_{111}=a/\sqrt3=0.3136$ nm. Bragg's law $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$ gives $\sin\theta=0.15406/0.6272=0.2456$, $\theta=14.22^\circ$, so the peak is at $2\theta=28.44^\circ$, the textbook Si(111) position. In reciprocal space the same statement is $|\mathbf G_{111}|=2\pi/d=20.0\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$, and diffraction occurs when the scattering vector equals a reciprocal-lattice vector. For the chain, the reciprocal lattice has spacing $G=2\pi/a=15.71\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$ and the Brillouin zone is $|k|<\pi/a=7.85\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$: wavenumbers differing by $G$ describe the same lattice wave, the aliasing of Lecture 4 in physical clothes.

Summary of the unit#

Complex exponentials are the natural waves; Fourier series and transforms express any function in terms of them, and the dictionary “derivative $\leftrightarrow\;ik$, convolution $\leftrightarrow$ product, width $\leftrightarrow$ inverse width” turns hard operations into easy ones. The diffusion equation is solved mode by mode; sampled data are handled by the DFT and FFT with the limits of resolution and Nyquist; and in a crystal, Fourier space is reciprocal space, where diffraction peaks, Brillouin zones and plane-wave bases live. These tools return in the next course, where Fourier methods and separation of variables solve the classic partial differential equations.

Key takeaways
  • Euler's formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ turns trigonometry into algebra; $e^{ikx}$ is a plane wave, and sines and cosines are combinations of $e^{\pm ikx}$.
  • Any reasonable periodic function is a sum of plane waves, $f(x)=\sum_nc_ne^{ik_nx}$, because the exponentials are orthogonal; the smoother the function, the faster the coefficients $c_n$ decay, and Parseval's theorem says total power is the same in real and Fourier space.
  • The Fourier transform extends this to non-periodic functions; differentiation becomes multiplication by $ik$, convolution becomes multiplication, and width in $x$ is inverse to width in $k$ (the uncertainty relation, with Gaussians the minimum).
  • The diffusion equation decouples in Fourier space, each mode decaying as $e^{-Dk^2t}$, so short wavelengths homogenise fastest and the solution from a point source is a Gaussian of variance $2Dt$.
  • The discrete Fourier transform computed by the FFT in $O(N\log N)$ underlies spectral solvers, plane-wave DFT and Ewald summation; sampling at spacing $\Delta$ resolves wavenumbers only up to $\pi/\Delta$, and faster oscillations alias to false lower frequencies.
  • Diffraction is the Fourier transform of the scattering density: a lattice transforms into a reciprocal lattice, a finite crystal gives peaks of width $\propto1/L$ (Scherrer), and Bloch's theorem expands electron states in plane waves $e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{r}}$.

Find the complex Fourier coefficients of $f(x)=\cos^2x$ (period $\pi$ or $2\pi$) and verify Parseval.

Solution

$\cos^2x=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2x=\tfrac12+\tfrac14e^{2ix}+\tfrac14e^{-2ix}$, so $c_0=\tfrac12$, $c_{\pm2}=\tfrac14$. Mean square: $\overline{\cos^4x}=\tfrac38$. Sum of $|c_n|^2=\tfrac14+\tfrac1{16}+\tfrac1{16}=\tfrac38$ ✓.

Quiz 3Smoothness

A periodic function is continuous but its first derivative has jumps. How do the Fourier coefficients decay? Does the Gibbs phenomenon occur?

Solution

Like $1/n^2$ (the triangle-wave case). There is no Gibbs overshoot, because the function itself has no jump; the partial sums converge uniformly.

Quiz 4Fourier transform

A Gaussian $e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ has $\sigma=2$ nm. What is the width of its transform, and what happens if $\sigma$ is halved?

Solution

$F(k)\propto e^{-\sigma^2k^2/2}$ is a Gaussian with standard deviation $1/\sigma=0.5\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. Halving $\sigma$ to $1$ nm doubles it to $1\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$: narrow in $x$, broad in $k$.

Quiz 5Sampling

A $70$ Hz tone is sampled at $100$ Hz for $N=2000$ points. Which frequency appears, and what is the frequency resolution?

Solution

Nyquist is $50$ Hz, so the tone aliases to $|70-100|=30$ Hz. The resolution is $f_s/N=0.05$ Hz.

Quiz 6Diffraction

Cu K$\alpha$ ($0.15406$ nm) diffracts from the (111) planes of aluminium, $d=0.2338$ nm. At what $2\theta$ is the peak?

Solution

$\sin\theta=0.15406/(2\times0.2338)=0.3295$, $\theta=19.24^\circ$, $2\theta=38.47^\circ$ (the known Al(111) position).
