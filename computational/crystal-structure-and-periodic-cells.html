Crystal structures, supercells and periodic boundaries

Almost every simulation of a solid begins with a crystal structure, and every diffraction pattern, band structure and phonon spectrum is plotted in terms of its reciprocal lattice. This unit is the language of that world: how to describe a periodic arrangement of atoms exactly, how to classify it by its symmetry, how to read the structure from a diffraction experiment, and how a periodic simulation cell relates to the real crystal. It continues the introduction of MSE 101 with the mathematics that is needed to compute with crystals, and every number was checked with the code of LAB 201.

Lecture 1. Lattices, bases and cells#

An ideal crystal is invariant under translation by any vector $\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3$ with integers $n_i$. The set of all such points is the Bravais lattice, and $\mathbf a_1$, $\mathbf a_2$, $\mathbf a_3$ are primitive vectors: the parallelepiped they span is a primitive cell, the smallest volume that, repeated, fills space; it contains exactly one lattice point. The crystal is the lattice together with the basis: the atoms placed at the same positions $\mathbf r_j$ relative to every lattice point. In short, crystal = lattice + basis. The volume of the primitive cell is $$V=|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)|=|\det\mathbf A|,\qquad\mathbf A=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf a_1\\\mathbf a_2\\\mathbf a_3\end{pmatrix}\text{ (rows)}.$$ The choice of primitive vectors is not unique (any other set related by an integer matrix of determinant $\pm1$ describes the same lattice), and neither is the shape of the cell; a unique choice is the Wigner–Seitz cell, the region closer to one lattice point than to any other, which has the full symmetry of the lattice. For convenience one often uses a larger conventional cell that shows the symmetry in its shape (a cube for fcc and bcc), containing $2$ or $4$ lattice points.

An atom position is written in fractional (scaled) coordinates $(s_1,s_2,s_3)$ with $$\mathbf r=s_1\mathbf a_1+s_2\mathbf a_2+s_3\mathbf a_3,$$ each between $0$ and $1$ inside the cell; with the matrix $\mathbf A$ above, $\mathbf r=\mathbf s\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf s=\mathbf r\mathbf A^{-1}$ (row vectors). Most structure files (CIF, POSCAR) and all electronic-structure codes use fractional coordinates, because they do not change when the cell is strained.

Worked example 1.1: the primitive cell of fcc

The fcc lattice with cubic lattice constant $a$ has the primitive vectors $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$. Compute the volume of the primitive cell for copper ($a=0.3615$ nm), the number of atoms per conventional cell, and the fractional coordinates of the atom at the centre of the face $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)a$.

$\det\mathbf A=(a/2)^3\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix}=(a/2)^3\times2=a^3/4$ (the determinant of the matrix is $2$). For copper $V=0.3615^3/4=0.01181$ nm$^3$ $=11.81$ Å$^3$ per atom, and the conventional cell is four times larger, $a^3=47.24$ Å$^3$, containing $4$ atoms. The face-centre atom: solve $\mathbf s\mathbf A=\mathbf r$, i.e. $\mathbf s=\mathbf r\mathbf A^{-1}$ with $\mathbf r=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)a$; since $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$ we have $\mathbf r=\mathbf a_3$ and $\mathbf s=(0,0,1)$, which is lattice-equivalent to the origin: the face-centre atom is a lattice point of the primitive description. An atom at $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)a$ is not a second atom of the basis but another lattice point: the fcc crystal has a one-atom basis. (In ASE, bulk("Cu", "fcc", a=3.615) returns the one-atom primitive cell and cubic=True the four-atom conventional cell: LAB 201, Step 2.)

The seven crystal systems and fourteen Bravais lattices

The lattice has a point-group symmetry (rotations and reflections that map it onto itself): there are only $7$ possibilities for the shape of the cell, the crystal systems, defined by the lattice parameters $a$, $b$, $c$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, and if one allows centred cells (additional lattice points at the body centre I, face centres F, the centre of one pair of faces A/B/C, or the rhombohedral R setting) one finds $14$ Bravais lattices (Bravais, 1848).

The volume of a general cell is $$V=abc\sqrt{1-\cos^2\alpha-\cos^2\beta-\cos^2\gamma+2\cos\alpha\cos\beta\cos\gamma},$$ which for a hexagonal cell reduces to $V=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2a^2c$, which gives $46.47$ Å$^3$ for magnesium ($a=3.209$, $c=5.211$ Å; two atoms per cell). Note that the face-centred cubic lattice can also be described as a simple cubic lattice with a four-point basis, and the body-centred one as simple cubic with a two-point basis: the conventional cubic cell is a convenient but redundant description, and the true (smallest) lattice cell is the primitive rhombohedron of the fcc lattice, with angle $60^\circ$ between the vectors, containing one lattice point.

Hexagonal close-packed is not a Bravais lattice

The hcp structure has a hexagonal lattice with a two-atom basis, at $(0,0,0)$ and $(\tfrac23,\tfrac13,\tfrac12)$ in fractional coordinates, because the atoms of the second layer are not related to those of the first by a lattice translation (the stacking is $ABAB$). The ideal axial ratio is $c/a=\sqrt{8/3}=1.633$ for touching spheres; real metals deviate: Mg $1.624$, Zn $1.856$, Ti $1.588$, Cd $1.886$. The deviation tells which interatomic forces dominate: when $c/a<1.633$ the atoms in the basal plane are further apart than the out-of-plane neighbours (the twelve neighbours are no longer at the same distance: for Mg, $3.209$ Å in the plane and $3.197$ Å out of the plane, found in LAB 201).

Lecture 2. Symmetry: point groups and space groups#

A symmetry operation maps the crystal onto itself. The operations that leave a point fixed are rotations ($n=1, 2, 3, 4, 6$ only: a lattice cannot have a five-fold axis because pentagons do not tile the plane, which is why quasicrystals are not periodic), reflections in mirror planes, inversion through a point, and rotoinversions. The set of them for a crystal is its point group; there are $32$ crystallographic point groups. Beyond these, a crystal has operations that include a translation by a fraction of a lattice vector: a screw axis (rotation plus translation along the axis) and a glide plane (reflection plus translation parallel to the plane). The complete set is the space group; there are exactly $230$ (Fedorov, Schönflies, Barlow, 1890s). A space group is named by the Hermann–Mauguin symbol, which gives the lattice type (P, I, F, C, R) and the main symmetry elements along the principal directions: $Fm\bar3m$ (No. 225) is a face-centred cubic lattice with mirror planes ($m$) and a three-fold rotoinversion axis ($\bar3$) along $\langle111\rangle$; $Fd\bar3m$ (227) has a diamond glide ($d$); $P6_3/mmc$ (194) has a $6_3$ screw axis.

The atoms of a crystal that are equivalent under the symmetry form an orbit, and the position of the orbit is given by its Wyckoff letter: the multiplicity (number of equivalent atoms in the cell) and the site symmetry. A structure is specified completely by the space group, the lattice parameters and the coordinates of one atom of each orbit (the asymmetric unit). This is how a CIF file describes a structure: for NaCl, space group $Fm\bar3m$, $a=5.640$ Å, Na at $4a$ $(0,0,0)$ and Cl at $4b$ $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$. spglib returns exactly this information; the table below was computed for ten structures built with ASE:

Read the table. Copper, NaCl and fluorite all have the same space group $Fm\bar3m$ (225) but different atom positions: the number of Wyckoff positions occupied tells the difference (“aaaa”: one orbit of four atoms in copper; “abababab”: two orbits in NaCl, $4a$ for Na and $4b$ for Cl; fluorite has Ca on $4a$ and F on $8c$). The number of operations ($192$ for $Fm\bar3m$) is the order of the point group ($48$) times the number of lattice translations in the conventional cell ($4$ for F). The same structure type can occur in different space groups: diamond cubic silicon is $Fd\bar3m$ (227) but the zincblende ZnS, with the same atom arrangement but two kinds of atoms, has lost the inversion centre and is $F\bar43m$ (216): lower symmetry because the two sites are no longer equivalent. Quartz ($P3_121$, No. 152) has only $6$ operations and no centre of symmetry; consequently it is piezoelectric, whereas $Fm\bar3m$ crystals cannot be: physical properties are constrained by the symmetry (MATH 302: a crystal with a centre of symmetry has no piezoelectric tensor; cubic crystals have three independent elastic constants).

Symmetry in simulations

Electronic-structure codes use the space group to reduce the work: the forces and the electron density have the symmetry of the crystal, so the Brillouin-zone sum can be restricted to its irreducible part (for fcc, $1/48$ of the zone). A structure that is slightly distorted from the ideal one by noise loses the symmetry in the code and costs up to $48$ times more; the tolerance with which symmetry is detected (symprec) therefore matters (LAB 201, Step 4: with $0.0005$ Å of noise, copper is $Fm\bar3m$ at a tolerance of $0.1$ Å but $P1$ at $0.001$ Å). The opposite happens in a study of a phase transition: a symmetry-breaking distortion must be allowed to appear, so the calculation is started from a slightly distorted structure.

Worked example 2.1: counting atoms from Wyckoff multiplicities

The fluorite structure of CaF$_2$ ($Fm\bar3m$) has Ca at the Wyckoff position $4a$ and F at $8c$. How many atoms are in the conventional cell, what is the formula unit per cell, and what is the density for $a=5.463$ Å? (Atomic masses: Ca $40.078$, F $18.998$.)

The multiplicities are $4$ and $8$: $4$ Ca and $8$ F, so $12$ atoms and $4$ formula units of CaF$_2$ per cell, in agreement with the Wyckoff string “aaaacccccccc” of the table. The mass of the cell is $4(40.078+2\times18.998)=312.3$ u. With $V=(5.463\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm})^3=1.630\times10^{-22}$ cm$^3$: $\rho=312.3/(6.022\times10^{23}\times1.630\times10^{-22})=3.18$ g/cm$^3$, equal to the measured density of fluorite ($3.18$). Each Ca is surrounded by $8$ F (cube) and each F by $4$ Ca (tetrahedron): coordination $8{:}4$.

Lecture 3. Common crystal structures and why atoms choose them#

Why does NaCl have the rocksalt structure and CsCl another one? In metals the bonding is non-directional and atoms pack as densely as possible: fcc, hcp ($74\%$) or, with a more open packing but a favourable electronic structure, bcc ($68\%$). In covalent crystals the structure follows the number and the geometry of the bonds: four tetrahedral $sp^3$ bonds give the diamond cubic structure (C, Si, Ge: $34\%$ packing), three $sp^2$ bonds give the layers of graphite. In ionic crystals the cations and anions arrange to maximise the electrostatic attraction of unlike ions while avoiding contact between like ions, subject to charge neutrality (Pauling’s rules). The first rule: each cation is surrounded by anions at the corners of a polyhedron, and the coordination number is fixed by the radius ratio $r_c/r_a$: the cation should touch all its anion neighbours, and the neighbours should not touch each other.

The limits follow from geometry: for octahedral coordination the cation touches the six anions, which in the plane form a square of side $2r_a$ with diagonal $2(r_a+r_c)$ along which the anions would just touch, so $(r_a+r_c)=\sqrt2r_a$ and $r_c/r_a=\sqrt2-1=0.414$ (below this, the anions touch each other and the cation rattles: the structure becomes unstable and the coordination drops to four). For the cube, the body diagonal is $2(r_a+r_c)=\sqrt3\cdot2r_a$ and $r_c/r_a=\sqrt3-1=0.732$. The rule is only a first guide (covalent character and polarisation matter): with the Shannon radii, Na$^+$ ($1.02$ Å, six-coordinate) and Cl$^-$ ($1.81$ Å) give $0.564$, octahedral (rocksalt); Cs$^+$ ($1.67$ Å) gives $0.923$, cubic (CsCl); and Zn$^{2+}$ ($0.60$ Å, four-coordinate) and S$^{2-}$ ($1.84$ Å) give $0.326$, tetrahedral (zincblende).

Lecture 4. Planes, directions and the reciprocal lattice#

A family of parallel lattice planes with Miller indices $(hkl)$ is described by its normal. The reciprocal lattice encodes all such families at once. Given the primitive vectors $\mathbf a_i$ define $\mathbf b_i$ by $$\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij},\qquad\mathbf b_1=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)}\ (\text{and cyclic}).$$ In matrix form, with the $\mathbf a_i$ as the rows of $\mathbf A$, the $\mathbf b_i$ are the rows of $2\pi(\mathbf A^{-1})^\mathrm T$. A vector of the reciprocal lattice is $\mathbf G_{hkl}=h\mathbf b_1+k\mathbf b_2+l\mathbf b_3$. Three facts connect it to the planes of Lecture 2 of MSE 101:

  1. $\mathbf G_{hkl}$ is perpendicular to the planes $(hkl)$;
  2. the interplanar spacing is $d_{hkl}=2\pi/|\mathbf G_{hkl}|$ (for cubic lattices $d=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$);
  3. the plane wave $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}$ has the periodicity of the lattice: every periodic function (the electron density, the potential) is a Fourier series in the $\mathbf G$ (MATH 205), which is the reason why the reciprocal lattice is the natural language of diffraction and of band theory (PHYS 302).

Proof of (2). The plane $(hkl)$ intercepts the axes at $\mathbf a_1/h$, $\mathbf a_2/k$, $\mathbf a_3/l$; the distance from the origin to the plane is the projection of $\mathbf a_1/h$ on the unit normal $\hat{\mathbf n}=\mathbf G/|\mathbf G|$: $d=\dfrac{\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf G}{h|\mathbf G|}=\dfrac{2\pi}{|\mathbf G|}$, since $\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf G=2\pi h$. $\square$

The reciprocal lattice of fcc (cube of side $a$) is a bcc lattice (with cube side $4\pi/a$) and that of bcc is fcc: with $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$ etc., the reciprocal vectors are $\mathbf b_1=\tfrac{2\pi}a(-1,1,1)$, $\mathbf b_2=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,-1,1)$ and $\mathbf b_3=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,1,-1)$ (checked numerically for $a=4.05$ Å). The volume of the reciprocal primitive cell is $(2\pi)^3/V$; for the fcc primitive cell with $V=a^3/4$ it is $4(2\pi/a)^3$.

Diffraction

X-rays, electrons and neutrons are scattered by a crystal; the wavelets from the planes interfere constructively when the path difference between neighbouring planes is a whole number of wavelengths. Bragg’s law is $n\lambda=2d\sin\theta$ ($\theta$ the angle between the beam and the planes, so the scattering angle is $2\theta$); equivalently, the von Laue condition says that the change of wave vector on scattering equals a reciprocal-lattice vector, $\mathbf k'-\mathbf k=\mathbf G$ (elastic: $|\mathbf k'|=|\mathbf k|=2\pi/\lambda$; with $|\mathbf G|=2\pi n/d$ this is Bragg’s law). In a powder pattern the crystallites have all orientations and each family $(hkl)$ gives one ring at the angle $2\theta_{hkl}$, with intensity proportional to $|F_{hkl}|^2$, where the structure factor sums the scattering of the atoms in the cell, $$F_{hkl}=\sum_jf_j\,e^{2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)}$$ ($f_j$ the atomic scattering factor, roughly proportional to the atomic number for X-rays; $(x_j,y_j,z_j)$ the fractional coordinates). The lattice type alone gives systematic absences: for a body-centred lattice, $F=0$ unless $h+k+l$ is even; for a face-centred lattice, $F=0$ unless $h,k,l$ are all odd or all even; for the diamond structure the all-even reflections also vanish unless $h+k+l=4n$. The positions of the peaks give the lattice, the intensities the atomic positions.

Worked example 4.1: the X-ray pattern of NaCl

Compute the positions (Cu K$\alpha_1$, $\lambda=1.5406$ Å) and the relative structure factors of the first reflections of NaCl ($a=5.640$ Å). Use $f_\mathrm{Na}=11$ and $f_\mathrm{Cl}=17$ (the atomic numbers, as a crude estimate of the scattering factor).

$d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$ and $2\theta=2\arcsin(\lambda/2d)$: $(111)$: $d=3.256$ Å, $2\theta=27.37^\circ$; $(200)$: $2.820$ Å, $31.70^\circ$; $(220)$: $1.994$ Å, $45.45^\circ$; $(311)$: $1.700$ Å, $53.87^\circ$; $(222)$: $1.628$ Å, $56.47^\circ$; $(400)$: $1.410$ Å, $66.23^\circ$. The mixed reflections $(100)$ and $(110)$ are absent (fcc rule). The structure factor: Na at $(0,0,0)$ and the three face-centring translations, Cl at $(\tfrac12,0,0)$ plus the same translations. For all-even $hkl$ all the Na and the Cl contributions are in phase: $F=4(f_\mathrm{Na}+f_\mathrm{Cl})=112$; for all-odd, the Cl contributions change sign: $F=4(f_\mathrm{Na}-f_\mathrm{Cl})=-24$. The intensity ratio of the all-odd to the all-even reflections is therefore $|F|^2=576$ against $12\,544$, a factor of $22$: the $(111)$ and $(311)$ peaks of NaCl are weak because Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$ scatter almost equally (for KCl, where K$^+$ and Cl$^-$ have the same number of electrons, they vanish altogether and KCl looks simple cubic with $a/2$). This is the kind of information used to locate atoms in an unknown structure.

The Brillouin zone

The Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice is the first Brillouin zone (BZ): the set of wave vectors closer to the origin $\Gamma$ than to any other reciprocal lattice point. A wave of wave vector $\mathbf k$ and one with $\mathbf k+\mathbf G$ are indistinguishable in a crystal (PHYS 302), so all the physics of electrons and phonons can be described by $\mathbf k$ in the first BZ. Its volume is $(2\pi)^3/V$ and the number of allowed $\mathbf k$ points in it equals the number of unit cells $N$ of the crystal. Lecture 5. Non-cubic lattices and interplanar spacings#

The spacing formulae of the cubic lattice generalise through the reciprocal lattice: for a hexagonal lattice (Miller indices $(hkl)$ with the three-index convention), $$\frac1{d_{hkl}^2}=\frac43\,\frac{h^2+hk+k^2}{a^2}+\frac{l^2}{c^2},$$ for a tetragonal lattice $\dfrac1{d^2}=\dfrac{h^2+k^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{l^2}{c^2}$, and for an orthorhombic one $\dfrac1{d^2}=\dfrac{h^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{k^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{l^2}{c^2}$. In the hexagonal system a four-index notation $(hkil)$ with $i=-(h+k)$ is also used to make the symmetry visible: the three equivalent directions in the basal plane are $[2\bar1\bar10]$, $[\bar12\bar10]$ and $[\bar1\bar120]$, which have the same indices after permutation of the first three entries.

Worked example 5.1: the X-ray pattern of magnesium

Magnesium is hcp with $a=3.209$ Å and $c=5.211$ Å. Compute the spacings and the diffraction angles ($\lambda=1.5406$ Å) of the reflections $(100)$, $(002)$, $(101)$, $(102)$ and $(110)$.

Using the hexagonal formula: $(100)$: $1/d^2=\tfrac43/a^2=0.12948$, $d=2.779$ Å, $2\theta=32.18^\circ$. $(002)$: $d=c/2=2.606$ Å, $2\theta=34.39^\circ$. $(101)$: $1/d^2=0.12948+1/c^2=0.16631$, $d=2.452$ Å, $2\theta=36.62^\circ$. $(102)$: $1/d^2=0.12948+4/c^2=0.27680$, $d=1.901$ Å, $2\theta=47.81^\circ$. $(110)$: $1/d^2=\tfrac43\cdot3/a^2=0.38845$, $d=1.605$ Å, $2\theta=57.38^\circ$. Lecture 6. Periodic cells, supercells and k-point sampling in simulations#

A real crystal has $10^{23}$ atoms; a simulation can handle $10^2$–$10^6$. Periodic boundary conditions solve this: the simulation cell is repeated infinitely in all directions, so that an atom leaving the cell on one side re-enters on the opposite side, and the cell represents a bulk crystal (and a bulk liquid: the box of COMP 102). The properties of the infinite periodic system follow from the cell, but not all of the properties of the real crystal, in three ways.

  • Supercells for defects. A single vacancy, an impurity or a dislocation breaks the periodicity. It is modelled in a supercell, an $n_1\times n_2\times n_3$ repetition of the primitive or conventional cell with one defect, which is then repeated periodically: the defect interacts with its own images. The fraction of vacancies is $1/N$, with $N=4n^3$ sites for a cubic fcc supercell: $32$ sites ($3.1\%$) for $n=2$, $108$ ($0.93\%$), $256$ ($0.39\%$), $500$ ($0.2\%$), $864$ ($0.12\%$). The vacancy formation energy of copper converged at $108$ atoms with a short-range potential in LAB 201 but would need several hundred atoms with the long-range elastic interaction of a DFT calculation: the supercell size is a convergence parameter, tested as in COMP 102 for the time step.
  • Commensurability. A periodic cell can only contain periodic structures whose period fits in the cell: a $2\times2\times2$ supercell of fcc describes phonons with $\mathbf q$ at X and $\Gamma$ but not at a general point (LAB 301), and a cell for an antiferromagnetic order or a charge-density wave must contain at least one period of the ordering.
  • Shape and boundary. Surfaces are modelled with a slab (periodic in $x$ and $y$, with vacuum in $z$; LAB 201, Step 7). Periodic cells cannot represent a long-range-ordered stress field or an electric field without special treatment; and a cell that is too small (a few atoms across) constrains correlations to the box size: finite-size effects (the diffusion coefficient $9\%$ lower for $108$ atoms in COMP 102).

k-point sampling

For a periodic cell, band theory expresses quantities (the total energy, the electron density) as integrals over the Brillouin zone of the cell, approximated by a sum over a finite k-point mesh, usually a regular $m_1\times m_2\times m_3$ grid (Monkhorst–Pack). Two relations are essential: (1) the larger the real-space cell, the smaller its Brillouin zone and the fewer k points are needed: a supercell with $8$ primitive cells ($2\times2\times2$) has a BZ $8$ times smaller, and a $4\times4\times4$ mesh on the primitive cell corresponds to a $2\times2\times2$ mesh on that supercell (the same sampling). (2) A $m\times m\times m$ mesh on the primitive cell is equivalent to a $\Gamma$-point-only calculation in an $m\times m\times m$ supercell: the $\Gamma$-point calculation of a $432$-atom supercell of silicon ($6\times6\times6$ cells of $2$ atoms) has the same sampling as a $6\times6\times6$ k mesh on the two-atom primitive cell, at a cost that is much higher (the cost of DFT grows as $N^3$ with the number of atoms). Insulators and semiconductors converge quickly with the mesh, because the occupied bands are smooth and filled, while metals need many more k points because the Fermi surface cuts through the zone and needs smearing. In LAB 501 the total energy of silicon changed by $1274$, $284$, $64$ and $0$ meV/atom (relative to the $5\times5\times5$ mesh) for meshes $2^3$, $3^3$, $4^3$ and $5^3$, showing the exponential convergence that is typical of insulators; always test the convergence of the quantity you care about, which for a difference of energies (a lattice constant, a defect energy) converges faster than the absolute energy.

What a CIF does not give you

  • Partial occupancy and disorder. In a solid solution such as Fe$_{0.5}$Ni$_{0.5}$ the CIF lists one site with an occupancy of $0.5$ for each element: the average structure. A simulation needs actual atoms, so an ordered configuration must be chosen. For a $2\times2\times2$ fcc supercell ($32$ sites) with $16$ Ni atoms there are $\binom{32}{16}=6.0\times10^8$ arrangements; a special quasirandom structure (SQS) is one arrangement chosen so that its short-range correlation functions match those of a truly random alloy, giving an accurate energy with a small cell. A virtual-crystal approximation (averaged pseudopotentials) is another route for weakly different atoms.
  • Missing light atoms. Hydrogen atoms are almost invisible to X-rays and are often absent from CIFs of hydrates, organic and hydride structures; a calculation on such a structure is of a different compound. Check the stoichiometry against the formula.
  • Temperature and thermal parameters. The CIF structure is measured at a given temperature (often $300$ K); a DFT calculation relaxes the structure at $0$ K, so the lattice constants differ by $0.1$–$1\%$ for the thermal expansion and by up to $2\%$ for the functional (CMS 501).
  • Errors. CIFs from the literature contain errors in space-group settings, unit-cell parameters and symmetry-equivalent atoms that coincide ($0$ distance) or overlap: a check of the shortest distance catches many.

Polymorphism, disorder and amorphous solids

The same composition can crystallise in different structures (polymorphs) that differ in energy by only a few meV per atom or by tenths of an eV: carbon as diamond ($Fd\bar3m$) or graphite ($P6_3/mmc$); SiO$_2$ as $\alpha$-quartz ($P3_121$, below $573{}^\circ$C), $\beta$-quartz, cristobalite, and glass; TiO$_2$ as rutile, anatase and brookite; iron as bcc ($\alpha$), fcc ($\gamma$) and hcp ($\varepsilon$, at high pressure). A phase transition is often a loss of symmetry: BaTiO$_3$ is cubic perovskite ($Pm\bar3m$) above $393$ K and distorts to tetragonal ($P4mm$), orthorhombic and rhombohedral structures on cooling, each with a lower symmetry and a spontaneous polarisation (ferroelectricity, allowed only in structures without a centre of symmetry). In an amorphous solid (a glass) the long-range order is absent: the diffraction pattern has no sharp peaks, only broad humps, and the structure is described statistically by the radial distribution function $g(r)$ (COMP 102), which shows well-defined first neighbours (a rigid tetrahedron in SiO$_2$) and a decaying oscillation instead of the sharp lines of a crystal. The first peaks of $g(r)$ of a dense liquid and a glass are similar; the difference is the absence of flow.

Key takeaways
  • A crystal is a lattice (three translation vectors a1, a2, a3, with cell volume V = |a1 . (a2 x a3)|) plus a basis of atoms; 14 Bravais lattices in 7 crystal systems, 32 point groups and 230 space groups classify all crystals, and spglib finds the space group of a structure (Fm-3m 225 for fcc copper and rocksalt, Fd-3m 227 for diamond, P6_3/mmc 194 for hcp, Pm-3m 221 for CsCl and perovskite).
  • Common ionic structures follow from the radius ratio r(cation)/r(anion): NaCl (0.56, coordination 6), CsCl (0.92, coordination 8), zincblende (about 0.33, coordination 4); density follows from the cell, for example NaCl 2.164 g/cm3 (a = 5.640 angstrom, 4 formula units), CsCl 3.989, SrTiO3 5.117.
  • Diffraction measures the reciprocal lattice: Bragg's law lambda = 2 d sin(theta) gives for NaCl with Cu K-alpha peaks at 27.4, 31.7, 45.5 and 53.9 degrees for (111), (200), (220) and (311); the structure factor removes the mixed-parity reflections of the fcc lattice and makes the all-odd ones weak (|F|^2 = 576 against 12 544 for all-even, since f(Cl) - f(Na) is small compared with the sum).
  • The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner-Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice, with volume (2 pi)^3/V; for fcc it is a truncated octahedron with the high-symmetry points Gamma, X (1,0,0), L (1/2,1/2,1/2), K and W in units of 2 pi/a, the points along which band structures and phonons are plotted.
  • Simulations use periodic cells: a supercell of n x n x n conventional fcc cells has 4 n^3 atoms (32, 108, 256 and 500 for n = 2 to 5), so one vacancy is 3.1 per cent to 0.2 per cent of the sites; and a k-point mesh of m x m x m for the primitive cell is equivalent to a Gamma-point calculation in an m x m x m supercell.

(b) With the cost $\propto N^3$: the $\Gamma$-only $128$-atom calculation is $(128/2)^3=64^3=2.6\times10^5$ times as costly as one k point of the primitive cell, while the mesh needs about $64$ k points (fewer after symmetry): the k-point mesh is cheaper by a factor of $\sim4000$. This is why a primitive cell with a k mesh is always preferred to a large cell at $\Gamma$, unless the cell must be large for another reason. (c) In an insulator or semiconductor all occupied bands are full and smooth functions of $\mathbf k$, so the integral over the zone converges exponentially with the number of points (the silicon energies of LAB 501: $1274$, $284$, $64$, $0$ meV/atom for meshes $2$ to $5$); in a metal the occupation changes abruptly at the Fermi surface, the integrand is discontinuous, and the convergence is much slower (algebraic), requiring smearing and meshes of $20\times20\times20$ or more.

(a) $4\times4\times4$ supercell, $128$ atoms (b) $\sim4000\times$ more expensive (c) metals have a Fermi surface

Problem 10Core

(a) For a $4\times4\times4$ conventional supercell of fcc copper with one vacancy, give the number of atoms, the vacancy fraction and the nearest distance between periodic images. (b) A calculation gives a vacancy energy that converges at $E_f=1.30\pm0.01$ eV for supercells of $108$ atoms and above: which supercell would you use and why? (c) Why does a vacancy in a $32$-atom supercell have a different energy from one in a $108$-atom supercell?

Hint

$N_\mathrm{perfect}=4n^3$ and the vacancy cell has $N-1$ atoms.

Full solution

(a) The perfect supercell has $4\times4^3=256$ atoms and the cell with the vacancy $255$; the vacancy fraction is $1/256=0.39\%$; the images are $4a=14.46$ Å apart. (b) The $108$-atom cell ($n=3$): it is the smallest in the converged range, so it gives the result at a minimum cost (the calculation time grows as $N^3$ for plane-wave DFT, i.e. $(256/108)^3=13$ times for the larger cell); test the convergence with one larger cell to confirm. (c) In the small cell each vacancy interacts with its $6$ nearest images, only $7.2$ Å ($2a$) away: through the elastic strain field of the relaxation (the neighbouring atoms move toward the vacancy) and, in a metal, through the Friedel oscillations of the electron density; the energy depends on the cell size until the images are far enough that the interaction is negligible compared with the required accuracy.

(a) $256$, $0.39\%$, $14.46$ Å (b) $n=3$ (108 atoms), verified with $n=4$ (c) interaction of the vacancy with its images

Problem 11Challenge

Zincblende ZnS has $192/2=96$ symmetry operations ($F\bar43m$) while diamond silicon has $192$ ($Fd\bar3m$). (a) Which operation is lost? (b) What physical properties become allowed for GaAs (zincblende) that are forbidden for Si? (c) Why is it important in a DFT calculation not to symmetrise the structure wrongly?

Hint

Diamond has an inversion centre at the midpoint of the bond, zincblende does not.

Full solution

(a) In diamond, the midpoint between two neighbouring atoms is an inversion centre; in zincblende the two sites are occupied by different atoms, so the inversion that exchanges them is lost. The point group falls from $m\bar3m$ (order $48$) to $\bar43m$ (order $24$); with the $4$ centring translations, $192\to96$ operations. (b) A crystal without a centre of symmetry can have a non-zero piezoelectric tensor, second-order nonlinear optical susceptibility (second-harmonic generation) and, in the polar subgroups, pyroelectricity. GaAs is piezoelectric (and has a nonlinear optical response), Si is not; the reduction of symmetry also splits the degeneracies of the electronic bands (spin–orbit effects). Si, with the inversion, has an infrared-inactive optical phonon at $\Gamma$ (no dipole), while the optical phonon of GaAs is infrared active and splits into a longitudinal and a transverse branch (LO–TO splitting). (c) The symmetry operations determine the irreducible k-point set and the symmetrisation of forces and densities: if the symmetry that is imposed (found with too large a tolerance) is higher than the true one, the calculation silently imposes a wrong constraint; if a distortion that lowers the symmetry (a phase transition, a Jahn–Teller distortion) is the physics of interest, a symmetrised starting structure can never find it (the state stays on the saddle point). Hence the tolerance of symprec must match the precision of the structure and the possibility of symmetry breaking must be tested by starting from a perturbed structure.

(a) inversion (b) piezoelectricity, second-harmonic generation, LO–TO splitting (c) wrong symmetry gives wrong constraints

Problem 12Challenge

A CIF for a Ni$_{0.5}$Fe$_{0.5}$ solid solution (fcc, $a=3.58$ Å) lists one site with $50\%$ occupancy of each element. (a) How many atoms and how many different arrangements are there in a $2\times2\times2$ conventional supercell with $16$ Ni? (b) Why is a single random arrangement not enough, and what is an SQS? (c) What property of the CIF should you check against the supercell you build?

Hint

$\binom{32}{16}=\dfrac{32!}{16!\,16!}$.

Full solution

(a) $4\times8=32$ atoms; the number of arrangements is $\binom{32}{16}=601\,080\,390$ (about $6\times10^8$; many are equivalent by symmetry, but a large number remain). (b) A single random arrangement has fluctuations in the local environments (the number of Ni neighbours of each atom varies by chance), so its energy differs from the ensemble average of the random alloy by an amount of order $1/\sqrt N$, large for $32$ atoms; one needs the average over many arrangements, or one arrangement chosen to reproduce the pair and higher correlation functions of the infinite random alloy up to some distance: a special quasirandom structure, whose energy is close to the average with a single small cell. (c) The composition (the number of Ni and Fe atoms in the supercell must reproduce the occupancy), the density from the average lattice parameter, and that the space group of the supercell (which will be low, P1 or a subgroup of $Fm\bar3m$) is not used to symmetrise it back into the average structure. Magnetism adds a further choice: the Fe–Ni alloy is ferromagnetic and the initial moments must be set.

(a) $32$ atoms; $6.0\times10^8$ arrangements (b) fluctuations; SQS matches correlation functions (c) composition, density, symmetry, magnetic moments

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Basic

What is the difference between a lattice and a crystal structure? Give an example of a crystal that is not a Bravais lattice.

Solution

The lattice is a set of points generated by three translation vectors; the crystal structure is the lattice plus a basis of atoms attached to each lattice point. hcp is not a Bravais lattice: it is a hexagonal lattice with a two-atom basis. Diamond is an fcc lattice with a two-atom basis; NaCl is an fcc lattice with a basis of one Na and one Cl.

Quiz 2Basic

How many atoms are in the conventional unit cell of rocksalt NaCl and what are their Wyckoff positions?

Solution

Eight: four Na at $4a$ $(0,0,0)$ and four Cl at $4b$ $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, space group $Fm\bar3m$ (No. 225); four formula units per cell.

Quiz 3Core

State Bragg’s law and the von Laue condition, and show that they say the same thing.

Solution

Bragg: $\lambda=2d\sin\theta$. Laue: $\mathbf k^{\prime}-\mathbf k=\mathbf G$ with $|\mathbf k^{\prime}|=|\mathbf k|=2\pi/\lambda$ for elastic scattering. The magnitude of $\mathbf k^{\prime}-\mathbf k$ is $2|\mathbf k|\sin\theta=4\pi\sin\theta/\lambda$ (for scattering angle $2\theta$), and $|\mathbf G|=2\pi/d$ for the first-order reflection from planes of spacing $d$; equating them gives $\lambda=2d\sin\theta$.

Quiz 4Core

Which reflections are absent in an fcc crystal and in a bcc crystal? Why does the $(111)$ reflection of NaCl have a small intensity?

Solution

fcc: reflections with mixed even and odd indices vanish (only all-odd or all-even are present); bcc: $h+k+l$ must be even. In NaCl, the all-odd reflections have $F=4(f_\mathrm{Na}-f_\mathrm{Cl})$, small because Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$ scatter X-rays similarly (here $-24$, against $112$ for the all-even ones), so $|F|^2$ is $22$ times smaller.

Quiz 5Core

How is a $4\times4\times4$ k-point mesh on the primitive cell related to a supercell calculation, and why is the mesh much cheaper?

Solution

It samples the same wave vectors as a $\Gamma$-point calculation on a $4\times4\times4$ supercell ($64$ times as many atoms). The cost of DFT grows as $N^3$ with the number of atoms, so one large cell costs $\sim64^3$ times a small cell, whereas the mesh needs only $\sim64$ (fewer with symmetry) calculations of the small cell.

Quiz 6Challenge

Why can a structure from a CIF file give a wrong simulation even if the file is read without errors? List three checks.

Solution

The file can have missing atoms (hydrogen), partial occupancies that need an ordering model, wrong symmetry settings or coordinates, a temperature-dependent lattice constant, or a polymorph different from the one intended. Checks: the formula and the number of atoms against the expected stoichiometry; the density against the measured one; the shortest interatomic distance against the sum of radii (no overlaps); the space group found by spglib against the one in the file; and the coordination numbers of the atoms.
