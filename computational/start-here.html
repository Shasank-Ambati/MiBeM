1. What this subject is#

Everything around you is a material: the steel in a bridge, the glass of a window, the plastic of a bottle, the silicon in a phone. What a material does (how strong it is, whether it conducts electricity, how it reacts to heat or to water) depends on what it is made of and how its tiny parts, its atoms, are arranged.

People who work on materials want to answer questions such as: Which mix of metals will make a lighter aircraft part that is still strong? Why does this steel crack in the cold? How can a battery material store more energy? The traditional way to find out is to make samples and test them. That is slow, expensive and sometimes impossible: you cannot easily look at what individual atoms are doing, and you cannot afford to melt and test a million recipes.

Computational materials science uses computers to help. We write down rules for how atoms, electrons, heat and forces behave, and then we let the computer work out what follows. The result is a prediction: how a crystal will deform, which structure is most stable, how fast atoms will move at a given temperature. Good predictions tell researchers which experiments are worth doing, explain results that are hard to see, and sometimes replace experiments that are impossible.

2. Models and simulations#

Two words appear everywhere on this site, so let us pin them down with a cup of tea.

A model is a simplified description of something that keeps the parts that matter and ignores the rest. For a cooling cup of tea, a very simple model says: the tea loses heat at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the room. That one sentence ignores the shape of the cup, the steam, the draught from the window. It is not perfect, but it is useful, and you can test it.

A simulation is the step-by-step calculation of what a model predicts. If the model says how fast the temperature changes, a simulation takes the temperature now, applies the rule for one minute, gets the temperature a minute later, and repeats. A computer can do this millions of times a second.

That is the whole recipe, and it is the same for every simulation in this series: (1) describe the state of the system, (2) write the rule for how it changes, (3) apply the rule over and over. What changes from topic to topic is what the state is (positions of atoms, concentrations, temperatures in a part) and how good the rule is. The last page of the Start here level, Your first simulation, builds one yourself.

There is one more essential step, which is what separates science from guessing: checking. A model is only trustworthy if its predictions agree with measurements, or with a case where the answer is known exactly. You will see this idea on every page.

3. Zooming in on a material#

A steel beam looks smooth and uniform. Zoom in and a different picture appears at each step, and different methods are needed for each.

  • At the scale of electrons (about a tenth of a nanometre, a nanometre being a billionth of a metre) the rules of quantum physics decide how atoms bond.
  • At the scale of atoms (a few nanometres) we can follow groups of atoms moving and vibrating, and watch defects move.
  • At the scale of the microstructure (micrometres, millionths of a metre) we see grains, tiny crystals packed together, and particles of other phases growing and shrinking.
  • At the scale of a component (millimetres to metres) we ask how the whole part heats, bends and survives.

5. How to learn from these pages#

Reading alone is the weakest way to learn. These habits make the time count:

  1. Do the demos. Every page has interactive pieces. Move the sliders before you read the explanation, and predict what will happen first.
  2. Try the questions before opening the answers. Struggling for a minute, even if you get it wrong, helps you remember more than reading the answer straight away.
  3. Keep a notebook. After each page write three sentences in your own words: what it was about, one thing that surprised you, one thing you are still unsure of. This takes five minutes and is the best memory aid there is.
  4. Use the words. Terms with a dotted underline show a short definition when you hover, tap or focus them, and the glossary collects them all. New words are the main barrier, not hard ideas.
  5. Go down a level, not harder. If a page feels like a wall, the right response is to read the page it builds on (shown at the top under “Builds on” and on the topic map), not to push through confused.
  6. Come back. Revisit a page a few days and then a few weeks later and try its questions again. Spaced repetition is how things move into long-term memory.
  7. Do something small yourself. From page 7 on you will run short pieces of code. Typing them in, changing a number and seeing what breaks teaches more than reading them.

Key takeaways
  • Computational materials science uses computers to predict how materials behave, by describing atoms, energy and heat with rules and calculating what follows.
  • A model is a simplified description; a simulation is the step-by-step calculation of what the model predicts, and it must be checked against reality.
  • The site has three levels: Start here (school-level), Background (university-level refresher) and Core topics (the methods).

8. Check your understanding#

In your own words, what is the difference between a model and a simulation?

A model is a simplified description of how something behaves (the rule). A simulation is the calculation that applies the rule step by step to find out what happens. You can have a model without simulating it (for example, solving it with pen and paper), but a simulation always needs a model.

Why can’t we just look at atoms to see what a material is doing?

Atoms are about a tenth of a nanometre across, far smaller than visible light can resolve, and the interesting events (atoms moving, bonds forming) are extremely fast and happen inside the material. Special microscopes can image atoms in thin samples, but not follow everything inside a working component, which is why simulation is so useful.

You find a Core topic page too hard. What should you do?

Look at the “Builds on” list at the top of the page (or the topic map) and read those pages first. The levels are designed so that each page rests on the ones before it, so going down one level is the intended solution, not a failure.

9. Where to go for more#

These are free or widely available and fit this level.

  • Khan Academy, free courses from basic algebra to calculus and introductory physics and chemistry.
  • 3Blue1Brown, “Essence of calculus” and “Essence of linear algebra”, short video series with excellent pictures.
  • MIT OpenCourseWare, free lecture notes and videos, including introductory solid-state chemistry and materials courses.
  • Lee, J. G., Computational Materials Science: An Introduction, CRC Press (an introductory textbook on the same subject).
  • Callister, W. D. and Rethwisch, D. G., Materials Science and Engineering: An Introduction, Wiley (the standard first textbook on materials).
