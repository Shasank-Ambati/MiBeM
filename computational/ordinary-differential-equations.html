Ordinary differential equations

Whenever something changes with time, a differential equation is at work: atoms moving under forces, a concentration relaxing to equilibrium, a phase growing, a temperature settling. Ordinary differential equations (ODEs), those with a single independent variable, are the simplest of these and the foundation for everything that follows: molecular dynamics integrates $3N$ coupled ODEs, kinetic models are systems of ODEs, and every PDE solver eventually reduces to ODEs in time. This course covers the analytic theory and the numerical methods, with emphasis on the questions that matter in simulation: how fast does it decay, is it stable, and what time step may I use?

Lecture 1. Basics: what an ODE is#

An ordinary differential equation of order $n$ relates an unknown function $y(t)$ to its derivatives up to order $n$. It is linear if $y$ and its derivatives appear only to the first power and are not multiplied together, and autonomous if $t$ does not appear explicitly. An initial value problem (IVP) supplies $y,y',\ldots,y^{(n-1)}$ at $t=0$. Any $n$-th order equation is equivalent to a system of $n$ first-order equations (set $y_1=y$, $y_2=y'$, and so on), so the general form to keep in mind is $$\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(\mathbf y,t),\qquad\mathbf y(0)=\mathbf y_0 .$$ For example Newton’s law $m\ddot x=F(x)$ becomes $\dot x=v$, $\dot v=F(x)/m$.

Existence and uniqueness

If $\mathbf f$ and its partial derivatives with respect to $\mathbf y$ are continuous near $(\mathbf y_0,0)$, the IVP has a unique solution for a short time. Without this smoothness, uniqueness can fail: $\dot y=\sqrt{|y|}$, $y(0)=0$ has the solutions $y=0$ and $y=t^2/4$. And solutions need not exist for all time: $\dot y=y^2$, $y(0)=1$ gives $y=1/(1-t)$, which blows up at $t=1$.

Worked example 1.1: verifying and fixing constants

Show that $y=Ae^{-t}+Be^{-2t}$ solves $\ddot y+3\dot y+2y=0$ and find $A,B$ for $y(0)=1$, $\dot y(0)=0$.

Substituting, each exponential gives $r^2+3r+2=(r+1)(r+2)=0$ for $r=-1,-2$, so both terms satisfy the equation, and so does any combination (the equation is linear). The conditions give $A+B=1$ and $-A-2B=0$, so $B=-1$, $A=2$ and $y=2e^{-t}-e^{-2t}$. Check: $\dot y(0)=-2+2=0$ ✓. As a first-order system, $\mathbf y=(y,\dot y)$ obeys $\dot{\mathbf y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{pmatrix}\mathbf y$ whose eigenvalues are the same $-1,-2$.

Lecture 2. First-order equations#

Separable equations#

If $\dot y=g(y)h(t)$, separate and integrate: $\int\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{g(y)}=\int h(t)\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}t$. The most important case in kinetics is the rate law $\dot c=-kc^m$ (reaction order $m$). For $m=1$: $c=c_0e^{-kt}$, with half-life $t_{1/2}=\ln2/k$ independent of $c_0$. For $m=2$: $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}c}{c^2}=-k\,\allowbreak \mathrm{d}t\Rightarrow\dfrac1c=\dfrac1{c_0}+kt$, so $c=\dfrac{c_0}{1+kc_0t}$ and the half-life $t_{1/2}=1/(kc_0)$ depends on $c_0$: that dependence is how reaction orders are determined experimentally.

Worked example 2.1: the Avrami (JMAK) equation

A solid-state transformation nucleates and grows in many places. If the particles did not impinge on each other, the transformed fraction would be the “extended” fraction $X_\mathrm{ex}(t)=(kt)^n$. Account for impingement and find the real fraction $X(t)$.

New growth only converts untransformed material, so the rate of change of the real fraction is the rate of change of the extended fraction multiplied by the untransformed fraction: $\dot X=(1-X)\,\dot X_\mathrm{ex}$. This is separable: $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}X}{1-X}=\mathrm{d}X_\mathrm{ex}$, so $-\ln(1-X)=X_\mathrm{ex}$ (with $X=0$ at $t=0$), i.e. $$X(t)=1-\exp\bigl[-(kt)^n\bigr].$$ The “Avrami plot” of $\ln[-\ln(1-X)]$ against $\ln t$ is a straight line of slope $n$ (between $1$ and $4$, indicating the nucleation and growth mechanism). The resulting S-shaped curve is the standard description of recrystallisation, precipitation and crystallisation kinetics. Problem 3 fits it to data.

Linear first-order equations#

The equation $\dot y+p(t)y=q(t)$ is solved by an integrating factor $\mu(t)=e^{\int p\,\mathrm{d}t}$: multiplying through gives $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mu y)=\mu q$, so $y=\dfrac1\mu\Bigl(\int\mu q\,\mathrm{d}t+C\Bigr)$. For constant coefficients $\dot y+ay=q$ with $a>0$, the solution is a decaying transient plus a steady state: $y=\dfrac qa+\bigl(y_0-\dfrac qa\bigr)e^{-at}$, relaxing to $q/a$ with time constant $\tau=1/a$. This describes Newton cooling ($\dot T=-(T-T_\infty)/\tau$), a rate balance of production and decay, and the relaxation of a concentration toward equilibrium. After $3\tau$ the transient has decayed to $5\%$, after $5\tau$ to $0.7\%$.

Fixed points and stability in one variable#

For an autonomous $\dot y=f(y)$, the fixed points (equilibria) satisfy $f(y^*)=0$. Linearise: with $y=y^*+\delta$, $\dot\delta\approx f'(y^*)\,\delta$, so $\delta\propto e^{f'(y^*)t}$. The fixed point is stable if $f'(y^*)<0$ and unstable if $f'(y^*)>0$, with relaxation time $1/|f'(y^*)|$. The same sign test that identifies a minimum of an energy $U$ (with $f=-U'$ and $f'=-U''$) shows up here: a minimum is a stable fixed point of the gradient flow $\dot y=-U'(y)$. When $f'(y^*)\to0$ the relaxation time diverges: critical slowing down, a hallmark of phase transitions.

In materials science: relaxation of an order parameter

Landau theory writes the free energy of an order parameter $\eta$ as $F=\tfrac12A\eta^2+\tfrac14b\eta^4$ with $A=a(T-T_c)$ and $b>0$. The simplest relaxational dynamics is $\dot\eta=-\Gamma\,\allowbreak \partial F/\partial\eta=-\Gamma(A\eta+b\eta^3)$, the zero-dimensional form of the Allen–Cahn equation of the phase-field method. Above $T_c$ ($A>0$) the only fixed point is $\eta=0$, stable with rate $\Gamma A$. Below $T_c$ ($A<0$), $\eta=0$ becomes unstable and two stable fixed points $\eta=\pm\sqrt{|A|/b}$ appear (a pitchfork bifurcation). The relaxation rate vanishes as $T\to T_c$ from either side, so near the transition ordering becomes slower (Problem 4).

Worked example 2.2: cooling of a quenched part

A steel part at $800^\circ$C is placed in a $25^\circ$C bath with $\dot T=-(T-T_\mathrm{b})/\tau$, $\tau=10$ min. How long until it reaches $100^\circ$C?

With $\theta=T-T_\mathrm b$ the equation is $\dot\theta=-\theta/\tau$, so $\theta=775\,e^{-t/\tau}$ and $T=25+775e^{-t/10}$. Setting $T=100$: $e^{-t/10}=75/775$, $t=10\ln(10.33)=23.4$ min. After one time constant the temperature excess has fallen to $37\%$, after three to $5\%$; the “fast first minutes” of a quench are all in the early part of the exponential. Real quenches also have a temperature-dependent $\tau$ (boiling at high $T$, convection at low $T$), which turns the problem nonlinear and is handled numerically in Lecture 5.

Lecture 3. Linear second-order equations: oscillators#

The equation $a\ddot x+b\dot x+cx=0$ with constant coefficients is solved by trying $x=e^{rt}$, which gives the characteristic equation $ar^2+br+c=0$. With $r_{1,2}$ its roots:

  • distinct real roots: $x=C_1e^{r_1t}+C_2e^{r_2t}$;
  • a repeated root: $x=(C_1+C_2t)e^{rt}$;
  • complex roots $r=\alpha\pm i\beta$: $x=e^{\alpha t}\bigl(C_1\cos\beta t+C_2\sin\beta t\bigr)$.

The general solution has two free constants, fixed by $x(0)$ and $\dot x(0)$. The equation $\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$ is the damped harmonic oscillator ($\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ the natural frequency, $\gamma=\eta/m$ the damping rate). Define the damping ratio $\zeta=\gamma/(2\omega_0)$. Then $$r=-\frac\gamma2\pm\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}4-\omega_0^2}.$$

CaseConditionBehaviour
Underdamped$\zeta<1$ ($\gamma<2\omega_0$)$x=e^{-\gamma t/2}\bigl(A\cos\omega_dt+B\sin\omega_dt\bigr)$ with $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}$: decaying oscillation
Critically damped$\zeta=1$$x=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\omega_0t}$: fastest return without oscillation
Overdamped$\zeta>1$sum of two decaying exponentials, the slow one dominating

The energy of a lightly damped oscillator decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$; the quality factor $Q=\omega_0/\gamma$ is approximately the number of radians of oscillation before the energy falls by $1/e$ (so $Q/\pi$ cycles). High-$Q$ resonators (quartz crystals, nanomechanical cantilevers, phonon modes of a perfect crystal) ring for a long time; internal friction measurements of $Q^{-1}$ detect dislocation motion and point-defect relaxation in metals.

The driven oscillator and resonance#

Add a sinusoidal drive, $\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=F\cos\omega t$. The solution is the sum of the decaying transient above and a steady-state response at the drive frequency, $x_p=X(\omega)\cos(\omega t-\delta)$, with $$X(\omega)=\frac{F}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}},\qquad\tan\delta=\frac{\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}.$$ The amplitude peaks near $\omega=\omega_0$ (exactly at $\omega_r=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2}$), where $X\approx F/(\gamma\omega_0)=Q\times F/\omega_0^2$: the static displacement magnified by $Q$. The width of the peak (full width at half maximum) is about $\gamma$. The phase lags by $\delta=\pi/2$ at resonance. Using complex exponentials, $F\cos\omega t=\operatorname{Re}\,\allowbreak Fe^{i\omega t}$ and $x_p=\operatorname{Re}\,\allowbreak \dfrac{Fe^{i\omega t}}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}$, which is the standard trick. This Lorentzian line shape is the shape of every spectral line broadened by damping: infrared and Raman absorption by lattice vibrations, ferromagnetic resonance, NMR.

Worked example 3.1: an underdamped solution

Solve $\ddot x+4\dot x+13x=0$, $x(0)=1$, $\dot x(0)=0$.

$r^2+4r+13=0$ gives $r=-2\pm3i$, so $x=e^{-2t}(C_1\cos3t+C_2\sin3t)$. $x(0)=1$ gives $C_1=1$; $\dot x(0)=-2C_1+3C_2=0$ gives $C_2=2/3$. The envelope $e^{-2t}$ decays with time constant $\tfrac12$ while the oscillation has angular frequency $3$ (period $2.09$): $\zeta=\gamma/(2\omega_0)=4/(2\sqrt{13})=0.55<1$.

Worked example 3.2: resonance of a cantilever

A cantilever has $\omega_0=10$ (in units of $10^3\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$) and damping rate $\gamma=0.5$, driven with $F/\omega_0^2=0.01$ (static deflection). Find the quality factor and the peak amplitude.

$Q=\omega_0/\gamma=20$. At resonance $X=F/(\gamma\omega_0)=Q\,\allowbreak F/\omega_0^2=20\times0.01=0.2$, twenty times the static deflection. The full width of the peak is about $\gamma=0.5$, i.e. $5\%$ of $\omega_0$. A material with more internal friction (a lower $Q$) gives a lower and broader peak; measuring that width is how $Q^{-1}$ is read in a mechanical spectroscopy experiment.

Lecture 4. Systems of ODEs and the phase plane#

A linear system $\dot{\mathbf y}=A\mathbf y$ with constant matrix $A$ is solved by the eigenvalue method of MATH 202: if $A\mathbf v_i=\lambda_i\mathbf v_i$ then $\mathbf y(t)=\sum_ic_ie^{\lambda_it}\mathbf v_i$, the $c_i$ fixed by $\mathbf y(0)$. For a two-variable system the behaviour near the origin is classified by the trace $\tau=\operatorname{tr}A$ and determinant $\Delta=\det A$, because $\lambda=\tfrac12\bigl(\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}\bigr)$:

Nonlinear systems: linearisation#

For a nonlinear $\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(\mathbf y)$ find the fixed points $\mathbf f(\mathbf y^*)=\mathbf0$ and linearise: $\dot{\boldsymbol\delta}=J\boldsymbol\delta$ with the Jacobian $J_{ij}=\partial f_i/\partial y_j$ evaluated at $\mathbf y^*$. If all eigenvalues of $J$ have non-zero real part, the fixed point has the same type and stability as the linear system. Eigenvalues with zero real part (centres) need a closer look, for example a conserved quantity. This is the same procedure as for the Hessian at a minimum of an energy surface, with a gradient flow $\dot{\mathbf y}=-\nabla U$ having $J=-H$.

Worked example 4.1: consecutive reactions

For $\mathrm A\xrightarrow{k_1}\mathrm B\xrightarrow{k_2}\mathrm C$ with $a(0)=a_0$, $b(0)=c(0)=0$, find $b(t)$ and the time of maximum $b$.

$\dot a=-k_1a$ gives $a=a_0e^{-k_1t}$. Then $\dot b+k_2b=k_1a_0e^{-k_1t}$, a first-order linear equation with the integrating factor $e^{k_2t}$: $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(be^{k_2t})=k_1a_0e^{(k_2-k_1)t}$, so $$b(t)=\frac{k_1a_0}{k_2-k_1}\bigl(e^{-k_1t}-e^{-k_2t}\bigr)\quad(k_1\ne k_2).$$ $\dot b=0$ gives $k_1e^{-k_1t}=k_2e^{-k_2t}$, so $t^*=\dfrac{\ln(k_2/k_1)}{k_2-k_1}$, with $b_\mathrm{max}=a_0(k_1/k_2)^{k_2/(k_2-k_1)}$. The intermediate rises, peaks and falls, and $c=a_0-a-b$ is S-shaped. If $k_2\gg k_1$, the intermediate is in a quasi-steady state: $b\approx(k_1/k_2)a$, found by setting $\dot b\approx0$. This steady-state approximation underlies reduced reaction mechanisms (Problem 8).

Worked example 4.2: a boundary value problem

Atoms diffuse into a slab from the surface $x=0$ and are consumed by a first-order reaction: $D\,c''=k\,c$ with $c(0)=c_0$ and $c\to0$ as $x\to\infty$. Find the profile and the penetration depth.

The characteristic equation is $Dr^2=k$, so $r=\pm\sqrt{k/D}$. Decay at infinity excludes the growing exponential: $c(x)=c_0\exp\bigl(-x/\ell\bigr)$ with $\ell=\sqrt{D/k}$. The penetration depth $\ell=\sqrt{D/k}$ is the geometric mean of the diffusion and reaction scales: fast reaction or slow diffusion gives a thin layer. The same structure governs oxide scale growth, hydrogen trapping, and screening of charge in an electrolyte (the Debye length). Note this is a boundary value problem: conditions are given at two places, not at one time.

Worked example 4.3: a damped double well

A particle in the potential $U=-\tfrac12x^2+\tfrac14x^4$ with friction obeys $\dot x=v$, $\dot v=x-x^3-0.5v$. Find and classify the fixed points.

Setting $\dot x=\dot v=0$: $v=0$ and $x(1-x^2)=0$, so $(0,0)$ and $(\pm1,0)$. The Jacobian is $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1-3x^2&-0.5\end{pmatrix}$. At the origin, $\Delta=-1<0$: a saddle (the hilltop between the wells), $\lambda=0.78,\,-1.28$. At $(\pm1,0)$, $\tau=-0.5$, $\Delta=2$, $\tau^2-4\Delta<0$: stable spirals with $\lambda=-0.25\pm1.39i$ (the particle rings down into a well, $U''(\pm1)=2$). This is the phase-plane picture of a bistable material: two stable states, an unstable barrier top, and the stable manifold of the saddle separating their basins.

Lecture 5. Numerical solution#

Most nonlinear ODEs cannot be solved in closed form, and simulations integrate $\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(\mathbf y,t)$ in steps $h$. Everything here is the Taylor series of MATH 101.

Euler’s method and its errors#

Truncate $y(t+h)=y+hy'+\tfrac12h^2y''+\cdots$ after the first-derivative term: the explicit Euler method is $$y_{n+1}=y_n+h\,f(y_n,t_n).$$ The error made in one step, the local truncation error, is $\tfrac12h^2y''=O(h^2)$. Over $T/h$ steps these accumulate to a global error $O(h)$: first order. To halve the error you must halve the step and double the work. Higher-order methods do better: the midpoint (RK2) rule evaluates $f$ at the half step; the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) $$k_1=f(y_n),\ k_2=f(y_n+\tfrac h2k_1),\ k_3=f(y_n+\tfrac h2k_2),\ k_4=f(y_n+hk_3),\quad y_{n+1}=y_n+\tfrac h6(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4)$$ has global error $O(h^4)$, so halving $h$ cuts the error by $16$, at the cost of four force evaluations per step.

Stability and stiffness#

Accuracy is not the only constraint. Apply Euler to the test equation $\dot y=-\lambda y$ ($\lambda>0$, exact solution decays): $y_{n+1}=(1-h\lambda)y_n$. The numerical solution decays only if $|1-h\lambda|<1$, i.e. $h<2/\lambda$; beyond that it oscillates with growing amplitude, nonsense produced by the algorithm. The implicit (backward) Euler method $y_{n+1}=y_n+hf(y_{n+1},t_{n+1})$ gives $y_{n+1}=y_n/(1+h\lambda)$, which decays for every $h>0$: it is unconditionally stable, at the price of solving an (often nonlinear) equation each step.

A system is stiff if it contains widely separated time scales, e.g. eigenvalues $\lambda_\mathrm{fast}\gg\lambda_\mathrm{slow}$. The explicit step is limited by the fast mode, $h<2/\lambda_\mathrm{fast}$, even after that mode has decayed and the interesting evolution is slow. Examples: reaction networks with short-lived radicals, phase-field and diffusion problems on fine meshes (the largest eigenvalue of the discrete Laplacian grows like $1/\Delta x^2$, MATH 301), and electronic-structure relaxation. Stiff problems call for implicit or semi-implicit integrators.

Molecular dynamics: Verlet and symplectic integration#

Newton’s equations of an isolated system conserve energy and are time-reversible. An integrator that respects this structure behaves much better over $10^6$ to $10^9$ steps than a generic high-order one. Adding the Taylor expansions of $x(t\pm h)$ cancels the odd terms and gives the Verlet scheme $x(t+h)=2x(t)-x(t-h)+h^2a(t)+O(h^4)$ with $a=F/m$. In its common velocity Verlet form, $$x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_n+\tfrac12h^2a_n,\qquad v_{n+1}=v_n+\tfrac12h\,(a_n+a_{n+1}),$$ where $a_{n+1}$ is computed from the new positions. It is second order, needs one force evaluation per step, is exactly time-reversible and symplectic: it conserves a slightly perturbed “shadow” energy, so the true energy oscillates within $O(h^2)$ and does not drift. By contrast explicit Euler applied to an oscillator multiplies the energy by $1+h^2\omega^2$ at every step (Problem 10): heating, without bound. Verlet is stable for $h\omega<2$ but accuracy needs far smaller steps: the usual rule is to take about $10$ to $20$ steps per period of the fastest vibration.

In materials science: choosing the time step

The fastest motion in most organic and hydrated materials is the O–H or C–H stretch (about $3000$–$3600$ cm$^{-1}$, period $\approx10$ fs), which forces $h\approx0.5$–$1$ fs; constraining those bonds or replacing H by D (or redistributing mass) allows $2$–$4$ fs. In metals and ionic solids the highest phonon periods are $\gtrsim50$ fs and $h=2$–$5$ fs suffices. A $1$ ns trajectory at $1$ fs needs $10^6$ steps, which is why MD accessible time scales (nanoseconds to microseconds) fall so far short of laboratory seconds, and why accelerated sampling methods exist (Molecular dynamics).

Worked example 5.1: orders of accuracy

Integrate $\dot y=-y$, $y(0)=1$ to $t=1$ with $h=0.1$. Compare Euler and RK4 with $e^{-1}=0.367879$.

Euler multiplies by $1-h=0.9$ each step: $0.9^{10}=0.348678$, error $0.0192$. RK4 multiplies by $1-h+\tfrac{h^2}2-\tfrac{h^3}6+\tfrac{h^4}{24}=0.904837$ (the Taylor series of $e^{-h}$ to fourth order): $0.904837^{10}=0.367880$, error $3\times10^{-7}$. RK4 costs four times as many function evaluations and is about $60\,000$ times more accurate here. This is why Euler is a teaching tool rather than a production method.

Worked example 5.2: energy drift and stiffness

(a) Apply explicit Euler to an oscillator with $\omega=1$, $h=0.1$ for 100 steps. (b) A reaction network has $\lambda_\mathrm{fast}=1000\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\lambda_\mathrm{slow}=1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$; how many explicit steps are needed for $10$ s?

(a) Euler multiplies the energy by $1+h^2\omega^2=1.01$ per step, so after 100 steps ($t=10$) the energy is $1.01^{100}=2.70$ times too high: a bound system that heats itself. Velocity Verlet keeps the energy within $O(h^2)$ of its initial value forever. (b) The stability limit is $h<2/1000=2\times10^{-3}$ s, so $5000$ steps are needed to reach $10$ s, although the slow mode alone could be followed accurately with $h\approx0.1$ s (100 steps). An implicit method removes the limit at the price of solving a linear system per step.

Summary of the unit#

First-order equations capture relaxation and kinetics; second-order linear ones capture oscillation, damping and resonance; systems unify both through eigenvalues and the phase plane; and linearisation extends the story to nonlinear problems, with the Jacobian playing the role the Hessian plays for energy minima. Numerically, the step size is set by accuracy (the order of the method) and by stability (the largest eigenvalue magnitude), and structure-preserving integrators win for long Hamiltonian runs. Time is only one variable; next we study functions as sums of waves, which will let us solve equations in space as well.

Key takeaways
  • An ordinary differential equation relates a function to its derivatives; with an initial condition it usually has a unique solution, and first-order separable and linear equations cover first-order kinetics, relaxation and nucleation-and-growth laws such as the Avrami equation.
  • For $\ddot{x}+\gamma\dot{x}+\omega_0^2x=0$ the roots of the characteristic equation decide the behaviour (overdamped, critically damped, oscillating decay), and a driven oscillator resonates near $\omega_0$ with amplitude amplified by the quality factor $Q=\omega_0/\gamma$.
  • A system $\dot{\mathbf{y}}=A\mathbf{y}$ is solved by eigenvalues, $\mathbf{y}=\sum c_ie^{\lambda_it}\mathbf{v}_i$; the fixed point is stable if all real parts are negative, and a nonlinear system near a fixed point behaves like its linearisation with $A$ the Jacobian.
  • Explicit Euler is first order and is unstable for $h>2/|\lambda|$; implicit methods remove this limit, which matters for stiff problems with widely separated time scales, such as reaction networks with fast radicals.
  • Velocity Verlet is second order, time-reversible and symplectic, so it conserves energy over millions of molecular-dynamics steps while explicit Euler gains energy at every step; the time step must resolve the fastest vibration, about a tenth of its period.

A second-order reaction $2\mathrm A\to\mathrm P$ obeys $\dot c=-kc^2$ with $k=0.50$ L mol$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ and $c_0=1.0$ mol/L. (a) Solve for $c(t)$. Hint

Separate: $\mathrm dc/c^2=-k\,\mathrm dt$ and integrate from $c_0$ to $c$.

Full solution

(a) $\dfrac1c-\dfrac1{c_0}=kt\Rightarrow c(t)=\dfrac{c_0}{1+kc_0t}=\dfrac{1.0}{1+0.5t}$ mol/L.

(b) Half-life: $1+kc_0t=2\Rightarrow t_{1/2}=\dfrac1{kc_0}=2.0$ s. For $c=0.10$: $1+0.5t=10\Rightarrow t=18$ s. (Note the slow $1/t$ tail: after $18$ s still $10\%$ left, whereas a first-order process with $t_{1/2}=2$ s would be down to $0.2\%$.)

(c) With $c_0=2.0$: $t_{1/2}=1/(0.5\times2)=1.0$ s: halved. For first-order kinetics $t_{1/2}=\ln2/k$ is independent of $c_0$. Measuring how the half-life depends on the starting concentration thus reveals the reaction order.

Problem 7.2Basic

(a) Solve $\dot y+2y=e^{-t}$ with $y(0)=0$. (b) A steel part at $900$ K is quenched in an environment at $300$ K and cools by $\dot T=-(T-300)/\tau$ with $\tau=120$ s. Find $T(t)$ and the time to reach $400$ K.

Hint

(a) Integrating factor $e^{2t}$. (b) Let $\theta=T-300$.

Full solution

(a) $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(e^{2t}y)=e^{2t}e^{-t}=e^{t}$, so $e^{2t}y=e^t+C$; $y(0)=0$ gives $C=-1$. Hence $y=e^{-t}-e^{-2t}$. Check: $\dot y+2y=-e^{-t}+2e^{-2t}+2e^{-t}-2e^{-2t}=e^{-t}$. ✓ (It rises and decays, like the intermediate in Example 4.1.)

(b) $\theta=T-300$ obeys $\dot\theta=-\theta/\tau$, so $\theta=600e^{-t/\tau}$ and $T=300+600e^{-t/120}$ K. $T=400$: $e^{-t/120}=\tfrac16$, $t=120\ln6=215$ s.

$y=e^{-t}-e^{-2t}$; $T=300+600e^{-t/120}$; 215 s

Problem 7.3Core

A recrystallisation experiment finds that $50\%$ of the material has transformed after $100$ s and $90\%$ after $200$ s. (a) Fit the Avrami law $X=1-\exp[-(kt)^n]$ for $n$ and $k$. (b) Predict the time for $10\%$ and for $99\%$ transformation. (c) What does the shape of the curve say about the transformation rate?

Hint

Linearise: $\ln[-\ln(1-X)]=n\ln t+n\ln k$. Use two points.

Full solution

(a) At $X=0.5$: $\ln[-\ln0.5]=\ln0.6931=-0.3665$. At $X=0.9$: $\ln[-\ln0.1]=\ln2.3026=0.8340$. The slope with respect to $\ln t$ is $n=\dfrac{0.8340-(-0.3665)}{\ln200-\ln100}=\dfrac{1.2005}{0.6931}=1.73$. Then $(kt)^n=0.6931$ at $t=100$: $kt=0.6931^{1/1.732}=e^{-0.2116}=0.8093$, so $k=8.09\times10^{-3}$ s$^{-1}$.

(b) $X=0.10$: $(kt)^n=-\ln0.9=0.1054$, $kt=0.1054^{1/1.732}=0.2727$, $t=33.7$ s. $X=0.99$: $(kt)^n=\ln100=4.605$, $kt=4.605^{0.5774}=2.415$, $t=298$ s.

(c) The transformation takes $34$ s to reach $10\%$, a further $66$ s to reach $50\%$, and about $200$ s to go from $50\%$ to $99\%$: the classic sigmoidal curve with a slow start (nucleation), fast middle (growth) and slow finish (impingement). An exponent $n\approx1.7$ between $1$ and $2$ suggests growth in one or two dimensions with a decreasing nucleation rate, or diffusion-controlled growth; $n=4$ would mean three-dimensional growth with constant nucleation rate.

$n=1.73$, $k=8.1\times10^{-3}$ s⁻¹; $t_{10\%}=34$ s, $t_{99\%}=298$ s

Problem 7.4Core

For $\dot\eta=-\Gamma(A\eta+b\eta^3)$ with $b>0$: (a) find all fixed points and their stability for $A>0$ and for $A<0$; (b) find the linear relaxation rate near each stable fixed point; (c) show that the ratio of relaxation times just below and above the transition, at equal $|A|$, is $\tau_-/\tau_+=\tfrac12$; (d) describe the dynamics starting from a small $\eta=0.01$ for $A<0$, $\Gamma=b=1$, $A=-1$.

Hint

$f(\eta)=-\Gamma(A\eta+b\eta^3)$, $f'=-\Gamma(A+3b\eta^2)$.

Full solution

(a) $f=0$ gives $\eta(A+b\eta^2)=0$: $\eta=0$, and $\eta=\pm\sqrt{-A/b}$ if $A<0$. $f'(0)=-\Gamma A$. For $A>0$: $f'(0)<0$, so $\eta=0$ is stable (the only fixed point). For $A<0$: $f'(0)=\Gamma|A|>0$, unstable; and at $\eta_0^2=|A|/b$: $f'=-\Gamma(A+3|A|)=-2\Gamma|A|<0$, so $\eta=\pm\eta_0$ are stable.

(b) Above $T_c$: rate $\Gamma A$, $\tau_+=1/(\Gamma A)$. Below $T_c$: rate $2\Gamma|A|$, $\tau_-=1/(2\Gamma|A|)$.

(c) At equal $|A|$: $\tau_-/\tau_+=\dfrac{1/(2\Gamma|A|)}{1/(\Gamma|A|)}=\tfrac12$: ordering is twice as fast just below $T_c$ as the relaxation just above. Both diverge as $|A|\to0$, i.e. $\tau\propto|T-T_c|^{-1}$: critical slowing down.

(d) With $A=-1$, $\Gamma=b=1$: $\dot\eta=\eta-\eta^3$. Near $0$ the growth is exponential, $\eta\approx0.01e^{t}$, until $\eta$ is of order $1$ (at $t\approx\ln100=4.6$), then it saturates and approaches $\eta_0=1$ with rate $2$: a logistic-like S-curve. The seed grows because the disordered state is unstable; a similar growth of fluctuations is spinodal decomposition (Phase-field modelling).

Problem 7.5Core

Solve $\ddot x+2\dot x+5x=0$ with $x(0)=1$, $\dot x(0)=0$. (a) Find $\omega_0$, $\zeta$ and the damped frequency. (b) Write $x(t)$. (c) How many oscillation periods pass before the envelope drops below $1\%$? (d) For which values of the damping coefficient $\gamma$ (keeping $\omega_0^2=5$) is the motion overdamped?

Hint

Characteristic equation $r^2+2r+5=0$ gives $r=-1\pm2i$. Fit $A,B$ to the initial conditions.

Full solution

(a) $\omega_0=\sqrt5=2.236$, $\gamma=2$, $\zeta=\gamma/(2\omega_0)=0.447<1$ (underdamped), $\omega_d=\sqrt{5-1}=2$.

(b) $x=e^{-t}(A\cos2t+B\sin2t)$. $x(0)=A=1$. $\dot x=-e^{-t}(A\cos2t+B\sin2t)+e^{-t}(-2A\sin2t+2B\cos2t)$, so $\dot x(0)=-A+2B=0\Rightarrow B=\tfrac12$. Therefore $x(t)=e^{-t}\bigl(\cos2t+\tfrac12\sin2t\bigr)$.

(c) The envelope is $\sqrt{1+\tfrac14}\,\allowbreak e^{-t}=1.12\,\allowbreak e^{-t}$, which falls below $0.01$ when $t>\ln112=4.7$ s. The period is $2\pi/\omega_d=\pi=3.14$ s, so about $1.5$ periods: heavily damped ($Q=\omega_0/\gamma=1.1$).

(d) Overdamped when $\gamma>2\omega_0=2\sqrt5=4.47$.

Problem 7.6Core

A lattice vibration mode is modelled as a driven damped oscillator with $\omega_0=1.0\times10^{13}$ rad/s ($1.59$ THz) and damping $\gamma=2.0\times10^{11}$ rad/s. (a) Find $Q$. (b) Find the ratio of the steady-state amplitude at resonance to the amplitude at zero frequency (the static response). (c) Find the resonance frequency shift $\omega_r-\omega_0$ and the width of the resonance (FWHM) in rad/s and in cm$^{-1}$. (d) What is the phase lag at $\omega_0$?

Hint

$X(\omega)=F/\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}$, so $X(0)=F/\omega_0^2$ and $X(\omega_0)=F/(\gamma\omega_0)$. Wavenumber: $\tilde\nu=\omega/(2\pi c)$ with $c=3.0\times10^{10}$ cm/s.

Full solution

(a) $Q=\omega_0/\gamma=50$.

(b) $X(\omega_0)/X(0)=\dfrac{F/(\gamma\omega_0)}{F/\omega_0^2}=\dfrac{\omega_0}\gamma=Q=50$.

(c) $\omega_r=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2}=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\tfrac12(0.02)^2}=\omega_0(1-10^{-4})$: a shift of $-10^{9}$ rad/s ($10^{-4}$ of $\omega_0$), negligible for high $Q$. The FWHM is $\approx\gamma=2.0\times10^{11}$ rad/s; in wavenumbers $\gamma/(2\pi c)=2.0\times10^{11}/(1.885\times10^{11})=1.06$ cm$^{-1}$. A peak at $\tilde\nu_0=\omega_0/(2\pi c)=53$ cm$^{-1}$ of width $1$ cm$^{-1}$: a sharp far-infrared/Raman line from a well-ordered crystal. Disorder and anharmonic decay (phonon-phonon scattering) increase $\gamma$ and broaden the line, which is how spectral line widths measure phonon lifetimes, $\tau=1/\gamma=5$ ps.

(d) At $\omega=\omega_0$: $\tan\delta=\gamma\omega_0/0=\infty$, so $\delta=\pi/2$: the displacement lags the drive by a quarter of a cycle, which is where the absorbed power is greatest.

$Q=50$; amplification $50$; FWHM $\approx2\times10^{11}$ rad/s $=1.06$ cm⁻¹; lag $\pi/2$

Problem 7.7Core

Classify the fixed point at the origin of $\dot{\mathbf y}=A\mathbf y$ for (a) $A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix}$, (b) $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$, (c) $A=\begin{pmatrix}-3&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$. (d) For the nonlinear system $\dot x=x(1-y)$, $\dot y=y(x-1)$ find the fixed points, linearise, and show that $H=x-\ln x+y-\ln y$ is conserved.

Hint

Compute $\tau$ and $\Delta$ for (a)–(c). For (d) the Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}1-y&-x\\y&x-1\end{pmatrix}$.

Full solution

(a) $\tau=-2$, $\Delta=1+4=5$, $\tau^2-4\Delta=-16<0$: $\lambda=-1\pm2i$, a stable spiral.

(b) $\tau=2$, $\Delta=1-4=-3<0$: a saddle, $\lambda=3,-1$ (eigenvectors $(1,1)$ unstable and $(1,-1)$ stable).

(c) Diagonal, $\lambda=-3,-1$: a stable node, trajectories approach tangent to the slow direction $(0,1)$.

(d) Fixed points: $x(1-y)=0$ and $y(x-1)=0$ give $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. At $(0,0)$: $J=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$, a saddle. At $(1,1)$: $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\pm i$: a linear centre. Linearisation cannot decide whether a centre persists, but $H$ settles it: $\dot H=(1-\tfrac1x)\dot x+(1-\tfrac1y)\dot y=(x-1)(1-y)+(y-1)(x-1)=0$. So trajectories stay on level curves of $H$, which are closed around $(1,1)$ (the minimum of $H$): neutral oscillations. This is the Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model, a template for oscillating reaction networks.

Problem 7.8Core

For $\mathrm A\to\mathrm B\to\mathrm C$ with $k_1=1.0$ s$^{-1}$, $k_2=3.0$ s$^{-1}$ and $a_0=1$: (a) find the time of maximum $b$ and its value; (b) compare the exact $b(t)$ with the steady-state approximation $b\approx(k_1/k_2)a$ at $t=0.55$ s and $t=3$ s; (c) what does the steady-state approximation require, and how good is it for this ratio?

Hint

Use $b(t)=\dfrac{k_1a_0}{k_2-k_1}(e^{-k_1t}-e^{-k_2t})$ and $t^*=\dfrac{\ln(k_2/k_1)}{k_2-k_1}$.

Full solution

(a) $t^*=\ln3/2=0.549$ s. $b_\mathrm{max}=(1/3)^{3/2}=0.192$. (Check: $\tfrac12(e^{-0.549}-e^{-1.648})=\tfrac12(0.5774-0.1925)=0.1925$. ✓)

(b) At $t=0.55$: exact $b=0.192$; approximation $\tfrac13e^{-0.55}=0.5769/3=0.192$. They agree at this point because the maximum of $b$ is where $\dot b=0$, which is exactly the condition of the approximation. At $t=3$: exact $b=\tfrac12(e^{-3}-e^{-9})=\tfrac12(0.04979-0.00012)=0.02483$; approximation $\tfrac13e^{-3}=0.01660$: exact is $50\%$ larger. At early times $t\to0$ the approximation gives $b=\tfrac13$ while the true $b=0$.

(c) The approximation requires $b$ to change slowly compared with how fast it relaxes, i.e. $k_2\gg k_1$ (and $t\gg1/k_2$). Here $k_2/k_1=3$ is too small for accuracy: the error at long times tends to the factor $k_2/(k_2-k_1)=1.5$. For $k_2/k_1=100$ the long-time error is only $1\%$. Reduced mechanisms in combustion and corrosion chemistry rely on this approximation for many fast intermediates.

Problem 7.9Core

Consider the stiff decay $\dot y=-100y$, $y(0)=1$. (a) Write the amplification factor per step for explicit Euler and for implicit Euler. (b) Starting from $y_0=1$, take explicit Euler steps with $h=0.01$, $h=0.015$ and $h=0.025$ and describe the results over a few steps. (c) What step size does implicit Euler allow with $h=0.1$, and how accurate is it? (d) What is the maximum stable $h$ for explicit Euler?

Hint

Explicit: $y_{n+1}=(1-h\lambda)y_n$. Implicit: $y_{n+1}=y_n/(1+h\lambda)$.

Full solution

(a) With $\lambda=100$: explicit $G=1-100h$; implicit $G=1/(1+100h)$. The exact factor is $e^{-100h}$.

(b) $h=0.01$: $G=0$, so $y_1=0$ (the decay is captured in one step, crudely but stably). $h=0.015$: $G=-0.5$: $y=1,-0.5,0.25,-0.125,\ldots$: oscillating but decaying (stable, wrong sign alternation: the exact solution is positive). $h=0.025$: $G=-1.5$: $y=1,-1.5,2.25,-3.375,\ldots$: unstable, growing by $50\%$ every step.

(c) $h=0.1$: $G=1/11=0.0909$, so $y_1=0.0909$ versus the exact $e^{-10}=4.5\times10^{-5}$: stable, positive and monotonic, but not accurate in the fast transient (first order). If only the slow solution matters, as for a stiff mode forced by a slow input, this is exactly what you want: the step is chosen for accuracy, not for the fast mode.

(d) $|1-100h|<1\Rightarrow h<0.02$. At the very boundary $h=0.02$ the factor is $-1$ and the solution flips sign forever.

Problem 7.10Challenge

For the harmonic oscillator $\ddot x=-\omega^2x$ with energy $E=\tfrac12(v^2+\omega^2x^2)$ (per unit mass): (a) Show that explicit Euler ($x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_n$, $v_{n+1}=v_n-h\omega^2x_n$) gives $E_{n+1}=(1+h^2\omega^2)E_n$. (b) For $\omega=1$, $h=0.1$, $x_0=1$, $v_0=0$, find the energy after $63$ steps ($t\approx2\pi$, one period). (c) Run velocity Verlet for the same problem and compare. (d) What is the stability limit of velocity Verlet?

Hint

(a) Square the update equations. (c) Velocity Verlet: $x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_n-\tfrac12h^2\omega^2x_n$, $v_{n+1}=v_n-\tfrac12h\omega^2(x_n+x_{n+1})$.

Full solution

(a) $v_{n+1}^2+\omega^2x_{n+1}^2=(v_n-h\omega^2x_n)^2+\omega^2(x_n+hv_n)^2=v_n^2+\omega^2x_n^2+h^2\omega^4x_n^2+h^2\omega^2v_n^2=(1+h^2\omega^2)(v_n^2+\omega^2x_n^2)$, because the cross terms $\mp2h\omega^2x_nv_n$ cancel. So $E_{n+1}=(1+h^2\omega^2)E_n$, a systematic energy gain at every step.

(b) $E_{63}/E_0=(1.01)^{63}=1.872$: after one period the energy has almost doubled (amplitude up $37\%$), and it keeps growing exponentially. A simulation of a vibrating solid with explicit Euler would heat itself up and eventually blow up.

(c) Running $63$ velocity Verlet steps gives $E$ between $0.49875$ and $0.50000$ (initial $E=0.5$): the energy fluctuates by about $0.25\%$ and does not drift. The final position is $x=0.99981$ against the exact $\cos6.3=0.99986$: the phase error is tiny.

(d) Verlet is stable for $h\omega<2$ (then the numerical solution remains bounded and oscillates at a slightly wrong frequency). For accuracy, though, far smaller steps are used: here $h\omega=0.1$ gives $63$ steps per period; the frequency error scales as $h^2\omega^2/24\approx0.04\%$ (for $h\omega=0.1$).

Problem 7.11Core

(a) The C–H stretching vibration is at $\tilde\nu=3000$ cm$^{-1}$. Find its period, and the time step that gives $10$ steps per period. (b) What is the velocity-Verlet stability limit for this mode? (c) If the hydrogen is replaced by deuterium (frequency lower by $\approx\sqrt2$, i.e. $2100$ cm$^{-1}$), what are the new period and step? (d) How many steps are needed to simulate $5$ ns at $1$ fs, and what wall-clock time at $10^{6}$ steps per hour does that take?

Hint

Period $T=1/(c\tilde\nu)$ with $c=3.0\times10^{10}$ cm/s. Stability: $h<2/\omega=T/\pi$.

Full solution

(a) $T=\dfrac{1}{(3.0\times10^{10})(3000)}=1.11\times10^{-14}$ s $=11.1$ fs. Ten steps per period: $h\approx1.1$ fs: the usual choice of about $1$ fs.

(b) $\omega=2\pi/T=5.65\times10^{14}$ rad/s; the Verlet limit is $h<2/\omega=3.5$ fs, which is $T/\pi$. So $1$ fs is well within the stable range, and chosen for accuracy.

(c) $T_D=1/[(3.0\times10^{10})(2100)]=15.9$ fs, so $h\approx1.6$ fs for $10$ steps per period (and a stability limit of $5.1$ fs). Heavier isotopes, or constraint algorithms that freeze the fast bonds (SHAKE, RATTLE), allow longer steps ($2$–$4$ fs).

(d) $5$ ns $=5\times10^{-9}$ s at $10^{-15}$ s per step is $5\times10^6$ steps; at $10^6$ steps per hour: $5$ hours. Reaching a microsecond would take $1000$ times longer ($5000$ hours), which is why rare events are studied with enhanced sampling or kinetic Monte Carlo rather than brute-force MD.

Problem 7.12Challenge

A reaction network linearised about a steady state has $\dot{\mathbf y}=A\mathbf y$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}-1000&0\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Find the eigenvalues, the stiffness ratio and the solution for $\mathbf y(0)=(1,1)$. (b) What is the largest stable step for explicit Euler, and how many steps are needed for $t\in[0,5]$? (c) What step would be adequate for accuracy of the slow component, and how many steps would that need? (d) Why is an implicit method the natural choice?

Hint

$A$ is lower-triangular, so $y_1=e^{-1000t}$ and $y_2$ solves $\dot y_2=-y_2+y_1$.

Full solution

(a) Triangular: eigenvalues $-1000$ and $-1$; the stiffness ratio is $1000$. $y_1=e^{-1000t}$. Then $\dot y_2+y_2=e^{-1000t}$, a first-order linear equation: $y_2=Ce^{-1000t}+Be^{-t}$; substituting the first term gives $-1000C+C=1$, so $C=-\tfrac1{999}$, and $y_2(0)=1$ gives $B=\tfrac{1000}{999}$. Hence $y_2=-\tfrac1{999}e^{-1000t}+\bigl(1+\tfrac1{999}\bigr)e^{-t}=\tfrac{1000}{999}e^{-t}-\tfrac1{999}e^{-1000t}$. After a few milliseconds only the slow mode $y_2\approx1.001e^{-t}$ remains.

(b) Explicit Euler is stable only for $h<2/1000=0.002$. Over $[0,5]$ that needs at least $2500$ steps.

(c) The slow mode has time scale $1$ s, and a first-order method with $h=0.05$ gives an error of order $h\,\lambda_\mathrm{slow}=5\%$ (RK4 would manage with $h=0.5$). With $h=0.05$ that is only $100$ steps: the fast mode dictates $25$ times as many steps as the physics of interest.

(d) Implicit Euler is stable for any $h$, so one can choose the step for the slow dynamics, solving a linear system (here $2\times2$; in a large reaction network or a discretised PDE, a sparse matrix) at each step. The fast mode is damped to its quasi-steady value automatically, just as the steady-state approximation of Problem 7.8 does by hand. Stiff solvers (BDF methods, Rosenbrock, implicit Runge–Kutta) are therefore standard in combustion kinetics, corrosion models and phase-field codes with long time steps.

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1First order

Solve $\dot y=-3y$, $y(0)=2$, and find when $y=0.5$.

Solution

$y=2e^{-3t}$. Setting $2e^{-3t}=0.5$ gives $t=\ln4/3=0.462$.

Quiz 2Integrating factor

Solve $\dot y+y=t$ with $y(0)=0$.

Solution

$\mu=e^t$: $(ye^t)^\prime=te^t$, so $ye^t=(t-1)e^t+C$. $y(0)=0$ gives $C=1$, so $y=t-1+e^{-t}$. Check: $\dot y+y=(1-e^{-t})+(t-1+e^{-t})=t$ ✓.

Quiz 3Oscillator

An oscillator has $\omega_0=2$ and damping rate $\gamma=1$. Classify it and give the damped frequency.

Solution

$\zeta=\gamma/(2\omega_0)=0.25<1$: underdamped. $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}=\sqrt{4-0.25}=1.936$. The amplitude decays as $e^{-t/2}$ and $Q=\omega_0/\gamma=2$.

Quiz 4Phase plane

Classify the fixed point of $\dot{\mathbf y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{pmatrix}\mathbf y$.

Solution

$\tau=-3$, $\Delta=2$, $\tau^2-4\Delta=1>0$: real eigenvalues $-1,-2$, both negative: a stable node (no oscillation; the slow mode $e^{-t}$ dominates at late times).

Quiz 5Stability limit

For a fastest mode with $\lambda=50\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, what is the largest stable explicit Euler step? What happens at $h=0.05$?

Solution

$h<2/\lambda=0.04$ s. At $h=0.05$ the factor is $1-\lambda h=-1.5$: the numerical solution flips sign and grows by $1.5$ each step.

Quiz 6Integrators

Why is velocity Verlet preferred to explicit Euler in MD? Quantify the Euler energy error for $\omega=2$, $h=0.05$, $100$ steps.

Solution

Euler multiplies the energy by $1+h^2\omega^2=1.01$ per step, so $1.01^{100}=2.70$: the energy nearly triples. Velocity Verlet is symplectic and time-reversible: its energy oscillates within $O(h^2)$ and has no drift, at the same cost of one force evaluation per step.
