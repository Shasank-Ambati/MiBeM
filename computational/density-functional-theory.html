Density functional theory I: from the Schrödinger equation to Kohn–Sham

Almost every property of a material that is not a thermodynamic or mechanical bulk number can in principle be traced to the behaviour of its electrons: the cohesive energy and lattice constant, elastic constants and phonons, the band gap, defect energies, surface energies, magnetism, the barrier of a chemical reaction. The theory of electrons in matter is quantum mechanics, but the exact equation is far too hard to solve for more than a few electrons. Density functional theory (DFT) is the method that made quantitative first-principles calculations of solids and large molecules routine: it is used in more research papers in materials science than any other single method. This course (Part I of two) develops the theory from the Schrödinger equation, writes a Hartree–Fock program from scratch to see how a self-consistent-field calculation works, derives the Kohn–Sham scheme, and then tests the approximations in the way a practitioner must: against atoms, atomisation energies, stretched bonds, ionisation energies, weak interactions and vibrations. Part II (CMS 511) applies the method to crystals: plane waves, pseudopotentials, k points, metals, and the workflows of materials research. All numbers come from calculations with the open-source package PySCF (LAB 501 shows how to install and use it); the programs are in labs/cms-501.

Lecture 1. The electronic-structure problem#

A material is a collection of nuclei and electrons interacting by the Coulomb force. Nuclei are $10^3$ to $10^5$ times heavier than electrons, so on the time scale of electronic motion they are almost stationary: the Born–Oppenheimer approximation solves for the electrons in the field of fixed nuclei at positions $\{\mathbf R_I\}$, and the resulting ground-state energy $E(\{\mathbf R_I\})$ is the potential-energy surface on which the nuclei move (classical molecular dynamics, CMS 502, and phonons, LAB 301, both use it). In atomic units ($\hbar=m_e=e=4\pi\epsilon_0=1$) the electronic Hamiltonian for $N$ electrons is $$\hat H=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^N\nabla_i^2+\sum_{i=1}^Nv(\mathbf r_i)+\sum_{i<j}\frac1{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|},\qquad v(\mathbf r)=-\sum_I\frac{Z_I}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf R_I|},$$ the kinetic energy, the attraction to the nuclei and the electron–electron repulsion, and the ground-state wave function $\Psi(\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_N)$ ($\mathbf x=(\mathbf r,\sigma)$ with spin $\sigma$) solves $\hat H\Psi=E\Psi$. It must be antisymmetric under the exchange of any two electrons (the Pauli principle for fermions).

Why this is hard

$\Psi$ is a function of $3N$ coordinates. A grid of $10$ points per coordinate for the helium atom ($N=2$) has $10^6$ values; for neon ($N=10$) $10^{30}$, far more than all the computer memory ever built; for a nanoparticle of $1000$ atoms ($10^4$ electrons) the number is $10^{30\,000}$. Exact solution is impossible and every method is an approximation. The art is to choose the approximation whose cost and accuracy fit the question. The correlation of motion between electrons, which $\Psi$ must describe, is the hard part.

The variational principle

For any normalised antisymmetric trial function $\Psi_\mathrm{t}$ the energy expectation value is never lower than the true ground-state energy, $E[\Psi_\mathrm t]=\langle\Psi_\mathrm t|\hat H|\Psi_\mathrm t\rangle\ge E_0$, with equality only for the exact $\Psi_0$. (Proof: expand $\Psi_\mathrm t$ in the eigenfunctions of $\hat H$, $\Psi_\mathrm t=\sum_nc_n\Psi_n$; then $E[\Psi_\mathrm t]=\sum|c_n|^2E_n\ge E_0\sum|c_n|^2=E_0$.) This turns the Schrödinger equation into an optimisation problem, and it is the foundation of Hartree–Fock theory, of DFT, of the basis-set expansions of all codes ($E$ can only decrease as the basis grows, as in the water calculation of LAB 501) and of quantum Monte Carlo. A consequence for practice: a lower energy computed with the same Hamiltonian means a better wave function; but this holds only for wave-function methods, not for DFT with an approximate functional (Lecture 3).

Slater determinants and the exchange hole

The simplest antisymmetric wave function for $N$ electrons is a Slater determinant of $N$ orthonormal spin-orbitals $\chi_a(\mathbf x)$, $$\Psi_\mathrm{SD}=\frac1{\sqrt{N!}}\det[\chi_a(\mathbf x_b)],$$ which describes independent electrons that respect the Pauli principle. Two same-spin electrons cannot be at the same place ($\Psi_\mathrm{SD}=0$ when $\mathbf x_a=\mathbf x_b$ for same spin): each electron is surrounded by a region where the probability of finding another electron of the same spin is reduced, the exchange hole. The energy of the determinant, minimised over the orbitals, is the Hartree–Fock energy. What a single determinant misses is the correlation between electrons of opposite spin and the additional correlation of same-spin electrons: they avoid each other more than a mean field suggests (dynamical correlation), and in some situations (stretched bonds, transition metals) the wave function needs several determinants of comparable weight (static correlation). The correlation energy is defined as $E_\mathrm{c}=E_\mathrm{exact}-E_\mathrm{HF}$ (negative). It is a small fraction of the total energy but large against what matters in chemistry and materials:

Lecture 2. Hartree–Fock theory and the self-consistent field#

Minimising the energy of a Slater determinant with respect to the orbitals, under the orthonormality constraint, gives the Hartree–Fock equations: each electron moves in the average field of the others, $$\hat F\,\chi_a=\varepsilon_a\chi_a,\qquad\hat F=\hat h+\sum_b\left(\hat J_b-\hat K_b\right),$$ where $\hat h=-\tfrac12\nabla^2+v$ is the one-electron operator, the Coulomb operator $\hat J_b$ is the classical repulsion from the charge density of orbital $b$, and the exchange operator $\hat K_b$ has no classical analogue: it acts on $\chi_a$ as $\hat K_b\chi_a(\mathbf x)=\chi_b(\mathbf x)\int\chi_b^\ast(\mathbf x^{\prime})\chi_a(\mathbf x^{\prime})/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^{\prime}|\,\mathrm d\mathbf x^{\prime}$. For the sum over all occupied $b$ the term $b=a$ appears in both $\hat J$ and $\hat K$ and cancels: Hartree–Fock is free of self-interaction, an electron does not repel itself (a property that Kohn–Sham DFT with an approximate functional loses, Lecture 4). Because $\hat F$ depends on its own eigenfunctions, the equations are nonlinear and are solved by iteration, the self-consistent-field (SCF) method: (1) guess the occupied orbitals; (2) build $\hat F$ from them; (3) diagonalise $\hat F$; (4) take the lowest $N/2$ orbitals as the new occupied ones; (5) repeat until the density no longer changes. In a basis of $n$ functions $\phi_\mu$ (LAB 501) the orbitals are $\chi_a=\sum_\mu C_{\mu a}\phi_\mu$ and the equations become the matrix problem (Roothaan) $$F\,C=S\,C\,\varepsilon,\qquad F_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}+\sum_{\lambda\sigma}D_{\lambda\sigma}\left[2(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)-(\mu\lambda|\nu\sigma)\right],$$ with the overlap matrix $S$ (the basis is not orthogonal), the density matrix $D=C_\mathrm{occ}C_\mathrm{occ}^T$ and the two-electron integrals $(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)$; the factor $2$ is for closed-shell spin pairing. The total energy is $E=\sum_{\mu\nu}D_{\mu\nu}(h_{\mu\nu}+F_{\mu\nu})+E_\mathrm{nuc}$. This is a generalised eigenvalue problem (COMP 202 Lecture 6) inside a nonlinear iteration (COMP 201 Lecture 2); we implement it. The program takes the integrals from PySCF (these are the expensive part and a topic in itself, with $n^4/8$ distinct integrals) and writes the loop:

"""A restricted Hartree-Fock self-consistent-field loop written from scratch (integrals from PySCF), compared with pyscf.scf.RHF."""
            import numpy as np
            from pyscf import gto, scf

            mol = gto.M(atom="O 0 0 0; H 0.7569 0.5859 0; H -0.7569 0.5859 0", basis="6-31g", verbose=0)
            S = mol.intor("int1e_ovlp"); T = mol.intor("int1e_kin"); V = mol.intor("int1e_nuc"); eri = mol.intor("int2e")   # eri[p,q,r,s] = (pq|rs)
            h = T + V; n = mol.nao; nocc = mol.nelectron // 2; Enuc = mol.energy_nuc()
            print(f"water, 6-31G: {n} basis functions, {nocc} occupied orbitals, nuclear repulsion {Enuc:.6f} Ha")

            # symmetric orthogonalisation
            s, U = np.linalg.eigh(S); X = U @ np.diag(s**-0.5) @ U.T

            def fock(D):
                J = np.einsum("pqrs,rs->pq", eri, D); K = np.einsum("prqs,rs->pq", eri, D)
                return h + 2 * J - K                                   # D = C_occ C_occ^T, closed shell
            def energy(D, F): return np.sum(D * (h + F)) + Enuc
            def density(F):
                e, Cp = np.linalg.eigh(X.T @ F @ X); C = X @ Cp
                return C[:, :nocc] @ C[:, :nocc].T, e

            def scf_loop(mode, damp=0.5, maxit=100, tol=1e-9):
                D, _ = density(h)                                       # initial guess: core Hamiltonian
                E_old = 0.0; hist = []; errs = []; Fs = []
                for it in range(1, maxit + 1):
                    F = fock(D); E = energy(D, F)
                    err = X.T @ (F @ D @ S - S @ D @ F) @ X             # commutator FDS - SDF: zero at convergence
                    if mode == "diis":
                        Fs.append(F.copy()); errs.append(err.copy()); Fs, errs = Fs[-8:], errs[-8:]
                        if len(Fs) > 1:
                            m = len(Fs); B = -np.ones((m + 1, m + 1)); B[m, m] = 0
                            for i in range(m):
                                for j in range(m): B[i, j] = np.sum(errs[i] * errs[j])
                            rhs = np.zeros(m + 1); rhs[m] = -1; c = np.linalg.solve(B, rhs)[:m]
                            F = sum(ci * Fi for ci, Fi in zip(c, Fs))
                    Dn, eps = density(F)
                    if mode == "damped": Dn = damp * D + (1 - damp) * Dn
                    hist.append((it, E, np.linalg.norm(err)))
                    if abs(E - E_old) < tol and np.linalg.norm(err) < 1e-6: return E, it, hist, eps
                    E_old = E; D = Dn
                return E, maxit, hist, eps

            ref = scf.RHF(mol).run()
            print(f"pyscf.scf.RHF: E = {ref.e_tot:.9f} Ha in {ref.cycles} cycles\n")
            print("our loop, plain iteration (core-Hamiltonian guess):  iteration   energy (Ha)         |FDS - SDF|")
            E, it, hist, eps = scf_loop("plain")
            for k, e, r in hist[:8] + hist[-3:]: print(f"                                                        {k:3d}     {e:16.9f}      {r:10.2e}")
            print(f"   converged in {it} iterations, E = {E:.9f}, difference from pyscf {E - ref.e_tot:.1e} Ha")
            for mode in ("damped", "diis"):
                E2, it2, _, _ = scf_loop(mode); print(f"   {mode:7s}: {it2:3d} iterations, E = {E2:.9f}, difference from pyscf {E2 - ref.e_tot:.1e} Ha")
            print("\norbital energies (Ha) of our final iteration: " + " ".join(f"{e:8.4f}" for e in eps[: nocc + 3]) + "   (first 5 occupied, then virtual)")
            print("pyscf orbital energies:                       " + " ".join(f"{e:8.4f}" for e in ref.mo_energy[: nocc + 3]))

The first energy ($-69.62$ Ha, from the core-Hamiltonian guess in which electrons do not see each other) is $6.4$ Ha too high; the energy then falls monotonically ($-75.4949$, $-75.7988$, $-75.9181$, $\dots$) and converges to $-75.983995777$ Ha after $32$ iterations, equal to PySCF’s value to $1.7\times10^{-13}$ Ha, with identical orbital energies. The residual $\|FDS-SDF\|$, which vanishes when $F$ and $D$ commute in the metric of the basis (the condition that $D$ is built from eigenvectors of $F$), falls from $2.6$ to $8\times10^{-7}$: it is the quantity to monitor, because the energy converges quadratically in the density error and looks converged long before the orbitals are. Simple iteration can oscillate or diverge for difficult systems, and two standard remedies are shown: damping (mix $50\%$ of the old density with the new one) needs $40$ iterations here, more than the plain loop, because the problem is easy; DIIS (direct inversion in the iterative subspace: extrapolate the Fock matrix from the last eight iterations by minimising the norm of the commutator error) converges in only $11$ iterations, and is the default of PySCF and of nearly every code. When an SCF will not converge the usual suspects are a bad starting guess, a small HOMO–LUMO gap (metals, stretched bonds: use smearing or level shifting), the wrong spin state, or a geometry that is far from reasonable.

What the orbital energies mean: Koopmans’ theorem

The eigenvalue $\varepsilon_a$ is the energy of an electron in orbital $a$ in the field of the other $N-1$. If the orbitals did not relax when an electron is removed, the ionisation energy would be $-\varepsilon_\mathrm{HOMO}$ (Koopmans’ theorem). In our six-function-per-atom calculation $-\varepsilon_\mathrm{HOMO}=13.64$ eV for water; in a larger basis it is $13.89$ eV (the table of Lecture 4), $1.3$ eV above the experimental vertical ionisation energy of $12.62$ eV, because the frozen orbitals ignore the relaxation of the other electrons towards the hole, which lowers the energy of the ion. The Hartree–Fock energy difference $E(\mathrm{cation})-E(\mathrm{neutral})$, which includes the relaxation but not the correlation, gives $10.99$ eV, $1.6$ eV too low (correlation is larger in the neutral molecule); the experiment lies between. The orbital energies of Hartree–Fock are therefore useful, if approximate, estimates of ionisation energies; those of Kohn–Sham DFT with an approximate functional are not (Lecture 4).

Beyond Hartree–Fock

The systematic improvements are the post-Hartree–Fock methods that recover the correlation energy: second-order Møller–Plesset perturbation theory (MP2, formal cost $O(n^5)$), coupled cluster with single and double excitations (CCSD, $O(n^6)$) and with perturbative triples, CCSD(T) ($O(n^7)$), the “gold standard” of quantum chemistry that reaches chemical accuracy for small molecules with large basis sets, and configuration interaction. Their cost makes them unusable for solids with hundreds of atoms but they provide the reference against which cheaper methods are tested, as we do in Lecture 4.

Lecture 3. Density functional theory: the Hohenberg–Kohn theorems and the Kohn–Sham method#

The wave function depends on $3N$ coordinates, but all the information a Hamiltonian with only pair interactions needs is much smaller. The electron density $$n(\mathbf r)=N\sum_{\sigma_1\dots\sigma_N}\int|\Psi(\mathbf r\sigma_1,\mathbf x_2,\dots,\mathbf x_N)|^2\,\mathrm d\mathbf x_2\cdots\mathrm d\mathbf x_N$$ is a function of three variables, whatever $N$ is, it can be measured (X-ray diffraction measures its Fourier transform, MSE 201), and it is intuitive (charge clouds, bonds). Is it enough?

The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (1964)

(1) For electrons in an external potential $v(\mathbf r)$, the ground-state density $n_0(\mathbf r)$ determines $v(\mathbf r)$ up to an additive constant, and hence the Hamiltonian, the wave function and every property of the ground state: all ground-state properties are functionals of $n_0$. (Proof by contradiction with the variational principle: if two potentials differing by more than a constant gave the same density, then $E_0+E_0^{\prime}<E_0+E_0^{\prime}$.) (2) There exists a universal functional $F[n]=T[n]+V_\mathrm{ee}[n]$ (the same for every system, independent of $v$) such that the energy functional $E_v[n]=F[n]+\int v(\mathbf r)n(\mathbf r)\,\mathrm d\mathbf r$ has its minimum, over all densities with $\int n=N$, at the true ground-state density $n_0$, where it equals the ground-state energy $E_0$.

These theorems are existence results: they say that a universal functional exists but not what it is. If we knew $F[n]$ we could find the ground state by minimising a function of three variables rather than $3N$. The kinetic energy $T[n]$ is the troublemaker: no useful explicit expression exists (Thomas–Fermi theory, which approximates $T$ by the kinetic energy of a uniform gas, is too inaccurate to describe chemical bonding).

The Kohn–Sham construction (1965)

Kohn and Sham replaced the interacting system by a fictitious system of non-interacting electrons that has the same density. For non-interacting electrons the kinetic energy is known exactly in terms of orbitals, $T_s[n]=-\tfrac12\sum_i\langle\varphi_i|\nabla^2|\varphi_i\rangle$, and the density is $n(\mathbf r)=\sum_{i}^{N}|\varphi_i(\mathbf r)|^2$. The energy is then written as $$E[n]=T_s[n]+\int v\,n\,\mathrm d\mathbf r+E_\mathrm H[n]+E_\mathrm{xc}[n],\qquad E_\mathrm H=\frac12\iint\frac{n(\mathbf r)n(\mathbf r^{\prime})}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^{\prime}|}\,\mathrm d\mathbf r\,\mathrm d\mathbf r^{\prime},$$ which defines the exchange–correlation energy $E_\mathrm{xc}[n]=(T-T_s)+(V_\mathrm{ee}-E_\mathrm H)$: everything that is not the independent-electron kinetic energy, the classical Coulomb (Hartree) energy and the external potential. It contains the exchange energy (the Pauli effect), the correlation energy and the small difference between the true and non-interacting kinetic energies. Minimising $E[n]$ with respect to the orbitals gives the Kohn–Sham equations $$\left[-\tfrac12\nabla^2+v_\mathrm{KS}(\mathbf r)\right]\varphi_i=\varepsilon_i\varphi_i,\qquad v_\mathrm{KS}=v(\mathbf r)+\int\frac{n(\mathbf r^{\prime})}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^{\prime}|}\,\mathrm d\mathbf r^{\prime}+v_\mathrm{xc}(\mathbf r),\qquad v_\mathrm{xc}=\frac{\delta E_\mathrm{xc}}{\delta n(\mathbf r)}.$$ They have the same structure as the Hartree–Fock equations with the nonlocal exchange operator $\hat K$ replaced by a local multiplicative potential $v_\mathrm{xc}(\mathbf r)$, and are solved by the same self-consistent iteration (the program of Lecture 2 with a different Fock matrix; in a basis, $F=h+J[D]+V_\mathrm{xc}[D]$, with the $V_\mathrm{xc}$ matrix elements computed by numerical integration on a grid of points around the atoms; this is the grids.level parameter you saw in the PySCF scripts). If the exact $E_\mathrm{xc}$ were known, Kohn–Sham theory would give the exact ground-state density and energy; the entire approximation is in $E_\mathrm{xc}$. The cost is that of mean-field theory ($O(n^3)$, with plane waves or with density fitting; $O(n^4)$ with the exact exchange of a hybrid), with the possibility to treat the correlation that Hartree–Fock lacks.

What the Kohn–Sham orbitals are, and are not

The orbitals $\varphi_i$ and eigenvalues $\varepsilon_i$ are mathematical devices to build the density: they have no strict physical meaning, except for one exact result, that for the exact functional the highest occupied eigenvalue is minus the first ionisation energy ($\varepsilon_\mathrm{HOMO}=-I$). The sum $\sum\varepsilon_i$ is not the total energy (the Hartree and exchange–correlation energies are counted twice and must be corrected). Using Kohn–Sham eigenvalues as excitation energies is common practice and works qualitatively (band structures, the shape of the density of states), but it underestimates gaps (Lecture 4).

The ladder of approximations to $E_\mathrm{xc}$

Perdew’s “Jacob’s ladder” classifies functionals by the ingredients they use, each rung adding information and cost:

  1. LDA (local density approximation): $E_\mathrm{xc}=\int n\,\epsilon_\mathrm{xc}^\mathrm{unif}(n)\,\mathrm d\mathbf r$, where $\epsilon_\mathrm{xc}^\mathrm{unif}(n)$ is the exchange–correlation energy per electron of the uniform electron gas of density $n$. The exchange part is known exactly, $\epsilon_\mathrm x=-\tfrac34(3/\pi)^{1/3}n^{1/3}$, and the correlation part was fitted to quantum Monte Carlo calculations of the uniform gas (VWN, PW92). LDA is exact for a uniform gas and works surprisingly well for solids with slowly varying density; it overbinds molecules and solids.
  2. GGA (generalised gradient approximation): adds the gradient of the density, $E_\mathrm{xc}=\int f(n,|\nabla n|)\,\mathrm d\mathbf r$. The PBE functional (Perdew, Burke, Ernzerhof 1996) has no fitted parameters, they are fixed by exact conditions; BLYP and PW91 are other GGAs. GGAs correct the overbinding of LDA and are the standard choice for solids.
  3. meta-GGA: adds the kinetic-energy density $\tau=\tfrac12\sum|\nabla\varphi_i|^2$ (TPSS, SCAN, r$^2$SCAN): better for a wide range of properties at little more cost.
  4. Hybrid functionals: mix a fraction of the exact (Hartree–Fock) exchange into a GGA: B3LYP ($20\%$ exact exchange, three fitted parameters, the most used functional in chemistry), PBE0 ($25\%$), HSE06 (exact exchange only at short range, used for band gaps of solids). They reduce the self-interaction error but cost much more with plane waves.
  5. Double hybrids and the random-phase approximation (use unoccupied orbitals, with the cost of MP2 or more): the most accurate rung in common use.

Dispersion (van der Waals) corrections, such as the empirical “D3” and “D4” additions of Grimme and non-local correlation functionals (vdW-DF, VV10), are added to any rung to describe the long-range attraction that local functionals cannot (Lecture 4). Which functional should one use? As a rule of thumb: PBE or PBEsol for solids and surfaces (PBEsol gives better lattice constants), a hybrid (B3LYP, PBE0, HSE06, with dispersion) for molecules, a meta-GGA when available, and always a test of the sensitivity of the conclusions to the functional.

Energies of atoms: what the functionals get right and wrong

The first test is the total energy of the atoms of Lecture 1 (table above). Relative to the exact values: LDA is too high by $0.069$ Ha for He, $0.221$ for Be and $0.710$ for Ne; PBE by $0.011$, $0.038$ and $0.077$; B3LYP is below the exact energy: by $0.011$ Ha for He ($-2.9151$ against $-2.9037$), $0.005$ Ha for Be ($-14.6726$ against $-14.6674$) and $0.038$ Ha for Ne ($-128.9757$ against $-128.9376$). The variational principle gives a lower bound only for the exact functional; an approximate functional can give an energy below the true one, and the DFT energy of an atom is therefore not evidence for or against the functional. Errors of the order of $0.1$–$1$ Ha in the total energies of atoms are, in fact, the normal situation for LDA; what saves DFT in practice is that these errors are nearly the same in the atoms and in the molecules that they form, so that energy differences are far more accurate than total energies: the subject of the next lecture.

Lecture 4. How good is DFT? Testing the approximations#

An approximate functional is useful only if one knows what it is good at. This lecture is a sequence of controlled experiments of the kind a practitioner should run, or at least read, before trusting a method: a benchmark (compare with a more accurate method or experiment on a set of systems), a diagnostic (a system chosen to expose a specific deficiency) and an error statistic (a summary that says how large the error typically is). We use the methods of the ladder (HF, LDA, PBE, the hybrid B3LYP) and CCSD(T) as the reference for the benchmark.

Diagnostic: self-interaction and delocalisation error

The simplest molecule with a deep problem is the hydrogen molecular ion H$_2^+$, one electron shared by two protons. For one electron the exact answer is known and Hartree–Fock is exact (the exchange term cancels the Hartree term: no self-interaction). When the protons are pulled apart, the exact energy tends to that of a hydrogen atom plus a free proton, and the electron ends up on one of the two centres. An approximate density functional has a positive self-interaction error: the Hartree energy $E_\mathrm H[n]$ of the density of a single electron is not cancelled completely by $E_\mathrm{xc}$, which favours spreading the electron over two centres (half an electron on each) to reduce the spurious self-repulsion. The result is the “delocalisation error”: the energy of the stretched ion relative to (H atom + proton), computed with the same method (exact value $\to0$):

Diagnostic: dispersion (van der Waals) forces

Two argon atoms, neutral and closed-shell, attract each other weakly at distances of $3.5$–$4$ Å by the dispersion force, the interaction of the instantaneous charge fluctuations of the two atoms. It is a correlation effect, long-ranged ($E\sim-C_6/R^6$), and the experimental binding is only $12.3$ meV at $3.76$ Å. The interaction energy must be computed with the counterpoise correction: the monomer energies are evaluated in the basis of the dimer (ghost atoms), otherwise each atom borrows the other’s basis functions and the energy is spuriously lowered (basis-set superposition error, BSSE; Lecture 5):

Lecture 5. Basis sets, integration grids and cost#

A calculation is defined by three choices that must be reported: the functional, the basis set and the numerical settings (integration grid, convergence thresholds). The basis of molecular codes consists of Gaussian-type orbitals because the integrals of products of Gaussians are analytic. The hierarchy of Dunning’s correlation-consistent bases cc-pV$n$Z ($n=$ D, T, Q, 5, ...) increases the size systematically towards the complete-basis limit; aug- adds diffuse functions (needed for anions, weak interactions, excited states); Pople bases (6-31G*) are smaller, and the minimal STO-3G is only for demonstrations. In LAB 501 the PBE energy of water falls from $-75.2256$ Ha (STO-3G, 7 functions) to $-76.3831$ Ha (cc-pVQZ, 115 functions): the errors relative to cc-pVQZ are $1157$, $85$, $50$ and $10$ mHa for STO-3G, 6-31G, cc-pVDZ and cc-pVTZ, the cost growing steeply. Energy differences converge much faster than total energies, because the basis-set error is nearly the same for the species compared. For correlated methods, with slow basis convergence, the standard practice is to extrapolate from two successive $n$.

Basis-set superposition error

In a finite basis the atoms of a dimer use each other’s functions to improve their own description, which lowers the dimer energy artificially and overestimates binding: the BSSE. The counterpoise correction of Boys and Bernardi evaluates each monomer in the full dimer basis, as done in the argon calculation (monomer with a ghost atom carrying the partner’s functions). For weak interactions the uncorrected result can be wrong by more than the interaction itself; the cheap alternative is a larger basis.

Cost scaling

The formal cost of a Hartree–Fock or hybrid-DFT calculation is $O(n^4)$ in the number of basis functions (the number of two-electron integrals), of a pure DFT calculation $O(n^3)$ after the Coulomb term is treated efficiently, of MP2 $O(n^5)$, CCSD $O(n^6)$, CCSD(T) $O(n^7)$. In practice thresholds and sparsity reduce the exponent. Density fitting (resolution of the identity) approximates the product of two basis functions by an auxiliary basis, reducing the four-index integrals to three-index and the cost of the Coulomb term. We measure the time of one self-consistent calculation for linear chains of $n$ H$_2$ molecules in the cc-pVDZ basis with density fitting:

Lecture 6. Forces, geometries and vibrations#

The force on nucleus $I$ is $\mathbf F_I=-\partial E/\partial\mathbf R_I$. The Hellmann–Feynman theorem says that for an exact eigenfunction (or any variational wave function that is complete in its basis) the derivative of the energy with respect to a parameter is the expectation value of the derivative of the Hamiltonian, $\partial E/\partial\lambda=\langle\Psi|\partial\hat H/\partial\lambda|\Psi\rangle$. For a nuclear coordinate $\partial\hat H/\partial\mathbf R_I$ involves only the electrostatic force of the electron density and of the other nuclei on nucleus $I$: forces follow from the density without recomputing the energy at displaced geometries (if a calculation takes time $T$, the forces on all atoms cost about $T$ more, not $3NT$). With atom-centred basis sets there are additional Pulay terms because the basis moves with the atoms. The forces drive the geometry optimisation of Lecture 5 of COMP 202 (the PySCF script uses the geomeTRIC optimiser, with internal coordinates, which converge faster than Cartesian ones for molecules), and the same forces drive ab initio molecular dynamics (CMS 502) and the training of machine-learning potentials (CMS 507).

At a minimum of the energy the second derivatives (the Hessian $H_{IJ}=\partial^2E/\partial\mathbf R_I\partial\mathbf R_J$) are positive, and the harmonic frequencies are $\omega=\sqrt{\lambda/m}$ for the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of the mass-weighted Hessian (COMP 202 Lecture 6), after removing the six translations and rotations; the three vibrations of water are the bend and the two O–H stretches. The calculation optimises the geometry with each method in the cc-pVTZ basis and computes the analytic Hessian:

Lecture 7. Choosing, testing and reporting a calculation#

The results of the previous lectures can be condensed into a protocol for a computational study. (1) Define the question and the observable (a bond energy, a geometry, a spectrum, a reaction barrier, a band gap). (2) Choose the method from the known accuracy for that observable: for the atomisation energies of small molecules a hybrid functional (mean error $0.18$ eV); for geometries and vibrations a hybrid or a GGA with the appropriate scale factor; for weak interactions a dispersion-corrected functional or a wave-function method; for band gaps a hybrid or $GW$, not eigenvalues of a semilocal functional. (3) Test the approximations on a small, closely related benchmark with a reference you trust, as we have done here. (4) Converge the numerical parameters (basis, grid, SCF thresholds, geometry criteria) on the quantity of interest, not on the total energy. (5) Check the SCF solution: stable (try different starting guesses and spin states), correct occupations, no symmetry breaking you did not intend. (6) Report everything needed to reproduce the calculation: code and version, functional, basis, pseudopotential, grid, thresholds, geometry; and deposit input and output files.

Key takeaways
  • The electronic Schroedinger equation for N electrons is a 3N-dimensional problem that cannot be solved exactly; all practical methods are approximations of increasing cost: Hartree-Fock (an independent-particle wave function, formal cost N^4), density functional theory (N^3 to N^4) and coupled cluster (N^7 for CCSD(T)).
  • Hartree-Fock misses the correlation energy: for He, Be and Ne it is 0.0422, 0.0944 and 0.3942 Ha (1.15, 2.57 and 10.7 eV), 25 to 250 times larger than the chemical accuracy of 1.6 mHa; a self-consistent-field loop written from scratch in 30 lines reproduces the PySCF energy of water in 6-31G (-75.983995777 Ha) to 2e-13 Ha, in 32 plain iterations, 40 damped and 11 with DIIS acceleration.
  • Kohn-Sham DFT replaces the many-electron problem by non-interacting electrons in an effective potential that reproduces the exact density; everything not known exactly is collected in the exchange-correlation functional, which is approximated on a ladder: LDA, GGA (PBE), hybrids (B3LYP) that mix exact exchange; the total energies of atoms are off by 0.03 to 0.7 Ha (LDA) but energy differences are much better.
  • Atomisation energies of H2, N2, CO, O2, H2O and CH4 against CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ have mean absolute errors of 3.30 eV for Hartree-Fock (underbinding), 1.71 eV for LDA (overbinding by up to 2.6 eV), 0.56 eV for PBE and 0.18 eV for B3LYP: the functional ladder works, and no functional is exact.
  • Approximate functionals have a self-interaction (delocalisation) error: the one-electron ion H2+ stretched to 12 Angstrom is 2 eV too low with B3LYP and about 2 eV with LDA and PBE near 3-6 Angstrom, where Hartree-Fock is exact; the highest Kohn-Sham orbital energy of water is 7.4 eV (LDA) and 8.8 eV (B3LYP) against an ionisation energy of 12.6 eV, whereas the total-energy difference E(cation) - E(neutral) gives 13.2 and 12.8 eV.
  • Semilocal functionals contain no long-range dispersion: the argon dimer, bound by 12.3 meV at 3.76 Angstrom, is unbound or nearly so with Hartree-Fock and PBE and correctly bound by MP2 and CCSD(T) (see the table); dispersion corrections or non-local functionals are required for molecular crystals, layered materials and adsorption.
  • Time to solution depends on the basis size N as N^p: on chains of H2 molecules (cc-pVDZ, density fitting) the measured exponent is given in the table of Lecture 5; forces are obtained by the Hellmann-Feynman theorem plus basis-set corrections, and harmonic frequencies from the Hessian, with errors that depend on the functional (see the table of Lecture 6).

Problem 8Core

From the H$_2^+$ table: (a) the error of PBE and B3LYP at $R=4$ Å relative to Hartree–Fock (exact). (b) The ratio of the two errors, and comparison with the fraction of exact exchange in B3LYP ($20\%$). (c) What is the physical origin of the error?

Hint

Error $=E_\mathrm{DFT}-E_\mathrm{HF}$ at $4$ Å: $-2.042$, $-1.676$, $-0.098$ eV.

Full solution

(a) PBE: $-2.042-(-0.098)=-1.94$ eV; B3LYP: $-1.676+0.098=-1.58$ eV; both too low (too much binding). (b) $1.58/1.94=0.81$, close to $1-0.2=0.8$: the error of the hybrid is approximately that of the GGA reduced in proportion to the fraction of the GGA exchange that remains (exact exchange has no delocalisation error, the remainder of the functional is the same). (c) The approximate functionals do not cancel exactly the Hartree self-repulsion of a single electron: the energy as a function of fractional occupation of two centres is convex rather than piecewise linear, so spreading half an electron over each centre lowers the energy. This is the delocalisation (many-electron self-interaction) error.

For water (exp. $12.62$ eV) compute the error of $-\varepsilon_\mathrm{HOMO}$ and of $\Delta$SCF for LDA, PBE, B3LYP and HF from the table of Lecture 4. Why can the correct asymptotic potential matter?

Unit quiz#

Closed-book, about 45 minutes, six questions. Attempt all of them before opening any solution, then mark yourself honestly.

Quiz 1Basic

State the Hohenberg–Kohn theorems in words and say what they do not tell us.

Solution

(1) The ground-state density determines the external potential up to a constant and therefore all properties; (2) a universal energy functional $E[n]$ is minimised by the ground-state density, giving the ground-state energy. They prove that a functional exists but do not give $F[n]$ (in particular, the kinetic energy and the electron–electron interaction), so all practical DFT depends on approximations to $E_\mathrm{xc}$ with no systematic way to improve them or to bound their error.

Quiz 2Core

Explain the two uses of the word “exchange” in Hartree–Fock and Kohn–Sham theory, and what “correlation” means in each.

Solution

In Hartree–Fock, exchange is the energy lowering that comes from the antisymmetry of the determinant (the Fermi hole); it is nonlocal ($\hat K$ operator) and exact for the determinant; correlation is everything beyond the determinant. In Kohn–Sham theory the exchange energy is defined in the same way from the Kohn–Sham determinant but approximated by a functional of the density (LDA, GGA), while the correlation energy of DFT also contains the correction to the kinetic energy; the DFT and Hartree–Fock correlation energies are therefore different quantities. Hybrids reintroduce the exact (Hartree–Fock) exchange in a fraction.

Quiz 3Core

A colleague computes the ionisation energy of a molecule as $-\varepsilon_\mathrm{HOMO}$ with PBE and gets $7$ eV for a molecule whose measured value is $12$ eV. Is the functional bad?

Solution

No: the result is expected. The Kohn–Sham HOMO energy of a semilocal functional is not an ionisation energy: the potential decays too fast so the HOMO is too high by several eV (here $5$ eV, as for water). The right comparison is $\Delta$SCF, $E(\mathrm{cation})-E(\mathrm{neutral})$, which for PBE is accurate to a few tenths of an eV; or a $GW$ calculation. The same applies to electron affinities and band gaps.

Quiz 4Core

Name three failures of semilocal functionals and for each one a remedy.

Solution

(1) Overbinding of molecules and solids (LDA) — GGAs, meta-GGAs, hybrids. (2) Missing dispersion — D3/D4 corrections or non-local functionals (vdW-DF) or wave-function methods. (3) Delocalisation (self-interaction) error: too small barriers and gaps, spurious fractional charges — hybrid or range-separated hybrid functionals, Hubbard $U$, $GW$. Also acceptable: the underestimate of band gaps (hybrids, $GW$), poor strongly correlated systems (DMFT, DFT$+U$, multireference methods).

Quiz 5Core

Why does the SCF iteration converge faster with DIIS, and what could prevent it from converging at all?

Solution

DIIS extrapolates the Fock matrix as a linear combination of previous ones that minimises the commutator error, which is a quasi-Newton step for the nonlinear equations; it reduces the iterations from $32$ to $11$ in the water example. Convergence can fail if the starting guess is far from the solution, if the HOMO–LUMO gap is small (charge sloshing between nearly degenerate orbitals: use smearing or level shift), if the system has several SCF solutions (stretched bonds, transition metals: try different guesses and spin states), or if the geometry is unphysical.

Quiz 6Challenge

You are asked to compute the binding energy of a benzene dimer (stacked) with a hybrid functional and a triple-zeta basis. List the checks you would make and the expected pitfalls.

Solution

(1) The functional must include dispersion (the dimer is bound by about $0.1$ eV, almost entirely by dispersion; plain B3LYP gives little or no binding): use B3LYP−D3(BJ) or a method that includes it, and compare with a wave-function reference (a benchmark such as the S66 set or DLPNO-CCSD(T)). (2) Counterpoise correction, because BSSE for a triple-zeta basis can be $20$–$50\%$ of the interaction. (3) Basis convergence (aug- functions, then quadruple-zeta or extrapolation). (4) A fine integration grid. (5) Geometry: relax each monomer, and sample several dimer configurations (parallel-displaced and T-shaped are close in energy). (6) Report the method, basis, grid and the correction schemes together with the result.
